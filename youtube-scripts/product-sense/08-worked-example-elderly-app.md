EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. We built the framework piece by piece; now we run it. The prompt is the kind you'll actually get: design a video-calling app for elderly users. I'll walk all six steps the way I would in a real interview, narrating as I go. Watch the structure carry the answer.

[VISUAL: The six-step badge appears in the corner and highlights each step as EV reaches it: CLARIFY · USER · NEEDS · SOLUTIONS · PRIORITIZE · METRICS.]

EV: Step one, clarify. I scope it, out loud. "A couple of questions first — standalone app, or a feature inside something that exists? Is there a business goal, like selling a device, or is it open-ended? Any platform constraint?" Say the interviewer tells me: standalone, open-ended, built for the best experience. Good. "One more — I'll take 'elderly' to mean people who genuinely find current smartphones difficult, not tech-comfortable seniors. Right?" They agree. Scope set, thirty seconds, and the answer already sounds considered.

[VISUAL: Step 1 checks off. Step 2 "USER" highlights. A crowd narrows to one spotlighted figure with a persona card.]

EV: Step two, the user. "Within 'elderly' there's a range — the active sixty-five-year-old who texts fine, and the eighty-five-year-old who's never really used a smartphone. The sharpest pain, and the more meaningful product, is that second group — I'll focus there." And I make her specific: "Picture Grace, eighty-four, lives alone, hands shake a little, eyesight fading, anxious about technology — and what she wants more than anything is to see her grandchildren's faces." From here, Grace is the compass for every decision.

[VISUAL: Step 2 checks off. Step 3 "NEEDS" highlights. A day-in-the-life timeline with friction; a three-layer set: functional / emotional / social.]

EV: Step three, needs and pains — the heart of it. I walk Grace's day. She wants to call the grandchildren, but can't find the app among a screen of tiny icons. She finds it — the text's too small to read. She taps a contact — shaky hands hit the wrong one. A call connects — she isn't sure it worked. And underneath all of it, each failure makes her feel a little less capable, a little more cut off. Then I name the layers. "Functionally, connect a call easily. Emotionally, feel capable, not foolish. Socially, feel like part of the family, not a burden." And the sharpest pains: "the fear of getting it wrong, and the isolation — those are the deep ones." A rich, human list.

[VISUAL: Step 3 checks off. Step 4 "SOLUTIONS" highlights. Several ideas appear, each tagged to a pain.]

EV: Step four, solutions. I go wide, anchoring each idea to a pain. Can't find the app? Make it the only thing on a dead-simple home screen — three large buttons, nothing else. Can't read or tap? Large contact photos instead of text, oversized tap targets. Shaky hands? Voice calling — just say "call my granddaughter." Not sure it connected? A clear, friendly "connected" confirmation with a reassuring sound. And a bolder one: an auto-call feature that rings the family at a set time each day, so Grace doesn't even have to start it. Wide set, each tied to a real pain, one safe idea and one bold one in the mix.

[VISUAL: Step 4 checks off. Step 5 "PRIORITIZE" highlights. A value-vs-effort 2×2; the winning idea moves to top-left and is marked "MVP."]

EV: Step five, prioritize to an MVP. I place the ideas on a value-versus-effort grid. "The large-photo, one-tap home screen is high value — it hits Grace's sharpest pains directly — and relatively low effort. That's the quick win, that's my MVP." I commit, out loud, with the reason. Then I sequence the rest: "Version two adds voice calling, for users who can't manage even large buttons. Version three brings in the automatic family-call feature." No hedging — one confident MVP, roadmap behind it.

[VISUAL: Step 5 checks off. Step 6 "METRICS" highlights. A goal→signal→metric chain; one north star brightens; a guardrail shield appears.]

EV: Step six, metrics. I close the loop with the chain. "Goal: reconnect Grace with her family. Signal: she's completing calls regularly. Metric — the north star — completed video calls per user per week." A couple of supporting metrics: "adoption, the percentage who complete a first call; retention, the percentage still calling after a month." And the guardrail: "I'd make sure we're not stressing her with too many notifications — so I'd track opt-outs or reported frustration, to protect the experience while it grows."

EV: And that's the whole answer, start to finish. Look what happened: an open-ended, intimidating prompt became a calm, structured, genuinely human answer. Clarify; a specific user, made real; her functional, emotional, and social pains; a wide set of solutions anchored to those pains; a confident MVP with a roadmap; and a close with a north-star metric and a guardrail. A complete answer — and notice it was never about a flashy feature. It was about Grace.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE RHYTHM." The six-step badge with rough time allocations.]

EV: The thing to take from seeing it live is the rhythm. Quick clarify, sharp user, deep dive on needs — that's where the heart is — then wide solutions, a confident call, a clean metrics close. Keep moving, keep narrating, keep Grace on your shoulder throughout. Do that, and you don't just answer the question — you leave the interviewer with a person they can picture. That's what gets remembered.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHEN THE INTERVIEWER PUSHES BACK." An interviewer raises a hand; the narration stays calm.]

EV: One more thing, because in a real interview they won't just sit there — they'll push back, and how you handle it matters as much as the answer. Say halfway through they cut in: "Why focus on the eighty-five-year-old? The active sixty-five-year-old is a far bigger market." Don't panic, don't cave. Acknowledge, then reason. "Fair point — the sixty-five-year-old is a bigger market. I chose the eighty-five-year-old because the pain is sharper, so we can build something they'll genuinely love and that's clearly differentiated. But if the goal is maximum reach, I'd happily shift — their pains are lighter, so the design would move toward convenience rather than accessibility." You defended your logic and showed you can adapt. That's what pushback actually tests — not whether you picked correctly, but whether you can reason and flex without unraveling. Treat the challenge as an opening, not a threat.
