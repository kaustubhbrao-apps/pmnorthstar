EV: There's a paradox at the center of product design: the way to make your answer feel broad and impressive is to narrow it — to stop designing for everyone and design for one person. That move, choosing the user, is the one this video is about, because it's the one that most often decides whether the rest of your answer holds together.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "STEP 2 · THE USER."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. We're on step two — clarify, then user. And the most common mistake I see is candidates skipping this or fudging it, then paying for it the whole rest of the answer. So let's get it right.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY 'EVERYONE' IS A TRAP." A product icon stretches to cover many figures and snaps back.]

EV: Why is "everyone" a trap? Take "design a music app." If your user is "everyone who likes music," every decision after that goes soft. Do you optimize for the commuter who wants one-tap playback, the audiophile who wants lossless, the gym-goer who wants offline, or the parent limiting explicit content? You can't serve them equally — their needs conflict. A product that pleases everyone fits no one. So narrowing doesn't shrink your answer. It's what makes it sharp.

[VISUAL: Header: "THREE MOVES." Three numbered cards.]

EV: There's a clean, three-move way to do this.

[VISUAL: Card 1: "1 · LIST THE SEGMENTS." A product icon branches into 3–4 figures.]

EV: Move one: list the segments, out loud. Not the first user that pops up — take ten seconds and lay out the real options. For the music app: casual listeners, serious audiophiles, workout users, maybe parents managing kids' listening. Just naming the landscape shows you see the whole board before choosing where to play. That's the difference between a reasoned pick and a guess.

[VISUAL: Card 2: "2 · PICK ONE — WITH A REASON." A spotlight settles on one figure; a "WHY" tag attaches.]

EV: Move two: pick one, and say why. The reason is what matters. Choose by where the pain is sharpest, or market size, or fit with a business goal. "I'll take the workout user — underserved by the big players, passionate, and growing." The reason proves you're reasoning, not guessing. And there's a bonus: if the interviewer had a different segment in mind, they'll just redirect you — no harm done, because you showed the logic.

[VISUAL: Card 3: "3 · A QUICK PERSONA." The figure gains labels: name, age, context, tech comfort, goal.]

EV: Move three: bring that user to life with a quick persona — a few strokes, thirty seconds. "Picture Maya, twenty-eight, runs four times a week, cheap wireless earbuds, hates fumbling with her phone mid-run, wants zero friction." Now she's not an abstract label; she's a person you and the interviewer can both picture. And that pays off everywhere after this, because every later decision collapses into one question — "what would Maya want?" The persona becomes your compass.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHICH SEGMENT?" A balance weighing three factors.]

EV: Which raises the practical question: how do you decide which segment? No single correct choice, but three solid lenses. One, pain intensity — which group hurts most right now, because sharp pain makes a product people genuinely value. Two, size and growth — which group is large or growing fast, which matters when there's a business angle. Three, strategic fit — which group aligns with the company's goals or strengths. Pick the lens that fits the prompt, name it, choose. "I'll go with pain intensity, and focus on the most underserved group." Clear, and defensible.

[VISUAL: Quiet lower-third: "Subscribe to pmnorthstar."]

EV: If the series is useful, subscribing to pmnorthstar helps it keep going. Now, two traps to avoid.

[VISUAL: Header: "TWO TRAPS."]

EV: Trap one: defaulting to yourself as the user. It's tempting to design for a tech-savvy twenty-five-year-old, because that's probably you. But when the prompt says "elderly," or "farmers," or "small-business owners," you have to genuinely leave your own perspective behind. Designing for yourself when you aren't the user is a classic empathy failure, and it shows immediately. Trap two: the opposite — over-segmenting. Some candidates get so into it they define six micro-personas and can't design for any of them. Name a few options, commit to one primary user. You can mention a secondary user if it's relevant, but the design serves one person well. Breadth of awareness, depth of focus.

[VISUAL: Header: "FOR 'IMPROVE' QUESTIONS." An existing app with user data; a magnifying glass finds an underserved group.]

EV: Quickly — how this works for an "improve product X" question, where the product exists. Same step, one shift. Instead of inventing a user, ask: who uses this today, and which group is it underserving? "This app serves the power user well, but new users get lost in onboarding — I'll focus on the new user." Still one segment, with a reason; you're just picking the one the product is failing.

[VISUAL: Recap: the spotlight-on-one-person image, now with persona labels and a "WHY" tag.]

EV: So: choosing the user is three moves — list the segments, pick one with a reason, sketch a quick persona. Decide with pain, size, or strategic fit. Dodge the two traps — don't design for yourself, don't over-segment. Do this, and every step after gets easier, because you're reasoning about a specific person instead of an abstraction.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE MORE, FAST — A TRAVEL APP." A crowd of traveler types; the spotlight picks one.]

EV: One more pass, so it feels natural — "design a travel app." Move one, segments: the budget backpacker, the luxury vacationer, the business traveler, the family with kids — different needs. Move two, pick with a reason: the business traveler, because the pain's sharp — they travel constantly, they're stressed, time beats money, and it's high-value and underserved. Move three, quick persona: Sam, thirty-five, flies twice a month for work, hates wasting time, wants flights, hotel, and expense receipts in one frictionless place. Fifteen seconds, and the problem's already sharper. Not "a travel app for everyone" — Sam, a specific person with specific pain. From here, every decision is just "what would Sam want?" That's what choosing the user buys you.
