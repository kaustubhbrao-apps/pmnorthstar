[VISUAL: Two identical candidates on a dark field. One stumbles, and a red flag drops over them; the other steadies, and a gold star rises. Same question, two outcomes.]

EV: Two candidates walk into the same interview, use the same framework, and get opposite results. One struggles; one gets the offer. The difference usually isn't talent — it's a handful of specific mistakes on one side, and a handful of specific moves on the other. This video is both lists: what to avoid, and what to borrow.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "MISTAKES & STANDOUT MOVES."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar — final video of the product-sense series. You've got the full framework now, six steps. But knowing it isn't quite enough, because there are classic traps that catch even people who know it cold, and moves that make an answer genuinely memorable. Mistakes first — avoiding them alone puts you ahead of most candidates.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 6 MISTAKES." Six red-flag slots, filling one at a time.]

EV: Mistake one: jumping straight to a solution — the most common by far. Interviewer says "design an app for X," candidate offers a feature in the first ten seconds. No clarifying, no user, no needs — just a feature. Reads as thinking like an inventor, not a product manager. Fix: walk the framework. The solution belongs in step four, not step one.

[VISUAL: Red flag 2 — a figure trying to serve a large faceless crowd.]

EV: Two: designing for everyone. We've covered it, but it's worth repeating because it's everywhere. "My user is anyone who likes music." Instead, pick one specific user, with a reason. A product for everyone delights no one, and interviewers know it.

[VISUAL: Red flag 3 — a shallow puddle, no depth.]

EV: Three: shallow needs — listing only the obvious functional pains and stopping. "They want to make a call." Fine, but why does it matter, and how do they want to feel? Skip the emotional and social layers and you sound surface-level. Go deeper — jobs-to-be-done, and asking why.

[VISUAL: Red flag 4 — an endless list of features scrolling off-screen.]

EV: Four: no prioritization — brainstorming twenty features and stopping, handing over a pile with no ranking. Reads as an inability to decide. Always narrow to an MVP, and commit out loud, with a reason. Deciding is the job.

[VISUAL: Red flag 5 — a mockup with a "...and then?" question mark, no metric.]

EV: Five: forgetting metrics — ending at the idea, never saying how you'd measure success. It leaves the answer unfinished. Thirty seconds on a north-star metric closes it. Don't skip the ending.

[VISUAL: Red flag 6 — a candidate thinking silently while the interviewer looks lost.]

EV: Six: poor communication — strong thinking that stays in your head, or rambling with no visible structure. The talking is the test. Signpost continuously: "Step one, clarify… now the user… here are their pains…" Make the structure audible. If the interviewer can always tell where you are, you're in good shape.

[VISUAL: All six red flags line up, then an "AVOID THESE" banner clears them.]

EV: Avoid those six and you're already ahead of most candidates. But the goal isn't just dodging mistakes — so let's turn to the moves that lift an answer.

[VISUAL: Quiet lower-third: "Subscribe to pmnorthstar."]

EV: Before that — if the series got you here, subscribing to pmnorthstar is what keeps it going. Now, the standout moves.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 5 STANDOUT MOVES." Five gold stars, one at a time.]

EV: One: genuine, specific empathy. Don't say "the user." Give them a name and a life — "Grace, eighty-four, whose hands shake when she tries to tap a contact." Make the interviewer see a real person, and you stop sounding like someone reciting a framework and start sounding like someone who cares about users. That specificity is rare, and it lands.

[VISUAL: Gold star 2 — three layers, functional/emotional/social, together.]

EV: Two: naming the emotional and social layers. Almost everyone lists functional needs. The candidate who adds "emotionally, she needs to feel capable, not embarrassed — and socially, part of the family" immediately sounds a level deeper. Small addition, large signal.

[VISUAL: Gold star 3 — a safe idea and a bold idea balanced.]

EV: Three: pairing one bold idea with the safe ones. Show range. Offer the solid, obvious solution and one creative, unexpected one — like the feature that calls family automatically, so the person never has to initiate. The safe idea shows you're grounded; the bold one shows vision. Memorable answers usually have both.

[VISUAL: Gold star 4 — a guardrail shield beside a rising metric.]

EV: Four: the guardrail metric. Name not just what you'd grow but what you'd protect — "I'd make sure we're not stressing the user with notifications" — and you signal responsible, senior-level thinking. Very few candidates do it, which is exactly why it stands out.

[VISUAL: Gold star 5 — a roadmap stretching from MVP into v2, v3.]

EV: Five: thinking in roadmaps. Don't drop an MVP and stop — briefly sequence what comes next. "Ship this first, then version two adds this, version three that." It shows you see the product as something that evolves, not a one-time idea. That's the difference between answering a question and showing how you'd run a product.

[VISUAL: The five gold stars form a constellation; the six-step framework reappears beneath them.]

EV: Put it together. The framework keeps you structured. Avoiding the six mistakes keeps you out of the ditch. And the five moves — specific empathy, deeper need layers, a bold idea, a guardrail, a roadmap — lift an answer from a solid pass to someone they want to hire.

[VISUAL: Series recap — the nine video thumbnails pass by: what-it-tests, framework, user, needs, solutions, MVP, metrics, live example, mistakes.]

EV: And that closes the product-sense series. You know what these questions really test. You have a six-step framework — clarify, user, needs, solutions, prioritize, metrics — that handles any "design" or "improve" prompt. You've watched it run live on a real product. And you know the traps to avoid and the moves that set the best answers apart. The product you design was never really the point. The thinking was.

[VISUAL: Header: "A 10-SECOND CHECKLIST." A checklist ticks off.]

EV: I'll leave you with something you can run in your head in the first ten seconds of any design question — a quick pre-flight check. The moment you hear the prompt, before you speak: one, don't jump to a solution — breathe. Two, clarify first. Three, pick one user, with a reason. Four, go deep on needs — functional, emotional, social. Five, go wide on solutions, then narrow. Six, commit to an MVP out loud. Seven, close with a metric and a guardrail. Eight, narrate throughout. Run that on autopilot and you've pre-loaded a structured answer before the interviewer's finished the question.

[VISUAL: The checklist becomes the six-step framework badge, complete.]

EV: Practice it on three products this week — one you love, one you find frustrating, one at random. Run all six steps out loud each time. After a few reps, the next real one feels much easier.
