[VISUAL: A line assembles: "People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch HOLE." HOLE is emphasized.]

EV: Old line in product circles: people don't want a quarter-inch drill — they want a quarter-inch hole. Sounds like a slogan, but take it seriously and you start finding needs most people walk straight past. That's this video: how to uncover what a user actually wants, underneath what they appear to want.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "STEP 3 · NEEDS & PAINS."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. This is the heart of the framework — step three, needs and pains. We've clarified and chosen one user. Now we get inside their day and work out what they're really trying to do, and where it hurts. Spend real time here, because every solution you invent later is only as good as the pain it solves. Weak pains, forgettable product. Sharp pains, memorable one.

[VISUAL: Header: "JOBS, NOT FEATURES." A drill becomes a hole, a milkshake, a messaging app — each with a "job" label.]

EV: The idea that powers this is jobs-to-be-done, and the core of it is simple: people don't buy products, they hire them to do a job. You don't want a messaging app — you want to feel connected to someone you care about. You don't want a food-delivery app — you want a hot meal, no effort, when you're exhausted. The product's the tool; the job's the real thing. Look for the underlying job instead of the obvious feature, and you find needs other people miss.

[VISUAL: Header: "FUNCTIONAL · EMOTIONAL · SOCIAL." Three layers stack.]

EV: And every job has three layers. The functional job — the practical task. The emotional job — how they want to feel. The social job — how they want to be seen. Take the elderly video-calling user. Functionally: connect the call to their grandchild. Emotionally: feel capable, not confused or embarrassed. Socially: feel like part of the family, not a burden. Most answers stop at functional. The ones that name the emotional and social layers sound like they understand people — which is exactly the impression you want.

[VISUAL: Header: "FINDING THE PAINS." A figure moves through a "day in the life" timeline; friction at each stuck point.]

EV: So how do you find these pains on the spot? The most reliable move: walk a day in the user's life, step by step, and watch for friction. Picture them trying to reach their goal start to finish, and at each step ask where they get stuck — confused, annoyed, anxious. For the elderly user making a video call: pick up the phone — can they find the app? Open it — can they read the text? Tap a contact — are the buttons big enough for shaky hands? It connects — do they know it worked? Each moment is a potential pain. Narrate the journey, collect the friction.

[VISUAL: A list builds as EV names each pain.]

EV: For this user, the list might be: can't find the right app among dozens of icons; text and buttons too small to see and tap; real fear of pressing the wrong thing and "breaking" it; unsure the call connected; and underneath it, a sense of isolation from distant family. Notice that's a mix of functional, emotional, and social — which is what makes it a rich list. And a rich pain list makes the next step, solutions, almost write itself.

EV: If jobs-to-be-done is shifting how you see products, subscribing to pmnorthstar keeps the series coming. Now, two ways to go deeper, fast.

[VISUAL: Header: "ASK 'WHY'." A pain, then several "why?" prompts drilling down to a root.]

EV: First: keep asking why. Spot a pain, don't stop at the surface — ask why a few times and follow it down. The user doesn't make video calls. Why? Buttons too small. Why does that stop them? Shaky hands, keeps misdialing. Why does that matter so much? Because after failing twice they feel foolish and give up — and stay isolated. Four steps, and you've moved from a small interface problem to a deep emotional need. The root is where the best products get built, so it's worth the extra questions.

[VISUAL: Header: "STATED vs REAL NEEDS." A spoken request; a thought bubble revealing the deeper truth.]

EV: Second: separate what the user says from what they actually need. People are famously bad at describing their own needs — the classic line is, ask people in 1900 and they'd have said faster horses, not a car. So when you imagine your user asking for something, look past the literal request to the underlying job. They say, "I want bigger buttons." The real need: "I want to not feel incompetent when I try to reach my family." Design for the real need, and you get ahead of anyone who took the request at face value.

[VISUAL: Header: "RANK THE PAINS." A few marked "SHARP," others dimmed "MINOR."]

EV: One last thing before we leave this step: not all pains are equal, so flag the sharp ones. As you list them, note which hurt most. "Of all these, the two that matter are the fear of breaking it and the isolation — those are the deep ones." That sets up prioritization later, and it shows judgment inside your empathy. You're not just listing problems; you're already sensing which ones are worth solving.

[VISUAL: Recap: the three-layer stack beside the day-in-the-life timeline.]

EV: So the heart of product sense: look for the job, not the feature. Work all three layers — functional, emotional, social. Walk the day in the life for friction. Drill to the root by asking why, read past stated wants to real needs, and flag the sharpest pains. Get this right, and the rest of the answer tends to follow from it.

[VISUAL: Header: "A PRODUCT YOU KNOW." A ride-hailing app; its three job layers separate.]

EV: See it in a product you already use — a ride-hailing app. On the surface the job's purely functional: get me from A to B. But look at the layers. Functional, sure — reach the destination. Emotional? Feel safe in a stranger's car, and feel in control — know exactly when it's arriving, so you're not anxious on a dark street. Social? Skip the awkwardness of haggling over a fare or fumbling for cash in front of people. And here's the striking part — the features that made ride-hailing explode weren't really about the ride. The live map showing the car approaching? That's the emotional, control need. Driver ratings and cashless payment? Safety and social. The product that won did it by solving the emotional and social jobs, not just the functional one. That's what the deeper layers unlock — and exactly the thinking an interviewer's hoping to hear.
