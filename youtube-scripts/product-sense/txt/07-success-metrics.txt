EV: You can design something genuinely good and still leave the answer half-finished. Because there's one question most product-sense answers never reach — the one that separates an idea from a decision: how would you know whether it actually worked? This video closes that loop.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "STEP 6 · METRICS."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Last step of the framework, and it's an odd one — the step candidates skip most, and quietly some of the easiest credit in the whole interview. You've designed the product and chosen your MVP. Now the question every PM faces after every launch: did it work?

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THIS STEP MATTERS." A split: "ends at the idea" fades; "ends at the metric" holds, with a checkmark.]

EV: It matters because shipping isn't the finish line — measuring whether it moved anything is. A PM who can't say how they'd measure success can't tell if they're winning or losing. So when you end with "and here's how I'd measure this," you sound like someone who's actually shipped and then watched what happened. That's the line between an idea person and a product person, and interviewers catch it fast.

[VISUAL: Header: "GOAL → SIGNAL → METRIC." Three boxes link with arrows.]

EV: Here's the clean way to choose a metric — a short chain: goal, to signal, to metric. Start with the goal: what are you actually trying to achieve for the user? Then the signal: what behavior would show you're achieving it? Then the metric: how do you turn that behavior into a trackable number? Run it on the elderly video-calling app. Goal: reconnect the person with family. Signal: they're completing video calls regularly. Metric: completed calls per user per week. Goal, signal, metric — and the number ties straight back to the real purpose of the product, which is exactly the connection interviewers listen for.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE NORTH STAR." A sky of stars; one brightens.]

EV: The key discipline: pick one primary metric — your north star for this product. Not fifteen. One number that, if it's rising, means you're genuinely winning. Here, that's completed calls per user per week, because it reflects the real job — reconnection. You can name a couple of supporting metrics too, but you lead with the one that matters most. List a dozen metrics and you sound unfocused; say "if I could watch only one number, it'd be this, because it captures the core value," and you sound like you understand the product.

[VISUAL: Header: "SUPPORTING METRICS." A small tree: north star on top, 2–3 branches.]

EV: Once you've got the north star, briefly add a few supporting metrics to round out the picture. They usually fall into three buckets: adoption — are people starting to use it at all? Engagement — how often, how deeply? Retention — do they keep coming back? For this app: adoption is the percentage who complete a first call; engagement is the north star, calls per week; retention is the percentage still calling after a month. Don't go deep — just show you see the fuller measurement picture beyond one number. Thirty seconds, well spent.

[VISUAL: Quiet lower-third: "Subscribe to pmnorthstar."]

EV: If metrics are starting to feel manageable, subscribe to pmnorthstar — the full metrics series is coming. Now, the move that reads as genuinely senior.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE GUARDRAIL METRIC." A primary metric rises; a warning meter rises with it; a shield appears.]

EV: Very few candidates do this, which is why it stands out: naming a guardrail metric. Every primary metric can be gamed, or can cause harm if pushed too hard, and a careful PM watches for that. So ask: if I pushed my north star as hard as possible, what might I accidentally break? For the video app — chasing more calls, you might flood the person with notifications and make them anxious. So a guardrail might be reported stress, or notification opt-outs. You protect the experience while you pursue the goal. A guardrail signals you think about responsible growth, not just growth — a mature instinct, and a memorable one.

[VISUAL: Header: "TWO TRAPS."]

EV: Two traps. One, the vanity metric — a number that looks impressive but doesn't reflect real value, like total downloads. A million downloads mean nothing if nobody makes a call. Pick a metric tied to real user value, not a flattering big number. Two, a metric you can't connect back to the goal. If you can't explain why it reflects success, it's the wrong metric — and the goal-signal-metric chain is what keeps you honest. If the chain doesn't connect, rebuild it.

[VISUAL: Recap: the goal→signal→metric chain, the single north star, a couple of supporting branches, and a guardrail shield.]

EV: So, closing the loop: build the chain — goal, to signal, to metric. Pick one north star that captures real value. Add a few supporting metrics across adoption, engagement, retention. Include a guardrail to protect the user. Avoid vanity metrics and disconnected numbers. Finish every product-sense answer here, and it won't feel unfinished.

[VISUAL: The full six-step framework lights end to end: Clarify · User · Needs · Solutions · Prioritize · Metrics. A checkmark lands.]

EV: And with that, you have the complete framework — all six steps. Clarify, user, needs and pains, solutions, prioritize to an MVP, metrics. Together, a full, structured answer to essentially any design question.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE MORE CHAIN — A FOOD-DELIVERY APP." The goal→signal→metric boxes rebuild.]

EV: Let's run the chain once more on a different product, so the pattern settles — a food-delivery app. Goal: get hungry users a meal with no hassle. Signal: they complete orders, and they come back. Metric, the north star: orders completed per user per month, because it captures both the no-hassle part and the coming-back part. Supporting: adoption, the percentage of signups who place a first order; engagement, the north star; retention, the percentage still ordering after sixty days. Guardrail: delivery time and order accuracy — because push order volume too hard and you overwhelm the kitchens and wreck the actual experience. Same structure as the video-calling app — goal, signal, metric, supporting, guardrail — on a completely different product. Once this chain is internalized, you can define sharp metrics for almost anything in under a minute.
