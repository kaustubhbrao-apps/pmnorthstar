[VISUAL: Black screen. A prompt types itself out: "Design a fridge for the blind." Then "Design an alarm clock for the elderly." Then "Improve the ticket machine at a train station."]

EV: "Design a fridge for the blind." "Improve the airport experience." "Design an app for your grandmother." Real questions, asked in real product interviews, every day. And most people freeze on them — because it feels like you can't prepare for a question that could be about anything. But you can. It starts with one shift: understanding what the question is actually for.

[VISUAL: The prompts snap into the pmnorthstar logo.]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. This is where we start on product sense — product design questions — the most common type of question in product interviews anywhere in the world. Google, Meta, Amazon, nearly every startup asks some version of "design something, for someone." The prompts are endless; the thing being tested underneath them is not. Nail that, and the whole category becomes learnable. Let's go.

[VISUAL: Title card: "PRODUCT SENSE." An underline sweeps across.]

EV: First, the single most expensive mistake — and it happens in the first few seconds, before you've said anything structured.

[VISUAL: A question mark opens into a brain with turning gears.]

EV: You hear "design an app for your grandmother," and the instinct is to produce something fast — a clever feature, a cool idea. To jump to the solution. And that's the quickest way to lose the question. Because the interviewer isn't buying your feature. They're watching whether you think like a product manager before you think like an inventor. Product sense isn't "can you come up with a product." It's "can you reason your way to the right product — for a real person, with a real need." The feature is the last thing that matters, not the first.

[VISUAL: Section header: "THE 4 THINGS BEING TESTED." Four slots line up.]

EV: So what are they actually scoring? Four things. Know them, and you can deliver each one on purpose.

[VISUAL: Slot 1 fills — two figures, one listening. Label: "USER EMPATHY."]

EV: One: user empathy. Can you get out of your own head and into someone else's life? When the user is "your grandmother," can you picture her day — her eyesight, how she feels about technology, what frustrates her, what she's afraid of? Strong candidates don't design for a generic "user." They design for a specific person they can almost see. Everything else is built on that.

[VISUAL: Slot 2 fills — a tangle resolving into labeled boxes. Label: "STRUCTURED THINKING."]

EV: Two: structured thinking. Can you take a huge, vague prompt and break it into a clear path? Strong candidates don't ramble — they say "here's how I'll approach this," and walk a visible structure. And the structure does the heavy lifting: it tells the interviewer your reasoning holds up under pressure, even when the problem is undefined.

[VISUAL: Slot 3 fills — many lightbulbs, then a funnel to one. Label: "CREATIVITY + JUDGMENT."]

EV: Three: creativity, paired with judgment — both halves. Creativity is generating options, going wider than the obvious. Judgment is knowing which ones matter and cutting the rest. Anyone can brainstorm; the skill is brainstorming and then choosing. We drill both later.

[VISUAL: Slot 4 fills — a ranked list, then a target with a metric. Label: "PRIORITIZATION + METRICS."]

EV: Four: prioritization and measurement. Can you decide what to build first — the one slice with the most value — and say how you'd know it worked? Interviews are short; you can't build everything. "Here's what I'd ship first, and here's the metric I'd watch" sounds like a real PM. Twenty features with no ranking sounds like a suggestion box.

[VISUAL: All four slots glow; a ribbon labeled "COMMUNICATION" wraps them.]

EV: And around all four — communication. Narrate your thinking, clearly, the whole way. A brilliant design that stays in your head scores zero; the talking is the test. Think of it as a guided tour of how a product mind works.

[VISUAL: Quiet lower-third: "Subscribe to pmnorthstar."]

EV: If this is already reframing these questions for you, subscribing to pmnorthstar keeps the series going. Now — two things that trip people, fast.

[VISUAL: Header: "'DESIGN' vs 'IMPROVE'." Two paths fork.]

EV: First, spot which flavor you've got. "Design a product for X" is a blank canvas — build from scratch. "Improve product X," or "what's your favorite product and how would you make it better?" — that's not blank. There's an existing product, real users, real data, and your job is to find the gap and close it. Here's the good news: the same framework handles both. For design, you start from the user and build up. For improve, you start from the product, work out who it's failing and where, and build up from that gap. One framework, both flavors.

[VISUAL: Background softens. Text: "THERE IS NO 'RIGHT' PRODUCT."]

EV: Second — the mindset to carry through all of it. There's no single right answer. Two strong candidates can design two completely different products for the same prompt and both do well, because they're graded on the thinking, not the product. Once that lands, the pressure drops, and these stop feeling like traps and start feeling like what they are — an open problem where you get to show how you reason.

[VISUAL: Roadmap — a path with 9 nodes. Node 1 lit; later: "FRAMEWORK," "USER," "NEEDS," "SOLUTIONS," "MVP," "METRICS," "LIVE EXAMPLE."]

EV: Here's the plan. Next, the full framework — the skeleton that turns any "design an X" into a structured answer. Then each step: choosing the user, uncovering real needs, generating solutions, narrowing to an MVP, defining metrics. Then we run the whole thing live on a real prompt. And we close with the mistakes that sink people and the moves that set the best answers apart.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE FIRST 30 SECONDS." Split screen: "WEAK" vs "STRONG."]

EV: Let me show you the payoff in the first thirty seconds, because that opening sets the tone for everything after. Weak answer: interviewer says "design an app for your grandmother," candidate goes, "Okay, I'd build an app with big buttons, video calling, maybe a game." Straight to features — no user, no problem, no structure. Impression formed. Strong answer, same prompt: "Good one. Before I design anything, let me clarify a couple of things, pick a specific user, understand their pains, and only then get to solutions and how I'd measure success." Nothing's designed yet — and it already reads ten times more credible, because it leads with structure and empathy instead of a feature. That contrast is basically the whole lesson. And here's the kicker: the strong opening isn't talent. It's just knowing the framework — which is exactly what's next.
