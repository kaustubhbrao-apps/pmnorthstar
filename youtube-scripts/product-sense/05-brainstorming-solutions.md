[VISUAL: A single dim lightbulb on a dark field. Then many bright bulbs rise and fill the screen, before a funnel narrows them to a few.]

EV: One rule runs this whole step: go wide before you go deep. Generate first, judge second. Most people do the opposite — grab one idea and start defending it — and it quietly costs them. Here's how to do it in the right order, which turns out to be a method you can learn, not a talent you're born with.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "STEP 4 · SOLUTIONS."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. We've clarified, chosen a user, dug out their real pains. Now — solutions, step four. People either love this step or dread it, because it feels like pure creativity. It isn't. Good brainstorming in an interview is a method, and once you have it, you don't freeze with a blank mind.

[VISUAL: Header: "DIVERGE, THEN CONVERGE." Two phases: a wide spread of ideas, then a narrowing funnel.]

EV: The governing rule: diverge, then converge. Two phases, and the mistake is mixing them. First you diverge — generate a lot, go wide, hold off judging. Then you converge — step back and filter to the best. The classic error is converging too early: you land on one idea, get attached, stop looking. So open things up first. Judgment comes later, on purpose.

[VISUAL: Header: "ANCHOR EACH IDEA TO A PAIN." The pain list on the left; arrows connecting each pain to solutions on the right.]

EV: Here's the trick that makes ideas flow without forcing them: anchor every solution to a specific pain. Don't brainstorm in a vacuum. Go pain by pain down your list and ask, "how could I solve this one?" The pains become launch points. Elderly user — buttons too small? Large tap targets, voice commands, a physical big-button mode. Afraid of breaking it? A simplified, locked-down mode with nowhere to get lost. The pains generate the ideas, so you never run dry — each one's a prompt.

[VISUAL: Header: "THREE LENSES." Three lenses pass across the screen, each revealing different ideas.]

EV: To go wider still, three lenses that surface ideas you'd otherwise miss. One, form factor — does it even have to be an app? A voice assistant, a physical device, a feature inside something they already use? For this user the best answer might be a dead-simple dedicated device, or voice only. Two, moments — you can solve a problem before, during, or after it happens: prevent the confusion at setup, help during the call, reassure afterward. Three, other industries — how do other products solve a similar job? "Large, friendly, one-tap" is something children's toys nail — borrow it. Three lenses, and the ideas open up fast.

[VISUAL: A board fills with 5–6 solutions, each tagged to a pain.]

EV: After diverging, the board reads: one-tap video calling with large contact photos; voice-activated calling, just say a name; a simplified home screen with three big buttons; a favorites screen of family faces; auto-answer for trusted contacts; and a reassuring "call connected" confirmation. Six real ideas, each tied to a real pain. Strong, wide set — now you narrow.

[VISUAL: Quiet lower-third: "Subscribe to pmnorthstar."]

EV: If the ideas are flowing, subscribe to pmnorthstar — next video we turn this pile into a shippable MVP. Now, phase two: converge.

[VISUAL: Header: "CONVERGE." The ideas funnel; weaker ones fade, stronger ones rise.]

EV: Only now does judgment come back. Step back from the pile and group and filter. Which ideas cluster into a bigger concept? Which clearly hit the sharpest pains? Which are exciting but impractical? You're not fully prioritizing yet — that's the next step, with its own framework — just a first pass to surface the two or three strongest directions. "Voice-activated calling and the one-tap big-button screen are the real candidates — they hit the deepest pains." Narrowing out loud shows your judgment is as strong as your creativity.

[VISUAL: Header: "SAFE + BOLD." A balance between a reliable idea and a more unexpected one.]

EV: Quick note on the mix: balance safe and bold. Include one solid, obvious solution that clearly works — that shows you're grounded. And one bolder, unexpected idea — that shows range. Here, the safe idea is large buttons; the bold one is a feature that calls family automatically at a set time each day, so the person never has to initiate. The best answers show both instincts — dependable and imaginative.

[VISUAL: Header: "TWO TRAPS."]

EV: Two traps. One, converging too early — don't marry your first idea; force yourself to three or four before judging. Two, the opposite — never converging: twenty ideas, then a dead stop, leaving the interviewer a messy pile with no direction. Always end by pointing at your top two or three. Wide, then narrow — both halves, every time.

[VISUAL: Recap: the wide-then-narrow motion, clean and quick.]

EV: So, brainstorming done right: diverge, then converge, don't mix them. Anchor every idea to a pain so you never run dry. Use the three lenses — form factors, moments, other industries — to widen. Balance one safe idea with one bold one. Finish by narrowing to your strongest two or three. Creativity and judgment — the interviewer's reading both.

[VISUAL: Header: "NARROW vs WIDE." Split screen: "WEAK" with one idea, "STRONG" with a fan of ideas across lenses.]

EV: Let me make the wide habit concrete, because this one difference separates a flat answer from a strong one. Take the pain "can't read the small text." Weak answer: one idea — make the font bigger — move on. Technically fine, but thin. Strong answer goes wide on the same pain with the lenses. Form factor: bigger font, sure, but why read at all? Make it voice-driven, or use large family photos instead of names. Moments: prevent it at setup by configuring large text the first time. Other industries: children's apps go large, colorful, icon-first — borrow it. Same pain — one candidate offered a single obvious tweak; the other spanned voice, visuals, setup, and borrowed design in about twenty seconds. The interviewer hears a flexible mind instead of a one-note one. Going wide isn't extra work — it's most of the impression.
