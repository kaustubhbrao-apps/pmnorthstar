EV: Every product manager hits this, and every good interview recreates it: ten reasonable ideas, resources to build one. Which one — and how you justify it — is the moment you either sound like someone who ships products, or someone who collects feature requests. This video is about making that call.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "STEP 5 · PRIORITIZE / MVP."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Last video we built a strong pile of solutions. But you can't build everything — time and resources are finite — and the interviewer is watching to see if you can decide. Step five is judgment: narrowing to the one thing you'd ship first. The MVP — minimum viable product.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHAT 'MVP' ACTUALLY MEANS." A skateboard → scooter → bike → car progression, next to a half-built car that doesn't move.]

EV: First, be precise about MVP, because it's badly misunderstood. An MVP is not a cheap, broken, half-finished product. It's the smallest thing you can build that delivers real value and lets you learn. The classic image: if the goal is getting someone from A to B, the MVP is a skateboard — not a car with no wheels. Small, but it actually works and actually helps. So your MVP is the smallest slice that genuinely solves your user's sharpest pain. Small, but real.

[VISUAL: Header: "VALUE vs EFFORT." A 2×2 grid draws itself: "Value" up, "Effort" right.]

EV: How do you choose? The fastest, cleanest tool for an interview is the value-versus-effort grid. A simple two-by-two: one axis is value to the user, the other is effort to build. Drop your ideas into the four boxes and the winner usually sorts itself out.

[VISUAL: The four quadrants label themselves as EV names them, with example dots.]

EV: Top-left, high value, low effort — the quick wins, do first. Top-right, high value, high effort — big bets, worth it, but later. Bottom-left, low value, low effort — nice-to-haves. Bottom-right, low value, high effort — the money pit, avoid. Your MVP lives top-left: most value for the least build. "One-tap video calling with large buttons is high value — deepest pain — and relatively low effort. That's the quick win, that's my MVP." Clear, and easy to defend.

[VISUAL: Header: "RICE — IF YOU WANT MORE RIGOR." Four letters build: Reach · Impact · Confidence · Effort.]

EV: Want more rigor, or the interviewer pushes? There's a sharper tool, RICE. Four factors: Reach — how many users? Impact — how much does it help each one? Confidence — how sure are you it works? Effort — how much to build? Reach times impact times confidence, divided by effort. You don't need exact arithmetic in an interview — just use the shape of it out loud: "this one reaches everyone, high impact, low effort — it wins on RICE." We go deep on RICE in the prioritization series, but value-versus-effort alone carries most product-sense answers.

[VISUAL: Header: "COMMIT — OUT LOUD, WITH A REASON." A single idea gets an "MVP" marker.]

EV: What separates a strong answer here from a weak one is simply committing. Weak candidates hedge — "maybe this one, or that one, they're all kind of good" — and never choose. Strong candidates plant a flag: "Of everything, I'd ship this first, because it solves the sharpest pain for the least effort." Said with conviction, reason attached. Even if the interviewer disagrees, a clear, reasoned decision beats an uncertain maybe. Product managers are paid to decide — show you can.

[VISUAL: Header: "SEQUENCE THE REST." A roadmap: MVP, then "v2," then "v3."]

EV: And one addition that makes the answer feel complete: don't just name the MVP — briefly sequence what comes after. "Ship one-tap calling first. Once it's working, version two adds voice activation for people who can't manage even large buttons, and version three adds the automatic family-call feature." That shows you think in roadmaps, not one-offs — you see the product evolving. Fifteen seconds, and it reads as real product maturity.

[VISUAL: Header: "TWO TRAPS."]

EV: Two traps. One, the never-ending MVP — candidates pack so much in that it's really the whole product. Twelve features isn't minimum. Be strict: what's the one thing that delivers the core value? Everything else is version two. Two, picking the fun idea over the valuable one. Tempting to lead with your most creative idea, but if the plain big-button screen delivers more value than the flashy AI feature, big buttons win. Value for the user beats impressive for the interviewer.

[VISUAL: Recap: the 2×2 with the top-left quick-win marked, and a small roadmap beside it.]

EV: So, narrowing to your MVP: it's the smallest thing that delivers real value — a working skateboard, not a broken car. Use value-versus-effort to find the quick win, reach for RICE if you want rigor, commit out loud with a reason, and sequence what's next. Dodge the bloated MVP and the fun-over-valuable trap. Do that, and you sound like someone who ships.

[VISUAL: Header: "PLACING IDEAS ON THE GRID." The 2×2 appears; four idea-dots drop into quadrants.]

EV: Watch the grid sort real ideas — it's fast. Elderly-app ideas. One, a large one-tap calling screen: value high, it hits the sharpest pain; effort low. Top-left — quick win, MVP candidate. Two, voice-activated calling: value high, genuinely helps shaky hands; effort high, because voice reliability is hard for elderly speech. Top-right — big bet for later. Three, a daily trivia game for seniors: value low, not the core job; effort medium. Cut it, it's a distraction. Four, a "call connected" confirmation sound: value medium, it kills anxiety; effort tiny. Top-left again — easy add, bundle it with the MVP. Twenty seconds, and the grid turned four competing ideas into a decision: ship the one-tap screen plus the confirmation, save voice for version two, drop the game. That's the grid reasoning out loud, in front of the interviewer.
