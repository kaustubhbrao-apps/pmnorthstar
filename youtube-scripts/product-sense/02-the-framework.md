[VISUAL: Six empty stepping-stones across a dark field, leading to a marker: "A STRUCTURED ANSWER."]

EV: One path, six steps. Learn these, and you can walk into almost any "design a product" question and immediately have somewhere to begin — which, when the prompt could be about anything, is most of the battle. This is the skeleton the whole series runs on, so let's build it properly.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "THE FRAMEWORK."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Last video: what these questions test — empathy, structure, creativity plus judgment, prioritization plus metrics. A good framework produces all four almost as a byproduct of following it. So here's the framework. I'll walk it once, fast, then later videos go deep on each step.

[VISUAL: Six stones light up with labels: CLARIFY · USER · NEEDS & PAINS · SOLUTIONS · PRIORITIZE / MVP · METRICS.]

EV: The path: clarify, pick your user, find their needs and pains, generate solutions, prioritize to an MVP, define your metrics. Clarify, user, needs, solutions, prioritize, metrics. Memorize those six and they do the structuring for you. Let's walk each one.

[VISUAL: Stone 1 "CLARIFY" enlarges. Question-mark bubbles.]

EV: Step one, clarify. Before you design anything, scope the question. Repeat the prompt back, ask the goal, pin down constraints. "Design an app for the elderly — are we solving a specific problem or is it open-ended? Any platform? A business goal behind it?" This buys you a moment to think, and it signals you don't build before you understand the problem. Thirty to sixty seconds here — enough to frame it, not so long you stall.

[VISUAL: Stone 2 "USER" enlarges. A crowd narrows to one figure.]

EV: Step two, the user — where most answers quietly go wrong, because the instinct is to design for everyone. Instead: list the segments, pick one, out loud, with a reason. "The elderly could mean the tech-comfortable sixty-five-year-old, or the eighty-five-year-old who's never used a smartphone. I'll take the second group — that's where the pain is sharpest." Narrowing isn't weakness; it's the clearest signal of product maturity you can give. A product for everyone is a product for no one.

[VISUAL: Stone 3 "NEEDS & PAINS" enlarges. The figure, with frustrations shown.]

EV: Step three, needs and pains — the heart of it. Get inside that user's day. What are they trying to do, and where does it hurt? Not solutions yet — the real problems. For the eighty-five-year-old: shaky hands make small buttons impossible, poor eyesight makes text unreadable, there's real anxiety about "breaking" the phone, and underneath it, a sense of being cut off from family. List several. This is where empathy turns concrete — and the sharper your pains, the better every later step gets.

[VISUAL: Stone 4 "SOLUTIONS" enlarges. Several lightbulbs.]

EV: Step four, solutions — now you get creative, and the move is to go wide first. For each major pain, a response. Shaky hands — large tap targets, or voice control. Can't read it — oversized fonts, high contrast. Afraid of breaking it — a simplified, locked-down mode. Three or four real options on the table. We'll cover how to do this well, without running dry, later.

[VISUAL: Stone 5 "PRIORITIZE / MVP" enlarges. Ideas sort into a ranked list; top one marked "MVP."]

EV: Step five, prioritize to an MVP. You've got a pile, you can't build it all, and the interviewer wants to see you decide. Rank them — usually impact against effort — and pick the one or two with the most value for your user, soonest. "The biggest unlock is one-tap video calling with large buttons — that's the MVP." Committing to a first slice is what separates a product manager from a list of ideas.

[VISUAL: Quiet lower-third: "Subscribe to pmnorthstar."]

EV: If the framework's landing, subscribe to pmnorthstar — the deep dives on each step are coming. One stone left, and it's the one people skip most.

[VISUAL: Stone 6 "METRICS" enlarges. A target with an arrow, a rising number.]

EV: Step six, metrics. How would you know it worked? Name the success metric. For the video-calling app: completed calls per user per week — because it maps straight onto the real goal, reconnecting the person with family. People forget this step, and it's some of the easiest credit in the whole answer. Closing with "here's how I'd measure success" is what makes you sound like someone who's actually shipped.

[VISUAL: The six stones form a continuous lit path.]

EV: That's the path. Clarify, user, needs, solutions, prioritize, metrics — six steps that turn an open-ended, nerve-wracking prompt into a calm, structured answer.

[VISUAL: A clock divides a 30-minute interview across the six steps.]

EV: Quick note on pacing, because a framework's useless if you spend twenty minutes on step one. In a thirty-minute question: a minute or two to clarify, a couple on the user, several digging into needs and pains — that's the core, give it room — a few on solutions, a few prioritizing, a minute or two on metrics. Needs-and-pains and solutions get the most time. Clarify and metrics are quick but not optional. Keep moving — an answer that never reaches metrics feels unfinished, however strong the middle was.

[VISUAL: The framework shrinks to a corner badge: "C-U-N-S-P-M." A second arrow appears for "IMPROVE" questions.]

EV: And remember — the same skeleton handles "improve product X." Clarify establishes the product's goal, user picks who it's underserving, needs becomes "where's this falling short for them?" Then solutions, prioritize, metrics — exactly the same. One framework, both flavors. That's why it's worth knowing cold.

[VISUAL: Header: "A SECOND PROMPT, FAST." A new prompt: "Design a fitness app for busy parents." The six stones light in sequence.]

EV: To prove it isn't tied to one example, a thirty-second pass on a totally different prompt — a fitness app for busy parents. Clarify: standalone app, goal is to actually get them exercising, open-ended. User: busy parents is broad, so focus on the parent of young kids with almost no free time — sharpest pain. Needs: functionally, a workout that fits tiny windows; emotionally, guilt and defeat; socially, wanting to feel they're still looking after themselves. Solutions: ten-minute no-equipment workouts; ones you can do while watching the kids; a two-minute option for the worst days. Prioritize: the MVP is the ten-minute no-equipment workout — high value, low effort. Metrics: workouts completed per user per week. Same six stones, different product, thirty seconds — that's the point of one engine for all of it.
