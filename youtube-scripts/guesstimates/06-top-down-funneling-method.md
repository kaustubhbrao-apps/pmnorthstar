EV: Hey hey, EV here from pmnorthstar, and this is method number two — the top-down funneling approach. If bottom-up is building a pyramid brick by brick, top-down is pouring a giant number through a series of filters until just the right amount drips out the bottom. It's the perfect partner to bottom-up, and once you've got both, honestly, no estimation question can touch you. Let's build it.

[VISUAL: Title card: "TOP-DOWN FUNNELING." A downward funnel animates, layers labeled with percentage filters.]

EV: Here's the core idea. Top-down runs the exact opposite direction from bottom-up. Instead of building up from a single unit, you start with a giant *known* quantity — usually a total population, or a total market — and you pass it through a series of filters. Each filter narrows the number down a little more, until what drops out the bottom is your answer.

[VISUAL: A stack of sieves appears. A huge pile of dots pours in the top; at each sieve, some dots are kept (glow) and some fall away (fade). A small glowing cluster emerges at the bottom.]

EV: Picture a stack of sieves, or that funnel. A huge number goes in the top. At each layer, you keep a percentage and filter the rest away. Keep multiplying by fractions as you go down, and the number that survives all the way to the bottom — that's your estimate. Simple, visual, powerful.

[VISUAL: Lightbulb icon. Text: "USE TOP-DOWN WHEN… you have a rock-solid big number to start from."]

EV: So when do you reach for this one? Here's your trigger. Use top-down when you've got a reliable *anchor* at the top — a big number you're genuinely confident about, like a country's population or a city's population — and the real question is just "what fraction of that giant number ends up in the specific bucket I care about?" If you can smell a solid population or market number at the top, funnel it.

[VISUAL: Section header slams: "THE 4-STEP FRAMEWORK." Four cards flip in face-down.]

EV: The framework — four steps again, mirror image of bottom-up. Let's flip them over.

[VISUAL: Card 1 flips: "1 · PICK THE ANCHOR." A giant number "140 CRORE / 1.4 BILLION" glows at the top of a funnel.]

EV: Step one — pick your anchor, the number at the top of the funnel. This is almost always a population or a total market you know cold. A country's population — India's about one point four billion, the US about three hundred thirty million. A city's population. The total number of households. Here's the rule: choose the *broadest* number that still has your answer hiding somewhere inside it. Start wide, then narrow.

[VISUAL: Card 2 flips: "2 · BUILD THE CHAIN OF FILTERS." A vertical list of conditions appears, each with a little funnel icon.]

EV: Step two — build your chain of filters. List out the conditions something has to satisfy to actually count toward your answer, from broadest to narrowest. Each filter is just a fraction between zero and one. Say the question is "how many people stream a paid video service." Your filters might be: has a smartphone or TV, has decent internet, can afford a subscription, and actually pays versus just using a free tier. Each one chops the number down.

[VISUAL: Card 3 flips: "3 · APPLY EACH FRACTION IN SEQUENCE." Numbers multiply down a cascade, each step smaller.]

EV: Step three — apply each fraction in sequence. Multiply the anchor by the first fraction, then that result by the next, and so on down the funnel. And — super important — narrate *why* you pick each fraction as you go. Not just "times sixty percent," but "times sixty percent, *because* that's roughly who's in the smartphone-using age band." The reasoning out loud is what they're grading.

[VISUAL: Card 4 flips: "4 · READ THE BOTTOM & SANITY-CHECK." A number emerges from the funnel's spout; magnifying glass sweeps with a green check.]

EV: And step four — read off the bottom, and sanity-check. Whatever survived all your filters is your answer. Then step back and ask the magic question: "does this final number actually make sense against anything else I know?" Same discipline as bottom-up. Never skip it.

[VISUAL: Section wipe. Header: "LET'S SEE IT LIVE — SMARTPHONE USERS." A phone icon floats up.]

EV: Let's run a quick one so you feel it. Question: how many smartphone users are there in a big country — let's use India. Watch the funnel work.

[VISUAL: Numbers cascade down a funnel as EV speaks: "1.4 BILLION" → "× 70% (age band) = 980M" → "× 65% (can afford/operate) = ~640M."]

EV: Anchor: India's population, about one point four billion. Filter one — age. Strip out the very young kids and the very old who mostly don't use smartphones. Say seventy percent are in the plausible smartphone-using age band — that's about nine hundred eighty million. Filter two — can they actually afford and operate one? Income, rural reach, literacy mean not everyone in that band has a phone. Say sixty-five percent of them do. Nine hundred eighty million times sixty-five percent is about six hundred forty million.

[VISUAL: "≈ 640 MILLION SMARTPHONE USERS" pulses, then a check: "real-world estimates: 600–750M ✓".]

EV: Out the bottom — roughly six hundred forty million smartphone users. And real-world published figures sit right around six hundred to seven hundred fifty million, so our funnel landed exactly where it should. Two filters, one clean answer. *That's* top-down.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE QUESTIONS YOU *MUST* ASK FIRST." Question bubbles bounce in.]

EV: Before you build any funnel, you scope — same as bottom-up, slightly different flavor. Here are the ones that matter most for a top-down problem.

[VISUAL: Each question pops as a clean bullet with an icon, timed to EV.]

EV: One — geography. "What's the scope — a city, a state, the whole country?" This literally decides your anchor, so get it first. Two — the segment. "Any specific demographic in scope? Only adults? Only urban? A certain income band?" Each one is a filter you'll need. Three — people, transactions, or money? "Are we estimating *users*, or *purchases per year*, or *revenue*?" Because a funnel to 'number of users' is different from a funnel to 'number of purchases' — that second one needs an extra frequency step at the end. Four — a point in time, or over a period? "Users right now, versus users who did something across a whole year?" Changes whether you add a time dimension. And five — any obvious sub-case to include or exclude? For streaming: "do we count free-tier users, or only paying subscribers?" Naming that up front saves you from a factor-of-two blunder.

[VISUAL: "SCOPING = POINTS ✓" stamp appears.]

EV: Ask these and you instantly signal that you define the boundaries of a problem before charging in. Interviewers eat that up.

[VISUAL: Header, slightly cautionary color: "WHERE TOP-DOWN GOES WRONG." Three warning icons.]

EV: Now the three classic ways people wreck a funnel — dodge these and you're golden.

[VISUAL: Mistake 1 — a funnel with way too many layers; the number ping-pongs wildly and ends up absurd.]

EV: Mistake one — too many filters. This is the big one. Every filter you add is another assumption that can be wrong, and the errors *compound*. Funnel through seven filters and a small misjudgment in each one can throw you off by a massive multiple by the time you hit the bottom. So keep your funnel to three or four *meaningful* filters. Combine the small ones, skip the trivial ones. Fewer, stronger filters beat many weak ones every time.

[VISUAL: Mistake 2 — two filters labeled "urban" and "high income" overlap in a Venn diagram, double-counting the overlap.]

EV: Mistake two — overlapping filters that secretly double-count. If one filter is "urban residents" and another is "high-income people," careful — those two overlap a lot, and applying both as if they're independent can wrongly shrink your answer. Make sure each filter is a genuinely *distinct* cut, not a repeat of one you already applied.

[VISUAL: Mistake 3 — a shaky anchor number wobbles, and the whole funnel below it collapses.]

EV: And mistake three — a shaky anchor. If you get that top number badly wrong, nothing downstream can save you — the whole funnel inherits the error. So anchor on numbers you're genuinely confident about. Populations are safest. And if you're unsure of the anchor, just say so out loud and pick a defensible round figure. Honesty about your anchor is a strength, not a weakness.

[VISUAL: Split: "TOP-DOWN → fraction of a big known number" glowing, "BOTTOM-UP → build from one unit" faded.]

EV: So — your quick rule for picking this method. Reach for top-down whenever the question is basically "what fraction of some big, known population or market ends up *here*?" — and you've got a solid anchor to start from. How many people watch a huge sporting event, how many credit cards are active in a country, how many students sit an exam, what's the market size for electric scooters. All natural funnels — clean population up top, chain of narrowing conditions down to the answer.

[VISUAL: Two methods shown converging on one number with a handshake animation. Text: "DO BOTH = INSTANT SANITY CHECK."]

EV: And here's a pro habit to leave you with — for a lot of questions, you can actually solve it *both* ways, bottom-up AND top-down. And if two totally independent methods land near each other? That's a built-in sanity check, and it's the strongest signal you can possibly give an interviewer. The best candidates pull this move when they've got time. Keep it in your pocket.
