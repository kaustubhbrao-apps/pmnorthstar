[VISUAL: Header: "STEP ZERO · SCOPE IT." Question bubbles pop, one highlighting a clock.]

EV: Step zero — scope, and it's extra important here. I'd pin down: "Friday night" — let's define it as the peak window, roughly seven PM to one AM, so six hours. Delhi city, about twenty million people. Uber specifically, so I'll need a market-share step at the end — flagging it now. And we're counting *completed rides*, not app opens. Locked. Notice I defined the time window explicitly — that's going to shape the entire funnel.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 1 · THE ANCHOR." A giant "20 MILLION" glows at the top of the funnel.]

EV: Step one — the anchor. Delhi's population, about twenty million. Top of the funnel, rock solid. Now we filter down to the people actually taking a ride-hail trip in that Friday-night window.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 2 · FUNNEL TO FRIDAY-NIGHT RIDERS." Three layers ready to fill.]

EV: Step two — the filters. And because of the time window, these filters are about who's *out and riding during those six hours* — not just who exists. Watch the cascade.

[VISUAL: Filter 1 fills: "OUT ON FRIDAY NIGHT · 20% → 4M." Icons of people dining, at events, commuting late.]

EV: Filter one — who's actually mobile and out on a Friday night? Strip out the kids, the elderly, and the huge chunk of people who are simply home for the evening. The folks genuinely out and about — dining, socializing, at events, heading home late from work — are a minority. Say twenty percent of the city is out during this window. Twenty million times twenty percent is four million people out on the town.

[VISUAL: Filter 2 fills: "RIDE-HAILING USERS · 25% → 1M." A smartphone-cab icon, with faded alternatives: bus, metro, own car, auto.]

EV: Filter two — of those who are out, who uses app-based cabs, rather than driving their own car, taking the metro, a bus, an auto, or a two-wheeler? Ride-hailing's a specific slice — people without a car handy, or who've been drinking, or who just want the convenience. Say twenty-five percent of the out-and-about crowd are app-cab users for this trip. Four million times twenty-five percent is one million potential riders.

[VISUAL: Filter 3 fills: "ACTUALLY RIDE TONIGHT · 40% → 400K people." A "booked ✓" icon.]

EV: Filter three — do they actually take a ride-hail trip in this specific window? Not every ride-hail user rides every Friday night. Of that one million, say forty percent actually book a trip between seven and one. That's four hundred thousand people riding.

[VISUAL: A new step appears: "× 1.4 round-trip factor → 560K trips." Two arrows showing out-and-back.]

EV: But hold on — here's a step people forget. Many of these folks take a *round* trip — out, and then back home. So one rider often equals more than one trip. Say on average one-point-four rides per person who rides — some go one-way, taking the metro home, some do the full round trip. Four hundred thousand people times one-point-four is about five hundred sixty thousand total ride-hail trips on a Friday night — across *all* apps.

EV: Quick nudge — if you're catching these wrinkles, subscribe to pmnorthstar so the finale lands in your feed. Now — the market-share step, just like our food-delivery example.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 3 · MARKET SHARE." A pie chart splits between two ride apps; Uber's slice highlights at 45%.]

EV: Step three — market share. We asked about Uber specifically. And Delhi ride-hailing is mainly a contest between Uber and its big rival Ola, with autos on the platforms too. Say the app-cab market splits roughly evenly, giving Uber about forty-five percent. Five hundred sixty thousand times forty-five percent is about two hundred fifty thousand.

[VISUAL: "≈ 2.5 LAKH / 250,000 UBER RIDES" pulses in the 7PM–1AM window.]

EV: So — roughly two and a half lakh Uber rides on a Friday night in Delhi, in that six-hour window. A lakh's a hundred thousand, so about two hundred fifty thousand rides. There's our answer.

[VISUAL: Header: "SANITY CHECK · TWO ANGLES." Magnifying glass sweeps.]

EV: Sanity check, two ways. First, from the supply side — can the fleet even handle that? Two hundred fifty thousand rides over a six-hour peak is about forty-two thousand rides an hour at the peak. If Uber's got, say, fifty to eighty thousand active drivers in Delhi on a busy Friday, and each does two or three trips in a peak hour, that's well over a hundred thousand trips an hour of *capacity*. So forty-two thousand an hour is comfortably servable. Supply and demand line up. Second, from the top — across a full day Uber Delhi might do around a million rides, and concentrating a big chunk of demand into the Friday-evening peak at two hundred fifty thousand is a believable fraction of that. Consistent again. The number holds.

[VISUAL: Header: "TWO LESSONS THIS ONE TEACHES." Two icons slam in.]

EV: Two lessons here that'll genuinely save you in an interview.

[VISUAL: Lesson 1 — a clock with a highlighted window. "TIME WINDOWS RESHAPE THE FUNNEL."]

EV: Lesson one — time windows need explicit handling. Because the question said *Friday night*, I couldn't use daily behavior. I had to define the window — six hours — then filter to people active *in that window*, and count trips *within it*. A candidate who estimates daily rides and forgets the question only asked about Friday night has answered a completely different question. Always let the time scope reshape the funnel from the start.

[VISUAL: Lesson 2 — one person icon splits into "out trip + back trip." "COUNT TRIPS, NOT JUST PEOPLE."]

EV: Lesson two — round trips. We caught that one person often generates more than one ride — the out-and-back. Multiplying riders by a trips-per-person factor is such an easy step to skip, and skipping it quietly understates your answer. So whenever your unit is a "trip" rather than a "person," stop and ask: does one person produce multiple units here? Often, yes.

[VISUAL: Header: "AND THE MARKET-SHARE REMINDER." A pie chart with a bright slice.]

EV: And the recurring reminder — the moment the question named a specific company, Uber, in a competitive market, we *had* to funnel the whole-market number down by market share. This is one of the most reliable patterns in the entire guesstimate game: *named company, in a known market, means a market-share step.* Build the total market first, then take the company's slice. Skip it, and you'll land at double or triple the real figure — guaranteed.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE FOLLOW-UP TO BE READY FOR." A rain cloud, a transit-strike sign, fireworks for New Year's.]

EV: And the curveball to prep for: "how would this change during a transport strike, or heavy rain, or New Year's Eve?" Don't recompute from scratch — reason about which *filter moves*, and in which direction. A metro strike pushes the "ride-hailing users" filter way *up*, because people lose their alternative. Rain does the same, and also bumps the round-trip factor. New Year's Eve inflates the "out on the night" filter dramatically *and* stretches the time window past one AM. Naming the exact filter each scenario moves, and which way, is what shows the interviewer you understand the *machinery* behind your number — not just the arithmetic. That's the senior-level signal.
