EV: Hey hey, EV here from pmnorthstar. This is our very first full worked example, and we're starting with the absolute classic — tea. Specifically chai, which if you're watching from outside India is just spiced milk tea, and it is *everywhere* in Indian cities. We'll use Mumbai, a mega-city of about twenty million people. But I want you to notice — the *method* works for coffee in São Paulo, tea in London, anything, anywhere. Watch how we build it.

[VISUAL: Four framework cards from last video flash briefly in the corner as a reminder: Define · Estimate one · Count · Multiply + check.]

EV: Remember our four steps from the last video? Define the unit, estimate one, count them, multiply and sanity-check. Let's run them.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP ZERO · SCOPE IT." Question bubbles pop.]

EV: But first — step zero, always. We scope. Before I touch a single number, I'd turn to my interviewer and ask: are we counting *sold* tea only — the roadside stalls, cafés, railway vendors, office canteens — and leaving out tea people brew at home? That's the natural reading of "sold," so let's confirm it and exclude home-brewed. I'd also confirm we mean the greater Mumbai area on a normal weekday, not a festival, and that we're counting cups, not money. Scope locked. See how much cleaner that makes everything?

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 1 · UNIT & POPULATION." A single person icon, then "20,000,000" counts up.]

EV: Step one — our unit is a single Mumbai resident, and we're estimating how many *purchased* cups of tea they drink per day. And our population anchor: Mumbai's metro area, roughly two crore people — that's twenty million for everyone watching who doesn't count in crores. A crore is just ten million, so two crore equals twenty million. We'll anchor on that.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 2 · SEGMENT THE DRINKERS." The 20M figure splits into three colored blocks that slide apart.]

EV: Now step two, and here's where we get smart — we segment. Because not everyone buys tea, and the people who do buy it at wildly different rates. If I used one blurry average for everyone, my answer would be mushy and hard to defend. So let's split the city into three clean groups.

[VISUAL: Block 1 glows, labeled "REGULAR · 40% · 8M people · 2.5 cups/day." A commuter, a shopkeeper, a laborer icon.]

EV: Group one — the regulars. Office workers, shopkeepers, laborers, commuters — the folks grabbing two or three cups a day from a stall or canteen. These are your heavy hitters. Say that's about forty percent of the city — that's eight million people — and they average, let's call it, two and a half purchased cups a day each.

[VISUAL: Block 2 glows, labeled "OCCASIONAL · 30% · 6M people · 1 cup/day." A student, a homemaker icon.]

EV: Group two — the occasional buyers. One cup a day, or a few times a week that averages out to about one. Students, people who mostly drink tea at home but grab one outside now and then. Say thirty percent — six million people — at one cup a day each.

[VISUAL: Block 3 glows, labeled "RARE / NON · 30% · 6M people · 0.1 cups/day." A child icon, a "no tea" icon.]

EV: And group three — the rare or non-buyers. Young children, people who never drink tea, folks who only ever have it at home. The remaining thirty percent — six million people. We'll give them a token one-tenth of a cup a day, which just captures the occasional rare purchase. Clean three-way split. Everybody's accounted for.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 3 · MULTIPLY EACH SEGMENT." Three multiplication lines animate one after another, numbers snapping into place.]

EV: Step three — multiply each group out. Stay with me, and watch the zeros. Regulars: eight million people times two and a half cups equals twenty million cups. Occasionals: six million times one equals six million cups. Rare: six million times one-tenth equals six hundred thousand cups. Three numbers, three segments, each one defensible on its own.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 4 · ADD IT UP." The three results stack and sum with a satisfying snap. Big number appears: "≈ 26.6 MILLION CUPS/DAY."]

EV: And step four — add them together. Twenty million, plus six million, plus six hundred thousand. That gives us roughly twenty-six and a half million cups of tea. So our answer: somewhere around twenty-five to twenty-seven million cups of tea sold in Mumbai every single day. Boom. There it is.

EV: Quick break — if you're following along and it's clicking, hit subscribe to pmnorthstar, it genuinely helps us make more of these. Okay, but we are NOT done. Because a great candidate never stops at the number. We sanity-check.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE SANITY CHECK · TWO WAYS." A magnifying glass sweeps in.]

EV: And I'm going to show you something that seriously impresses interviewers — checking your answer *two different ways*. First, the per-person view. Twenty-six and a half million cups across twenty million people is about one-point-three purchased cups per person per day, averaged across everyone, including the people who never drink it. For a city where tea is basically a daily ritual, a little over one purchased cup per person feels completely right — maybe even a touch conservative. Good. The number survives.

[VISUAL: A second check animates — a tea stall icon, "300 cups/day per stall," divides into the total, revealing "≈ 88,000 stalls → 1 per 230 people."]

EV: But here's the power move. Let's check it from a totally different angle — the supply side. If a typical tea stall sells, say, three hundred cups a day, then twenty-six and a half million cups would need about eighty-eight thousand tea-selling points across Mumbai. For a city of twenty million, that's one tea outlet for every two hundred and thirty people. And if you've ever walked a Mumbai street — there's a stall on practically every corner. So that's not just believable, it might even be a little low, which tells us our estimate is nicely conservative. Two completely independent methods landing in the same neighborhood? That is the strongest signal you can give that your reasoning is solid.

[VISUAL: Replay montage — the four steps flash by quickly as a recap filmstrip.]

EV: So let's zoom out on what just happened, because this is the template for every bottom-up example. We scoped the question before calculating. We chose the individual as our unit, because that's where our intuition is strongest. We split into three segments instead of one mushy average, which made every single assumption easy to defend. We did clean arithmetic with rounded numbers and kept our zeros straight. And then we validated the whole thing two separate ways. The twenty-six million? Honestly, never the point. The *structure* was the point.

[VISUAL: Header: "BONUS · TURN IT INTO MONEY." A ₹ symbol and a $ symbol float up together.]

EV: And here's a bonus that shows you how powerful a clean structure is. What if the interviewer leans in and goes, "nice — now estimate the daily *revenue* of Mumbai's tea market"? Watch how little extra work that is. You already have twenty-six and a half million cups. Just layer a price on top — say an average of twelve rupees a cup, which is roughly fifteen US cents. Twenty-six and a half million times twelve is about thirty-two crore rupees a day — call it three point eight million dollars daily, or well over a billion dollars a year. Same skeleton, one extra multiplication. That is exactly why we obsess over building a clean structure — it extends to any follow-up they throw at you, effortlessly.
