[VISUAL: Title card: "THE BOTTOM-UP APPROACH." An upward arrow animates from floor to ceiling.]

EV: Here's the core idea in one sentence. In bottom-up, you build your answer from the smallest unit *upward*. Instead of starting with some giant number and slicing it down, you start with one single thing — one person, one store, one machine, one hour — you figure out what that one unit does, and then you multiply all the way up to the whole population.

[VISUAL: The pyramid reappears. One brick highlights and glows. A label reads "1 brick = known weight." Then the whole pyramid glows: "total = weight × number of bricks."]

EV: Picture that pyramid again. You know how much one brick weighs, and you know roughly how many bricks are in the pyramid. So you can find the weight of the entire thing — multiply one by the other — without ever putting the pyramid on a scale. That's the whole philosophy. Small thing you understand, times how many of them there are, equals big thing you don't.

[VISUAL: A lightbulb icon pops. Text: "USE BOTTOM-UP WHEN… you understand the individual unit."]

EV: So when do you reach for this method? Here's your trigger. Use bottom-up when you have a really good *feel* for the behavior of a single unit. If the question is about something one person does in their daily life — how much tea they drink, how many messages they send, how often they order food, how many times they get a haircut — you almost definitely have solid gut instinct about that one person. Because you *are* a person. You've watched people do these things your whole life. Bottom-up lets you cash in on that everyday intuition.

[VISUAL: Section header slams: "THE 4-STEP FRAMEWORK." Four numbered cards flip in, face-down, ready to reveal.]

EV: Alright, the framework. Four steps. Memorize these four and you can run bottom-up on anything. Let's flip them over one at a time.

[VISUAL: Card 1 flips: "1 · DEFINE THE UNIT & THE OUTPUT." A fuzzy blob labeled "a person?" sharpens into a crisp figure labeled "an average city resident · tea cups per day."]

EV: Step one — define your unit and your output. Decide what your single building block is. Usually it's a person, a household, a store, or a device. And here's the key — be *precise*. "A person" is way too vague. "An average resident of this city, and the number of tea cups they drink per day" — now *that's* a unit you can actually estimate. Specificity is your friend. The sharper you define the unit, the easier everything downstream gets.

[VISUAL: Card 2 flips: "2 · ESTIMATE ONE UNIT'S BEHAVIOR." A single person icon with a thought bubble cycling "2? 3?" cups.]

EV: Step two — estimate the behavior of that one unit. This is where your everyday intuition does the heavy lifting. How many cups does one tea-drinker drink a day? Two, maybe three. And you *know* this — not because you looked it up, but because you're a human who has watched people drink tea. Pin that per-unit number down, say it out loud, and lock it in. Don't agonize. Your gut is good here.

[VISUAL: Card 3 flips: "3 · COUNT THE UNITS." A population figure "20,000,000" counts up rapidly, then a slider trims it down with a percentage.]

EV: Step three — count your units. How many of these things actually exist? This almost always routes through population. Say the city has twenty million people. But not everyone's a tea-drinker, right? So you apply a fraction — maybe sixty percent drink tea. Now you've got your real count of active units. Notice we're already combining a big number with a sensible percentage — totally natural.

[VISUAL: Card 4 flips: "4 · MULTIPLY UP & SANITY-CHECK." Two numbers multiply with a satisfying snap, then a magnifying glass sweeps over the result with a green check.]

EV: And step four — multiply it all up, and then sanity-check. Multiply the per-unit behavior by the number of units, and boom — there's your total. But don't stop there. Stop and ask: "does this number actually make sense?" If it doesn't, don't panic — just go back and find which assumption broke. That final gut-check is non-negotiable.

[VISUAL: Section wipe. Header: "LET'S SEE IT LIVE — MESSAGES IN A CITY." A phone icon floats up with chat bubbles.]

EV: Okay, four steps are nice, but let's actually run one fast so you feel the rhythm. Question: how many text-style messages get sent on a popular messaging app in a tech-heavy city in one day? Let's build it bottom-up.

[VISUAL: Step-by-step numbers animate in sequence on screen as EV speaks. "1.3 crore people" → that's glossed with "(13 million)" → "× 70% smartphone = 9 million users" → "× 50 messages/day" → "= 450 million messages."]

EV: Our unit is one smartphone user. Say the city has thirteen million people. Maybe seventy percent use smartphones — that's about nine million users. Now, how many messages does one person fire off a day? Think about your own thumbs — family chats, work groups, friends, memes. Call it fifty a day as a blend of heavy and light users. Multiply: nine million users times fifty messages. That's four hundred and fifty million messages a day. Done.

[VISUAL: The final number "450 MILLION" pulses. Then a magnifying glass sweeps in, showing "50 per person ✓ feels right for a connected city."]

EV: And the sanity check — four hundred fifty million messages across nine million people is fifty per person, which is exactly what we assumed and totally believable for a connected city. Here's what I want you to notice: we never needed to know a single thing about the app's servers or databases. We built the entire answer from one person's thumbs. That's the magic of bottom-up.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE QUESTIONS YOU *MUST* ASK FIRST." A row of question-mark bubbles bounces in.]

EV: Before you calculate a single thing, you buy yourself time *and* score major points by scoping the question. Great candidates always ask first. For a bottom-up problem, here are the questions that matter most.

[VISUAL: Each question pops onto screen as a clean bullet with an icon, timed to EV.]

EV: One — geography. "When you say the population, do you mean one specific city, or the whole country?" Scope changes everything, so nail it down immediately. Two — timing. "Are we counting a specific day — a weekday, a weekend, a holiday — or just an average day?" Tea consumption on a rainy Monday is not the same as on a festival. Three — the segment. "Should I include everyone, or a specific group? Do we count children? Tourists? Only city residents?" Four — count versus value. "Are we estimating volume — total cups — or value, the actual money spent?" Sometimes they secretly want revenue, and the path is different. And five — channels. "Any channel to include or exclude? For tea, do we count only roadside stalls, or also cafés and tea brewed at home?" That single question can swing your answer by five times.

[VISUAL: A little "NOT STALLING ✓ SCOPING" stamp appears.]

EV: And listen — asking these is not stalling. It's the opposite. It signals that you understand real problems are messy and ambiguous, and that defining the boundaries is step zero of any serious estimate. Interviewers love a candidate who scopes before they sprint.

[VISUAL: Header shifts, slightly cautionary color: "WHERE BOTTOM-UP GOES WRONG." Three warning icons line up.]

EV: Now let me save you from the three classic ways people blow this method, because if you avoid these you're ahead of most candidates.

[VISUAL: Mistake 1 appears — a confused figure next to a chai stall with "??? cups per stall" floating above.]

EV: Mistake one — picking a unit you have no intuition for. If you choose "one tea stall" as your unit, but you have no idea how many cups a stall sells or how many stalls exist — congrats, you just made the problem *harder*. Pick the unit you understand best, which is almost always the individual person. Play to your intuition.

[VISUAL: Mistake 2 — one blurry "average" bar splits into two crisp bars of very different heights.]

EV: Mistake two — forgetting to segment. If heavy tea-drinkers have five cups and occasional ones have one, and those groups are really different in size and behavior, don't smush them into one fuzzy average — split them. Estimate each group separately, then add them up. But — don't overdo it either. Two or three segments is plenty. More than that and you're just creating extra ways to be wrong.

[VISUAL: Mistake 3 — a number loses a zero and visibly wobbles; then zeros snap into neat place with "2 crore = 2 × 10⁷".]

EV: And mistake three — losing track of your zeros. When you're multiplying crores by tens, slow down for one second, write it down, count your zeros deliberately, and round to clean figures. Twenty million is two, times ten to the seven. Keep it in that tidy form if it helps. Dropping a zero is the single most common way a *perfect* reasoning chain produces a *ridiculous* final number.

[VISUAL: Split comparison: "BOTTOM-UP → individual behavior you know" vs a faded "TOP-DOWN → next video." Bottom-up side glows.]

EV: So — the quick rule for when to pick this method. Reach for bottom-up whenever the question centers on individual human behavior and you can confidently estimate what one person does. How many pizzas a chain sells in a city, how many rides get booked on a Friday night, how many haircuts happen in a day — all of these have a natural individual unit, and all of them are perfect bottom-up problems.
