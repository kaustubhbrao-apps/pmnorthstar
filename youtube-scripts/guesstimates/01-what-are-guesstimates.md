[VISUAL: Word "GUESS" slides in from left, word "ESTIMATE" slides in from right, they collide in the middle and fuse into "GUESSTIMATE" with a spark.]

EV: It's exactly what the word looks like — a guess, plus an estimate. You're asked for a number you cannot possibly know exactly. How many tea cups in a city. How many flights take off today. How many text messages get sent in your country every hour. And here's the thing that surprises people — you are *not* supposed to know it. Nobody knows it. You're supposed to *reason* your way to a number you can stand behind and defend.

[VISUAL: Split screen. Left: "PURE GUESS" — a number drops from the sky surrounded by floating question marks. Right: "ESTIMATE" — a little chain of boxes links together and builds up to a number. The right side glows green and pulses.]

EV: Let me make the difference super concrete. A pure guess is a number pulled straight out of thin air — "uhh, a billion?" An estimate is a number you *build*, step by step, with a chain of logic holding it up. And a guesstimate is that second thing done under pressure — a disciplined guess. A number you can walk another human through, out loud, brick by brick, so that even if they disagree with your final figure, they can't argue with *how you got there*.

[VISUAL: Record-scratch freeze. Big bold text pops onto screen: "BUT WHY DO INTERVIEWERS LOVE THESE SO MUCH?"]

EV: Now here is the single biggest thing people get wrong, so lean in. The interviewer sitting across from you? They genuinely do not care about your final number. At all.

[VISUAL: A final answer "6 MILLION" appears proudly. Then a big red X stamps over it. Then it dissolves and morphs into a glowing brain with gears spinning inside.]

EV: Let me prove it. Say you answer "four million," and the real number happens to be six million. You did *not* fail that question. You might've aced it. The number is almost never the point. What the interviewer is actually doing — the whole time — is watching how your brain behaves when it has zero data and no safety net. And here's why that matters: that is *literally the job* they're hiring for.

[VISUAL: Quick montage — a product manager at a whiteboard, a consultant pointing at a chart, an analyst staring at an empty spreadsheet. Labels pop in one by one: "How big is this market?" "How many users will this feature reach?" "Should we launch in a new city?" "What's the revenue upside?"]

EV: Think about it. In the real world of product and strategy, nobody walks over and hands you a tidy spreadsheet with the answer. You get handed the fog. How big is this opportunity? How many people would even use this thing? Is it worth building? And you have to size that unknown yourself — out loud, in a room, while smart people decide whether your logic holds up. A guesstimate is just that exact skill, squeezed down into a fifteen-minute test. It's a simulator for the real decisions you'll make every single week on the job.

If you're going into consulting anywhere on the planet, assume you'll get at least one. This is where the legendary questions come from — how many golf balls fit inside an airplane, how many gas stations are in the country, how many windows in a city. They sound absurd on purpose.

Sizing a market, forecasting how many users will adopt a feature, sanity-checking a wild growth projection. It's a guesstimate, except now it's your actual roadmap on the line.

[VISUAL: Big section header: "WHAT'S *REALLY* BEING TESTED?" Four empty numbered slots appear in a row, ready to fill.]

EV: So underneath all the tea cups and golf balls, every single guesstimate is secretly testing four specific things. If you know what they're looking for, you can deliver it on purpose. Watch.

[VISUAL: Slot 1 fills — icon of one big block splitting cleanly into many small blocks. Label snaps in: "STRUCTURED THINKING."]

EV: Number one — structured thinking. Can you take a huge, scary, vague question and break it down into small pieces that are each easy to handle on their own? This is the big one, the one that matters most. A strong candidate never attacks "how many tea cups" head-on. They break it apart — how many people, how many drink tea, how often, from where. And here's the secret: that structure basically *is* the answer. Get the breakdown right and the math is almost an afterthought.

[VISUAL: Slot 2 fills — icon of a hand confidently placing puzzle pieces. Label: "COMFORT WITH ASSUMPTIONS."]

EV: Number two — comfort with assumptions. You are going to have to assume things. A population. A percentage. An average. And interviewers want to watch you state each assumption clearly, pick a reasonable number, and then just *move on*. The people who freeze up trying to find the one "perfect" assumption — they sink. The people who go "let's assume twenty million people, I'll refine it later if I need to" — they absolutely fly. Confidence here is everything.

[VISUAL: Slot 3 fills — icon of numbers multiplying, clean zeros lining up in a neat row. Label: "NUMBER FLUENCY."]

EV: Number three — number fluency. Can you multiply big numbers, out loud, without a calculator, without panicking and without dropping a zero somewhere? You don't need to be a human computer, I promise. You need two things: round your numbers so they're clean to work with, and keep your zeros organized. We'll drill exactly how to do that so it feels easy.

[VISUAL: Slot 4 fills — icon of a magnifying glass hovering over a number, with a little speech bubble: "wait… does this make sense?" Label: "THE SANITY CHECK."]

EV: And number four — the sanity check. Oh, this is the one that separates good candidates from *great* ones. After you land on your number, do you stop, step back, and go — "hold on, does this even make sense?" If your guesstimate says a single city drinks more tea than is physically possible to brew in a day, a great candidate catches that and says it out loud. That willingness to pressure-test your *own* answer? Interviewers find it incredibly impressive, because it's exactly what they need from someone they're about to trust with real decisions.

[VISUAL: All four slots glow together, then a ribbon sweeps across and wraps all of them, labeled "COMMUNICATION."]

EV: And wrapped around all four of those — communication. Because here's the brutal truth: a brilliant guesstimate done silently inside your head is worth absolutely nothing in that room. The talking *is* the test. You have to narrate every step so clearly that the interviewer can follow your brain in real time. Think of it less like a math exam and more like giving someone a guided tour of your own reasoning.

[VISUAL: Background softens, warmer tone. EV's key message appears in big kinetic text that assembles letter by letter: "YOU CAN'T BE WRONG."]

EV: Okay, real talk for a second, because this is the mindset shift that changes everything. Most people walk into these questions absolutely terrified of being wrong. And that fear is the enemy — it makes you hesitate, hedge, second-guess, and freeze right up. So here's the reframe I want you to tattoo on your brain: you literally *cannot* be wrong. Because there is no right answer. There's only reasoning that holds together, and reasoning that falls apart. That's it. Those are the only two outcomes. The second you truly believe that, the whole exercise stops being a terrifying test of knowledge and becomes what it actually is — a genuinely fun conversation about how you think.
