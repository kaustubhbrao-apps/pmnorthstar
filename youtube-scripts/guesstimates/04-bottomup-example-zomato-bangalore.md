EV: Hey hey, EV here from pmnorthstar, back with bottom-up example number two. Today's question: how many orders does a major food-delivery app handle in a city on a typical day? I'll use Zomato — one of the big delivery apps in India — in Bangalore, India's tech capital. If you're watching from elsewhere, just picture your local delivery giant — the method's identical. And here's the twist: the unit is NOT just "a person" this time. Getting the unit right is the whole game. Watch.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP ZERO · SCOPE IT." Question bubbles pop in rapid-fire.]

EV: Step zero, same as always — scope. I'd confirm four things fast. One: Bangalore city proper, the urban area, not the whole state. Two: Zomato specifically, not Zomato plus every competitor combined — which means I'll need to deal with market share later, flag that now. Three: a normal weekday average, knowing weekends run hotter. And four: we're counting *delivered orders*, not app opens or people just browsing menus at midnight. Scope set. Let's build.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 1 · ANCHOR & FUNNEL TO THE REAL UNIT." A population figure appears, then narrows through stages like a shrinking tube.]

EV: Step one, and here's the big lesson of this whole video. In the tea example, our unit was basically everyone. Here? Most people in the city are *not* food-delivery customers. So if I just grab the whole population and multiply, I'll be massively wrong. Instead, I funnel down to find the *right* unit first. Watch the number shrink.

[VISUAL: Numbers animate in sequence: "1.3 crore (13M) people" → "× 70% smartphone = 9M" → "× 40% active delivery users = 3.6M."]

EV: Bangalore's urban population is about one point three crore — thirteen million. But now I filter. Smartphone users? Bangalore's the tech capital, penetration is high — say seventy percent — that's about nine million smartphone users. And of those, who actually has a delivery app and has used it in the last month? Bangalore over-indexes hard here — young working crowd, long commutes, tiny apartment kitchens, money to spend. Say forty percent of smartphone users are active delivery users — that's about three point six million people. *Those* three point six million are our real unit. Not the thirteen million.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 2 · ORDER FREQUENCY." The 3.6M block splits into two segments.]

EV: Step two — how often does an active user actually order? Not everyone orders daily, so let's segment into two groups. Keeps it honest.

[VISUAL: Segment 1 glows: "FREQUENT · 30% · 1.1M people · 1 order/day." A young professional icon.]

EV: Group one — the frequent orderers. Young professionals, bachelors, people ordering lunch and dinner regularly. Say thirty percent of active users — about one point one million people — ordering on average once a day. Some order twice, some skip a day, it averages to roughly one.

[VISUAL: Segment 2 glows: "OCCASIONAL · 70% · 2.5M people · 0.3 orders/day." A family icon.]

EV: Group two — the occasional orderers. The other seventy percent, about two and a half million people, ordering maybe twice a week, which averages to around zero-point-three orders a day.

[VISUAL: Two multiplication lines animate, then sum: "1.1M + 0.75M ≈ 1.85M orders/day (all apps)."]

EV: Multiply it out. Frequent: one point one million times one equals one point one million orders. Occasional: two and a half million times zero-point-three equals about seven hundred fifty thousand. Add them — roughly one point eight five million food-delivery orders a day. But wait — that's across *all* apps combined. We're not done.

[VISUAL: Friendly lower-third: "👉 Subscribe to pmnorthstar." Pulse.]

EV: Quick nudge — if the funnel just made something click, hit subscribe to pmnorthstar so you catch the top-down method coming up, it pairs perfectly with this. Now — the step everyone forgets.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 3 · MARKET SHARE." Two competing app logos split a pie chart; one slice highlights at 45%.]

EV: Step three — market share. The question asked about Zomato *specifically*. And in India, food delivery is basically a two-horse race between Zomato and Swiggy, split pretty close to fifty-fifty, with the balance shifting city to city. In Bangalore — which is Swiggy's home turf — Swiggy's got a slight edge. So let's give Zomato forty-five percent.

[VISUAL: "1.85M × 0.45 ≈ 830,000" snaps onto screen, then rounds to "≈ 8–8.5 LAKH ZOMATO ORDERS/DAY."]

EV: One point eight five million times zero-point-four-five is about eight hundred thirty thousand. So — roughly eight to eight and a half lakh Zomato orders a day in Bangalore. A lakh, for everyone outside India, is a hundred thousand — so call it around eight hundred thousand orders a day. And there's our answer.

[VISUAL: Header: "SANITY CHECK · TWO ANGLES." Magnifying glass sweeps.]

EV: Sanity check — two ways, like always. First, per user: eight hundred thirty thousand Zomato orders from three point six million active delivery users means each active user places about half a Zomato order a day — one Zomato order every two days. For a city as delivery-addicted as Bangalore, that's reasonable, maybe even light. Second, from the top down: the company has publicly reported order volumes in the low millions per day across all of India. If Bangalore is one of its top three markets, a city chipping in several hundred thousand orders a day fits perfectly with a national total in the low millions. The scales line up beautifully.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TWO BIG LESSONS." Two icons slam in.]

EV: Okay — this example taught two things that'll save you in real interviews, so let me hammer them home.

[VISUAL: Lesson 1 — the shrinking funnel replays. "FIND THE RIGHT UNIT FIRST."]

EV: Lesson one — find the *right* unit before you multiply. If we'd taken all thirteen million people and multiplied by some order frequency, we'd have been off by a mile, because most of the city never touches a delivery app. That funnel — population, to smartphone users, to active delivery users — found the correct unit first. Whenever your "thing" is only done by a slice of the population, funnel down to that slice before you do anything else.

[VISUAL: Lesson 2 — the pie chart replays. "NAMED COMPANY = MARKET-SHARE STEP."]

EV: And lesson two — and this is a classic interviewer favorite — whenever the question names a *specific company* in a market with known competitors, you must ask yourself: am I estimating the whole market, or just this one player's slice? Forgetting the market-share step is one of the most common ways candidates land a number that's double or triple what it should be. Named company, competitive market — that's your signal to add a market-share step at the end. Tattoo that one on your brain.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE FOLLOW-UP TO BE READY FOR." A rain cloud, a weekend calendar, and a sports-match icon pop in.]

EV: And one more thing — be ready for the curveball. A sharp interviewer might go: "okay, how does this change on a rainy Saturday evening during a big sports match?" Don't recalculate from scratch — show them you understand the *drivers*. Rain spikes delivery because nobody wants to step out. Weekends run thirty to forty percent above a weekday. A huge match concentrates demand into just a few hours and can push orders way up. So you'd take your base of eight hundred thousand and bump it up by some multiple — maybe one-and-a-half to nearly two times — and explain exactly why. That shows you see the real-world levers moving behind the number, not just the arithmetic.
