EV: Hey hey, EV here from pmnorthstar, and welcome to the grand finale of our guesstimates series. We're going global for this one — total annual revenue of all coffee shops in the United States. I picked this deliberately for two reasons. One, it proves the method works anywhere on Earth, not just the examples closer to home. And two, the answer's in *dollars*, not a headcount — so the funnel grows a little tail at the end that converts people into money. That revenue twist is a whole skill of its own. Let's funnel.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP ZERO · SCOPE IT." Question bubbles pop.]

EV: Step zero — scope. I'd confirm: "coffee shops" means cafés where you buy prepared coffee — the big chains, the independents — and it *excludes* coffee you buy as beans at a grocery store or brew at home. All fifty US states. And we want annual revenue, in dollars. Locked. That home-versus-shop distinction is huge, by the way — get it wrong and your answer's off by a mile.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 1 · THE ANCHOR." A giant "330 MILLION" glows at the top of a funnel.]

EV: Step one — the anchor. US population, about three hundred thirty million people. Rock-solid number, top of the funnel. Now we filter down to coffee-shop customers.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 2 · FUNNEL TO CUSTOMERS." Two layers ready to fill.]

EV: Step two — the filters. For the *people* part of this, I only need two clean cuts. Watch.

[VISUAL: Filter 1 fills: "COFFEE DRINKERS · 60% → 200M." A coffee-bean icon.]

EV: Filter one — coffee drinkers. Coffee is basically the national beverage in the US; a big majority of adults drink it. Strip out the kids and the genuine non-drinkers. Say sixty percent of the total population are regular coffee drinkers — that's about two hundred million people.

[VISUAL: Filter 2 fills: "BUY FROM SHOPS · 50% → 100M." A café-storefront icon, with a faded home-brew mug beside it.]

EV: Filter two — of those drinkers, who actually buys from a *shop*, versus only ever brewing at home? Tons of people drink coffee but almost never buy a café cup. Say fifty percent of drinkers buy prepared coffee from a shop with some regularity. Two hundred million times fifty percent is one hundred million coffee-shop customers. And here's the key moment — if I stopped right here, I'd have a *headcount*, but the question wants *money*. So the funnel keeps going.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 3 · THE REVENUE TAIL — FREQUENCY × PRICE." Two new gears labeled "HOW OFTEN" and "HOW MUCH" engage with the funnel.]

EV: Step three — the revenue tail. This is the part that's unique to money questions. To turn people into dollars, I need two more things: how *often* they buy, and how *much* they spend each time. Two extra gears.

[VISUAL: "FREQUENCY" gear: a calendar showing ~2 visits/week → "~100 visits/year."]

EV: Frequency first. Among those hundred million shop customers, habits range from the daily-latte devotee to the once-a-week treat buyer. Let's take an average of about two coffee-shop visits per week per customer. That's roughly a hundred visits a year each.

[VISUAL: "PRICE" gear: a receipt showing "$6 avg per visit" (a drink, sometimes a pastry).]

EV: Price second. Average spend per visit — a drink, sometimes a pastry on the side — call it six dollars on average, blending the cheap drip coffees and the fancy specialty drinks.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 4 · MULTIPLY IT OUT." Three numbers chain-multiply with satisfying snaps.]

EV: Step four — multiply it all out, and watch the zeros. One hundred million customers, times a hundred visits a year, equals ten billion visits a year. Then ten billion visits times six dollars a visit... equals sixty billion dollars a year.

[VISUAL: "≈ $60 BILLION / YEAR" pulses huge on screen.]

EV: Sixty billion dollars a year in US coffee-shop revenue. From a population number and four sensible fractions. That's it.

[VISUAL: Header: "SANITY CHECK." Magnifying glass sweeps; a band appears "credible range: $45–95B ✓".]

EV: Sanity check. The US coffee-shop and café industry is widely cited somewhere in the range of forty-five to ninety-five billion dollars a year, depending on exactly what's counted — and one giant chain alone does over thirty billion globally, a big slice of that in the US. Our sixty billion sits squarely in the middle of credible estimates. For a number we built *entirely* from first principles, landing right inside the real band is exactly the win we want.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THE REVENUE FUNNEL HAS A TAIL." A count funnel stops short; a money funnel grows two extra stages labeled "× frequency × price."]

EV: Now the headline lesson of this whole video. A *count* funnel stops the moment you've isolated the right group of people. A *revenue* funnel keeps going — it multiplies that group by frequency, and by price. People, times how often they buy, times how much per purchase, equals total money. If you stop at "one hundred million customers," you've answered a different question than the one they asked. So whenever the ask is in dollars or rupees or euros — remember the funnel has a tail: frequency and price. Don't drop it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY TOP-DOWN WAS THE RIGHT CALL." Bottom-up and top-down icons weigh on a scale; top-down tips favorably.]

EV: And here's a meta-lesson worth the whole series. Could we have done this bottom-up instead — start with one café, estimate its revenue, multiply by the number of cafés? Sure, in theory. But notice *why* that's harder: most of us have terrible intuition for how many coffee shops exist in a country, or what one café grosses in a year. Our intuition is *way* stronger about our own behavior — how often we buy coffee, what we spend. Top-down let us anchor on the rock-solid population number and lean on personal-behavior fractions we actually understand. So the real skill underneath *every* guesstimate is this: choose the method that routes through your *strongest* intuitions. That's the meta-move.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE DUAL-METHOD POWER MOVE." Two funnels converge on "$60B" with a handshake.]

EV: And let me leave you with the move that genuinely impresses interviewers. After landing sixty billion top-down, you could offer a lightning bottom-up cross-check: "Let me verify from the supply side. The US has maybe seventy to eighty thousand coffee shops. If an average one grosses around seven hundred to eight hundred thousand dollars a year, that's fifty to sixty-five billion — which brackets my top-down answer nicely." Two completely independent methods, converging on the same number? That is the single strongest signal you can give that your reasoning is sound. You won't always have time for both — but offering it when you can is what turns a good answer into a *memorable* one.
