So the interesting move here is converting an *infrequent* behavior into a daily rate. Nail this and you can handle anything people do rarely — buying a car, a mattress, a phone, you name it. We'll use Delhi, a mega-city of about twenty million.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP ZERO · SCOPE IT." Question bubbles pop.]

EV: Step zero — scope, always. I'd confirm: Delhi city, or the whole sprawling metro region? Let's take Delhi city, about two crore people — twenty million. I'd confirm we mean *all* haircuts — fancy salons, barbershops, roadside barbers, home visits — and both men's and women's. And a normal day, averaged across the week. Locked.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 1 · THE KEY INSIGHT." A single person icon with a calendar; the calendar highlights one day per month, then an arrow divides by 365.]

EV: Step one — and here's the whole secret of this video. Our unit is one Delhi resident, and the behavior is "haircuts per day." But nobody gets a haircut daily, so if I try to estimate a tiny daily fraction directly, I'll end up with an ugly decimal and probably fumble it. Instead — I estimate haircuts per *month* or per *year*, where my intuition is actually strong, and only at the very end do I divide down to a daily rate. Estimate where your gut works, convert later. That's the move.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 2 · SEGMENT BY FREQUENCY." The 20M figure splits into two big blocks.]

EV: Step two — segment. And here the natural split is by how often people actually cut their hair, which differs a lot. Let's split by that.

[VISUAL: Block 1 glows: "MEN · ~50% · 10M people · ~12 cuts/year." A barber-chair icon.]

EV: Group one — men. Roughly fifty percent of the population, so ten million. Men typically get a haircut about once a month — call it twelve times a year. That's a number you can feel in your gut, right? Monthly. Easy.

[VISUAL: Block 2 glows: "WOMEN · ~45% · 9M people · ~4 cuts/year." A salon icon.]

EV: Group two — women. Roughly forty-five percent, about nine million. Women get haircuts far less frequently on average — many go once every two or three months, and plenty trim at home. Say four times a year on average. And the kids? We'll just fold young children's occasional trims into these two groups to keep it clean — everyone's represented.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 3 · ANNUAL HAIRCUTS." Two multiplication lines animate, then sum.]

EV: Step three — compute annual haircuts for each group, because yearly is where our intuition lives. Men: ten million times twelve equals one hundred twenty million haircuts a year. Women: nine million times four equals thirty-six million a year. Add them — about one hundred fifty-six million haircuts per year in Delhi. Big number — but we're not done.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 4 · CONVERT TO A DAILY RATE." The annual figure divides by a spinning "365." Result snaps: "≈ 4.3 LAKH / 430,000 HAIRCUTS PER DAY."]

EV: And step four — the conversion. Divide by three hundred sixty-five days. One hundred fifty-six million divided by three sixty-five is about four hundred thirty thousand. So — roughly four to four and a half lakh haircuts a day in Delhi. A lakh's a hundred thousand, so that's around four hundred thirty thousand haircuts, every single day. And notice — because we estimated yearly and converted at the very end, the numbers stayed clean the whole way. No ugly decimals, no fumbling.

[VISUAL: Header: "SANITY CHECK · TWO WAYS." Magnifying glass sweeps.]

EV: Two angles, like always. First, per person: one hundred fifty-six million haircuts a year across twenty million people is about seven to eight haircuts per person per year, averaged across men, women, and kids. That's somewhere between a monthly and a bi-monthly haircut — totally sensible for a mixed population. Good.

[VISUAL: Supply-side check animates — a barber icon, "20 haircuts/day per barber," divides into the total, revealing "≈ 21,500 barber chairs → 1 per ~930 people."]

EV: Now the fun one — the supply side. If Delhi does four hundred thirty thousand haircuts a day, how many barbers does that take? A barber spends maybe twenty to thirty minutes per cut and works, say, eight to ten productive hours — so one barber does roughly twenty haircuts a day. Four hundred thirty thousand divided by twenty is about twenty-one and a half thousand working barber chairs. For a city of twenty million, that's one barber for every nine hundred thirty people. And given there's a barbershop on practically every street, plus big salons with loads of chairs — that's believable, honestly maybe even a little low, which means our estimate is nicely conservative. Two independent methods, same ballpark. Chef's kiss.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THE CONVERSION MATTERS." A clock morphs between "DAY," "MONTH," "YEAR."]

EV: So let me really drive home the big lesson, because it generalizes way beyond haircuts. The single most important move here was recognizing that the behavior is monthly or yearly — not daily — and converting it at the end. A candidate who tries to estimate "haircuts per person per day" directly ends up with this tiny awkward decimal and usually trips over the math. But by estimating at the *natural* frequency — once a month for men — and only *then* dividing by three sixty-five, the numbers stayed clean and intuitive the entire way.

[VISUAL: Examples flash: a car "1/5 years," a mattress "1/8 years," a flight "2/year," a phone "1/3 years."]

EV: And this applies to any rare-behavior guesstimate. How many cars get bought, how many mattresses sold, how many flights someone takes, how often people replace their phone. Estimate at the frequency your intuition actually works in — yearly for a car, monthly for a haircut — then convert to whatever rate the question wants. Never, ever force your gut into an unnatural time unit. That's the takeaway.

[VISUAL: Header: "BONUS · THE SEGMENTATION LESSON." Two bars of very different heights glow.]

EV: One more quick gem — the segmentation lesson. We split by gender here, and that was exactly right, because men and women genuinely have very different haircut frequencies — like a three-times difference. If I'd used one blended "once every six weeks" average, I'd have hidden that real gap and made my estimate way harder to defend. So here's your rule for a good segment: split when the behavior *inside* each group is roughly uniform, but genuinely *differs across* groups. That's the test. If splitting doesn't reveal a real difference, don't bother — you're just adding ways to be wrong.

[VISUAL: Header: "BONUS · MAKE IT MONEY." A ₹/$ symbol floats up over two segments.]

EV: And just like with tea — what if they ask for the market size in money? Easy, because you've already got the structure. Layer price on each segment: a men's cut at a barber might be a hundred rupees or so, a woman's salon cut runs way higher — five hundred to fifteen hundred. Multiply each group's annual haircuts by its average price, add them, and you've got a market size. Same skeleton you already built. The structure keeps paying off every time they push further.
