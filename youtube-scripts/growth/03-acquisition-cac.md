And there are *many* ways to do it, but here's the thing beginners miss: it's not about finding *a* channel, it's about finding the *right* channel at the *right cost*. Because a channel that costs more to acquire a user than that user is ever worth will quietly kill your business. So today — the channels, and the number that governs all of them, CAC. Let's go.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 3 FAMILIES OF CHANNELS." Three groups light up.]

EV: First, the landscape. Acquisition channels fall into three big families. Family one — *organic*: free-ish channels like SEO, content, and word of mouth. Slow to build, but cheap and durable once they work. Family two — *paid*: ads on search, social, wherever — you pay money, you get users, instantly and at scale, but it stops the moment you stop paying. And family three — *viral*: users bringing other users through sharing and referrals — the holy grail, because it's a loop that compounds for free. Organic, paid, viral. Every acquisition strategy is some mix of these three, and the art is finding *your* mix.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE KEY NUMBER — CAC." A price tag assembling: "Customer Acquisition Cost."]

EV: Now the number that rules them all — CAC, Customer Acquisition Cost. It's dead simple: how much does it cost you, on average, to acquire *one* customer through a channel? If you spend ten thousand dollars on ads and get a thousand customers, your CAC is ten dollars. Easy. But this number is *everything*, because it tells you whether a channel is a money-maker or a money-pit. A channel with a CAC higher than your customers are worth isn't "growth" — it's *paying* to lose money faster. CAC is the reality check on every acquisition dream.

[VISUAL: Header: "CAC vs LTV — THE GOLDEN RATIO." A scale: CAC on one side, LTV on the other.]

EV: And CAC only means something next to its partner — LTV, Lifetime Value: how much *total* money a customer brings you over their entire life with the product. Here's the golden rule of all acquisition: *LTV must be greater than CAC* — ideally by a healthy multiple. If a customer is worth a hundred dollars to you over time, and costs thirty dollars to acquire, beautiful — you make seventy. But if they cost a hundred and twenty to acquire and are only worth a hundred, every customer *loses* you twenty dollars, and growing just digs the hole faster. The common healthy benchmark people cite is an LTV-to-CAC ratio of around three-to-one. This single comparison — what a customer costs versus what they're worth — is the heartbeat of a sustainable business. Memorize it.

[VISUAL: Header: "PAYBACK PERIOD." A clock showing how long until a customer 'pays back' their CAC.]

EV: There's a close cousin worth knowing — *payback period*: how *long* it takes for a customer to earn back what you spent to acquire them. Because even if LTV beats CAC eventually, if it takes *three years* to recoup your cost, you've got a cash-flow problem — you're fronting a fortune and waiting ages to get it back. A short payback period — say, under a year — means you recover your acquisition spend fast and can reinvest it to acquire *more* users: that's the paid loop from last video, spinning fast. Long payback starves the loop. So it's not just *whether* you make money per customer, but *how fast* — and fast payback is what lets growth compound.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO CHOOSE CHANNELS." A funnel testing many channels, a few winning.]

EV: So how do you choose your channels? Three principles. One — *go where your specific customers already are*. If you're targeting developers, you win in developer communities, not on daytime TV. Match the channel to the customer from our GTM video. Two — *test small, then double down*. You rarely know your best channel upfront, so run small experiments across a few, measure the CAC of each, and pour money into the ones that work while killing the ones that don't. Growth teams are constantly running these channel tests. And three — *beware channel saturation*. A channel that's cheap today gets *more expensive* as you and everyone else pile in — CAC rises as you scale. So a channel that works at small scale might not work at large scale, which is why you keep diversifying. Match, test, and watch for saturation.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE DREAM — ORGANIC & VIRAL." Paid (treadmill) fading vs organic/viral (compounding) glowing.]

EV: And here's the strategic North Star of acquisition — you want to graduate *away* from pure paid toward organic and viral over time. Why? Paid is a *treadmill*: the second you stop spending, the users stop coming. Organic and viral *compound*: content you wrote years ago still pulls in users for free today, and a viral loop brings users who bring more users at near-zero cost. Paid is great for jump-starting and for scale you can control, but a business that *only* buys users is fragile and expensive forever. The healthiest growth engines use paid to prime the pump, while building organic and viral channels that keep working for free. Rent growth to start; own it over time.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SHOWS UP IN INTERVIEWS." A growth question answered with channel + CAC reasoning.]

EV: In interviews, this shows up when you're asked how to grow or launch something. The strong answer names *specific* channels matched to the customer, *and* brings up the economics: "I'd test a couple of channels where our target users are, measure CAC on each, and double down on the winners — always making sure LTV comfortably beats CAC, and watching the payback period so we can reinvest fast." Mentioning CAC, LTV, and payback instantly signals you understand that growth isn't just *getting* users — it's getting them *profitably*. That economic literacy is a huge differentiator.

[VISUAL: Recap: three channel families, the CAC-vs-LTV scale, and payback clock.]

EV: So — acquisition and CAC. Three channel families: organic, paid, viral. The number that governs everything is CAC, and it only means something next to LTV — LTV must beat CAC, ideally around three-to-one — with a short payback period so growth can compound. Choose channels by going where customers are, testing small then doubling down, and watching for saturation. And over time, graduate from rented paid growth toward owned organic and viral. Get users *profitably*, or you're not growing — you're just spending.

[VISUAL: Header: "A QUICK CAC/LTV GUT-CHECK — LIVE." Numbers assemble into a healthy vs unhealthy verdict.]

EV: Let me run a quick CAC/LTV gut-check live, because seeing the math makes it stick forever. Say we're running ads for our app. We spend fifty thousand dollars and get two thousand new customers — so CAC is twenty-five dollars per customer. Now, what's a customer *worth*? Say they pay us ten dollars a month and stay, on average, ten months — that's an LTV of a hundred dollars. So: LTV of a hundred, CAC of twenty-five. Ratio of four-to-one — *excellent*, well above the healthy three-to-one benchmark. This channel is a money-maker; pour more in. But now watch what happens if retention is *worse* — say customers only stay *two* months instead of ten. Now LTV is just twenty dollars, against a CAC of twenty-five. We're *losing* five dollars on every customer — and scaling this channel would just make us lose money *faster*. Notice the punchline: the *same* ad spend flipped from brilliant to disastrous purely because of *retention*. That's why everything connects — your acquisition math lives or dies on retention, which is exactly why it's our most important stage, two videos from now. CAC never stands alone.
