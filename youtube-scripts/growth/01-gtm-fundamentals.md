EV: Let's start at the beginning — what does a real go-to-market plan, a GTM, actually need? Because most people think "GTM" just means "do some marketing." It's way more structured than that.

[VISUAL: Header: "GTM = HOW YOU REACH & WIN CUSTOMERS." A product connecting to its market through a deliberate plan.]

EV: So first, the definition. A go-to-market strategy is your *plan for how you'll reach your target customers and win them over* — how a product gets from "it exists" to "the right people know about it, try it, and love it." It's the bridge between building and growing. And a great GTM answers a specific set of questions, every time. Let me give you the five that matter most — think of these as your GTM checklist for any launch.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 5 GTM QUESTIONS." Five cards flip in.]

EV: Question one — *Who* is the target customer? And just like in product design, be *specific*. Not "everyone" — the precise segment you're launching to first. A focused launch to one clear group beats a vague launch to the masses every time. Question two — *What* is the value proposition? In one sharp sentence, why should this customer care? What problem does it solve, and why is it better than what they do today? If you can't say it crisply, neither can your customers. Question three — *Where* are these customers, and how do you reach them? Which channels — social, search, communities, sales, word of mouth? You go where your specific customers already are. Question four — *What's the message*? How do you talk about the product so it resonates with *that* customer in *that* channel? And question five — *How do you measure success*? What's the one metric that tells you the launch worked? Who, what, where, message, metric. Answer those five and you have a real GTM.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BIGGEST MISTAKE — PRODUCT-FIRST, NOT CUSTOMER-FIRST." A builder obsessed with features, ignoring who it's for.]

EV: Now the single biggest GTM mistake, so you avoid it from day one — being *product-first* instead of *customer-first*. Builders fall so in love with their features that they launch by shouting about the *product* — "look at all these amazing features." — instead of about the *customer's problem*. But customers don't care about your features; they care about *their* problem getting solved. So a great GTM flips it: lead with the customer and their pain, and position the product as the *solution* to that pain. "Tired of X? Here's how we fix it" beats "Introducing our 47 new features" every single time. Customer-first, always.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE GOLDEN RULE — PMF BEFORE GTM." A leaky bucket being filled vs a solid bucket being filled.]

EV: And here's the golden rule that governs this entire series — *product-market fit comes before go-to-market*. Here's why it matters so much. If your product doesn't yet retain users — if it's a *leaky bucket* where people try it and leave — then pouring marketing money into the top is *wasted*, because they drain right out the bottom. You'd be paying to acquire users who immediately churn. So before you scale a GTM, you need signs that users who try the product actually *stick*. Get the bucket *solid* first, *then* pour water in. A huge number of startups die by scaling growth before they had fit — they spent their way to a bigger leak. Fix retention first; scale second.

[VISUAL: Header: "DISTRIBUTION IS A FEATURE." A product with a built-in way to spread, vs one that just sits there.]

EV: One more mindset that separates sophisticated thinkers — treat *distribution as part of the product*, not an afterthought. The best products have *how they spread* built right into them. A tool that's more useful when you invite coworkers has distribution baked in. A product people naturally show off creates its own marketing. So don't finish building and *then* ask "how do we market this?" — ask, *while* building, "how will this naturally reach new people?" When you design distribution into the product itself, growth stops being something you have to constantly pay for and starts being something the product *does on its own*. That's the dream, and it starts at the design stage.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHAT'S BEING TESTED." Four quick signals: customer focus, channel sense, funnel thinking, metric discipline.]

EV: And quickly — what are interviewers testing with GTM and growth questions? Four things. One — are you *customer-first*, leading with the user's problem, not features? Two — do you understand *channels* — where customers are and how to reach them efficiently? Three — do you think in *funnels and loops* — the whole journey from stranger to loyal user, which is our next video? And four — *metric discipline* — can you say how you'd measure growth honestly, without vanity numbers? Keep those four in mind and your growth answers will land.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE HARD TRUTH — BETTER PRODUCTS LOSE." Two products: a superior one with no distribution fading, an inferior one with great distribution winning.]

EV: Let me drive home the uncomfortable truth at the heart of this whole series, because it reframes everything — *the better product does not automatically win*. History is littered with examples of a technically *superior* product getting absolutely crushed by an inferior one that simply had better distribution and growth. The team with the better product assumed quality would speak for itself, sat back, and watched a worse competitor with a louder megaphone and a smarter growth engine take the whole market. It feels *unfair* — and it's completely real. Here's the lesson: your product quality gets you a *seat at the table*, but distribution is what *wins the game*. So if you're a builder who takes pride in craft — good, keep that — but never, ever assume great work markets itself. The most successful product people are the ones who obsess over distribution *as much as* the product itself. Growth isn't the thing you do *after* building something great; it's half of what *makes* something great actually succeed. Internalize that, and you're already ahead of most builders out there.
