[VISUAL: Header: "THE MAIN MONETIZATION MODELS." Model cards flip in.]

EV: First, the menu of models — the main ways products make money. Model one — *subscription*: users pay a recurring fee, monthly or yearly. Beloved because it creates *predictable, recurring* revenue and compounds beautifully with retention. Model two — *freemium*: a free tier for everyone, with paid upgrades for premium features or more usage. Great for acquisition — free pulls people in — but only works if enough free users *convert* to paid. Model three — *usage-based*: you pay for what you consume, like per-unit or metered pricing. Fair and scalable — costs grow with value received. Model four — *transactional or marketplace*: you take a cut, a commission, of transactions that happen on your platform. And model five — *ads*: the product is free, and you monetize attention by selling ads, meaning your *users* are the product being sold to advertisers. Subscription, freemium, usage-based, transactional, ads. Most businesses pick one primary model, sometimes blending a couple.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO CHOOSE A MODEL." The product's value pattern matched to a model.]

EV: So how do you *choose* a model? The key is to *match the model to how your product delivers value*. If value is ongoing and continuous — like a tool people use daily — *subscription* fits, because they get value continuously and pay continuously. If value scales with usage — like cloud storage or API calls — *usage-based* fits, because heavy users pay more, which feels fair. If you facilitate transactions between others — *marketplace commission* fits. If your product is mass-market and attention-rich but hard to charge for directly — *ads* might fit. And *freemium* layers on top when free usage is a great acquisition channel and there's a clear reason to upgrade. The principle: the way you charge should *mirror* the way you deliver value. When those align, pricing feels fair and natural; when they clash, users resent it.

[VISUAL: Header: "FREEMIUM — THE TRICKY ONE." A free tier funneling into paid, with a 'conversion' gate.]

EV: Let me zoom in on freemium, because it's the most popular and the most *misunderstood* model. Freemium's power is acquisition — "free" is the best word in marketing, so you get tons of users in the door. But the whole model lives or dies on one thing: *conversion* — do enough free users become paying ones? And the secret to that is *where you draw the line* between free and paid. Draw it wrong and you lose both ways. Give away too much for free, and nobody ever needs to pay — you've built an expensive charity. Give away too little, and users never experience enough value to *want* to pay — they just leave. The art is: free tier delivers *real* value, so people love it and stick — but the *paid* tier unlocks something they'll genuinely, increasingly *need* as they get more invested. Free to fall in love; paid for what they can't live without.

[VISUAL: Header: "VALUE-BASED PRICING." A price anchored to the value delivered, not the cost to build.]

EV: Now, the actual *price* — the number. And here's the most important pricing principle there is: price based on *value*, not on *cost*. The beginner mistake is cost-plus pricing — "it costs us two dollars to serve a customer, so we'll charge three." That leaves massive money on the table, because it ignores what the product is actually *worth* to the customer. Value-based pricing asks instead: "how much value does this *create* for the customer?" If your software saves a business a thousand dollars a month, you can charge hundreds and it's still a steal *for them* — regardless of what it costs you to run. So anchor your price to the value delivered, not your costs. Capture a fair slice of the value you create, and both sides win.

[VISUAL: Header: "PRICE IS A SIGNAL + TIERS CAPTURE MORE." A price tag doubling as a quality signal; good/better/best tiers.]

EV: Two more sharp pricing ideas. First — price is a *signal*. Price too *low* and people may assume your product is low-quality or not serious — counterintuitively, raising a price can sometimes *increase* sales by signaling value and attracting more serious customers. Price isn't just a cost; it's a message about what you're worth. Second — use *tiers* to capture more value. Different customers will pay different amounts, so a good-better-best tier structure lets the budget customer pay a little and the power customer pay a lot — you capture value across the *whole* range instead of forcing one price on everyone. That's why you see those three-column pricing pages everywhere. And a classic trick: a cleverly-placed middle or "decoy" tier nudges people toward the option you most want them to pick. Tiers turn one price into a value-capturing machine.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SHOWS UP IN INTERVIEWS." A monetization question answered with model + value-pricing.]

EV: In interviews, pricing questions — "how would you monetize this?" — are common, and most candidates give shallow answers. You'll stand out by reasoning structurally: "First, I'd match the model to how we deliver value — this is a daily-use tool, so subscription fits. I'd use freemium to drive acquisition, drawing the free-paid line so the free tier delights but power users need to upgrade. And I'd price based on the *value* we create for customers, not our costs, using tiers to capture across budget and power users." That answer shows you see pricing as a strategic lever, not a number you guess. Huge differentiator.

[VISUAL: Recap: the five models, value-based pricing, and good/better/best tiers.]

EV: So — pricing and monetization. Five main models: subscription, freemium, usage-based, transactional, and ads — choose by *matching the model to how you deliver value*. Freemium lives on conversion, which lives on where you draw the free-paid line. Price based on *value*, not cost. Remember price is a *signal*, and use *tiers* to capture value across all your customers. Treat pricing as the high-leverage strategic lever it is — because it multiplies every dollar you make.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY PRICING IS THE HIGHEST LEVER." Three levers — acquisition, retention, price — with price lifting the most.]

EV: Let me prove that pricing is the most underrated lever in the whole business, with a bit of math that stuns people. There's a famous finding that, across companies, a one-percent improvement in *pricing* drives a bigger profit increase than a one-percent improvement in acquiring more customers *or* cutting costs — often several times bigger. Think about why: when you raise price intelligently, that extra margin drops almost *straight* to the bottom line — there's no extra cost to serve, no extra acquisition spend. A dollar of smart price increase is nearly a dollar of pure profit. And yet — here's the tragedy — most teams agonize endlessly over squeezing a few more signups from an ad channel, while setting their price *once*, with a gut guess, and never touching it again. They're polishing the small lever and ignoring the giant one. So here's your takeaway: treat pricing as a *first-class experiment*, just like you'd test onboarding or ad copy. Test prices, test tiers, test where the free-paid line sits. Because of all the levers in growth, pricing is the one most likely to be *way* underpriced relative to the value you deliver — and fixing that is often the fastest profit you'll ever find.
