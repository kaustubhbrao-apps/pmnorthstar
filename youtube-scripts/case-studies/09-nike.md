[VISUAL: Cold open. A plain shoe on a shelf, a price tag. Then a swoosh appears, music swells, an athlete pushes through exhaustion, a crowd roars — and the same shoe is suddenly something people line up overnight to buy. Caption: "same shoe. different feeling."]

EV: Here's a strange fact. A shoe is, functionally, a shoe. Rubber, fabric, laces. And yet people will camp overnight, pay a premium, and feel genuine pride wearing one particular swoosh. Nobody lines up for rubber and fabric. They line up for what it *means.* Nike didn't get to be a giant by making the best rubber. It got there by selling a feeling — and then by owning the relationship with you directly. Two lessons in one today. Let's go.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "NIKE — sell the feeling, own the relationship."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Nike — you know the swoosh. The principle I want you to take is two-part, and both parts are gold for anyone building products. One: **people don't buy what a product does, they buy who it makes them feel they are.** Two: **owning a direct relationship with your customer is worth more than any single sale.** Let me show you how Nike mastered both.

[VISUAL: Header: "PART 1 — THE FEELING." Competitors' ads list specs: "cushioning, grip, support." Nike's ad shows no specs — just a person refusing to quit, and three words: "Just Do It."]

EV: Part one — the feeling. Look at how most companies sell a performance product. They list features — the cushioning, the grip, the tech in the sole. Specs, specs, specs. Now look at classic Nike. They barely talk about the shoe. They show a person struggling, pushing, overcoming — an ordinary human finding their inner athlete. And three words: *Just Do It.* They're not selling you a shoe. They're selling you *determination.* The identity of someone who shows up and pushes through. And here's why that's so powerful — features can be copied and compared, and someone can always claim better cushioning. But a feeling, an identity? That's yours. That's a moat made of emotion, and competitors can't spec-sheet their way past it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY FEELINGS WIN." A chart: features compete on a crowded, comparable axis; identity sits alone, incomparable.]

EV: And think about what that does to pricing. The moment you're competing on features, you're in a race — someone's always cheaper or claims better numbers, and the customer coldly compares. But when people buy an identity, price comparison melts away, because how do you put a price on feeling like the athlete you want to be? Nike escaped the features race by playing a different game entirely. They made the brand itself the product, and the shoe the thing you buy to join it. That's why a swoosh commands a premium a generic shoe never could.

[VISUAL: Header: "PART 2 — CUT OUT THE MIDDLE." A chain: Nike → big retail store → customer. Then the store fades, and the arrow goes straight: Nike → customer, through its own app and stores.]

EV: Part two — owning the relationship. For a long time, Nike mostly sold through other people's stores — big retailers. And when you sell through a middleman, something crucial happens: *they* own the customer relationship, not you. You don't know who bought, what they like, or how to reach them again. So Nike made a big strategic push to sell *directly* — through their own app, their own stores, their own membership. Cutting out the middle. Direct-to-consumer. On the surface it's about keeping more margin. But the deeper prize is something else entirely.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE REAL PRIZE — DATA + RELATIONSHIP." A member profile fills with preferences, sizes, workout habits; Nike sends a perfectly tailored offer.]

EV: The real prize is the direct relationship and the data that comes with it. When you buy through Nike's own app as a member, Nike *knows* you — your size, your sport, what you browse, when you buy. Now they can talk to you directly, recommend the right thing, build a membership that makes you loyal, and never pay a retailer to stand between you again. A customer you own is one you can serve better *and* bring back again and again — and the data makes every future product and pitch sharper. One sale through a retailer is a transaction. A member in your own app is a relationship that compounds for years. That's the shift from selling products to owning customers.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." The straight Nike→customer arrow is shown quietly starving the retail shelves; a new CEO redraws it to include BOTH the app AND wholesale partners, in balance.]

EV: Now — and this is the part that makes the lesson honest — Nike pushed that "own the customer" idea *too far.* In chasing direct sales, they pulled back from a lot of the retail stores that used to carry them. And it backfired. Those shelves didn't stay empty — rivals like On and Hoka walked right into the space Nike vacated, and Nike lost some of the everywhere-ness that made it Nike in the first place. Sales stumbled. So in late 2024 a new CEO, Elliott Hill, a Nike veteran, came in with a "win now" plan — and a big piece of it is *rebuilding* those wholesale partnerships, getting Nike back onto shelves, while keeping the direct app and membership too. Direct selling isn't dead for them. They just learned it works best *alongside* wide distribution, not instead of it. Owning the relationship is powerful. Being everywhere is also powerful. The win is both.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Sell identity, not specs. Own the relationship — without abandoning reach."]

EV: So the principles, now with the full picture. One — compete on identity and emotion, not just features, because feelings can't be copied or out-spec'd, and they free you from the price-comparison race. That part's rock solid; it's still Nike's superpower. Two — yes, own a direct relationship with your customer for the data and the loyalty, but — here's the hard-won nuance — don't torch your distribution to do it. Nike proved both sides of that: the direct push was smart; overdoing it and abandoning the shelves was not. The mature answer isn't "DTC good, wholesale bad." It's balance — be close to your customer *and* be everywhere they shop.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you differentiate a product in a crowded, commoditized market?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a sophisticated answer. If they ask how you'd stand out in a crowded, commoditized market — everyone's shoe is basically fine — don't reach for "add more features." Say: "I'd compete on identity, not specs — what does using this product say about who you are? That's a moat features can't touch. And I'd build a direct relationship with customers to capture data and loyalty — but I'd be careful not to abandon my wider distribution to do it, because reach is its own moat. The goal is to be close to the customer *and* everywhere they shop." And then land it: "Nike actually over-rotated to direct and had to rebuild wholesale — so the real lesson is balance." Citing how a famous strategy *went wrong* shows you don't just repeat playbooks, you know how they break. That's the move that makes an interviewer trust you.

[VISUAL: Recap: "Just Do It" identity on one side, the direct Nike-to-customer arrow on the other, meeting in a loyal member.]

EV: So — Nike. They took a commodity — a shoe — and made it a symbol of who you want to be, which lets them charge a premium nobody questions. That emotional moat is still unmatched. They also showed us the *edges* of the "go direct" playbook — push it, yes, but not so hard you disappear from the shelves, which is exactly the rebalancing they're doing right now. Sell the feeling. Own the relationship. Stay everywhere. Get all three right and you're not in the product business anymore — you're in the belonging business.
