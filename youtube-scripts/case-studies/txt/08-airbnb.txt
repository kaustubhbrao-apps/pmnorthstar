[VISUAL: Cold open. A near-flat revenue line, barely alive. Then two founders grab a camera, go door to door, and photograph apartments. The line twitches, then climbs. Caption: "the fix wasn't code. it was a camera."]

EV: A startup is nearly dead. Revenue flat for months. The founders are smart engineers, so what do they do? They... put down the laptop, borrow a camera, and go knock on doors to take photos of people's apartments themselves. One by one. By hand. It sounds like the opposite of building a tech company. It's one of the most famous turning points in startup history. Airbnb. Today's lesson — do things that don't scale — is a principle that feels wrong and is deeply right. Let's go.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "AIRBNB — do things that don't scale."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Airbnb — the platform where regular people rent out their homes to travelers. Today it's everywhere. Early on, it was barely surviving. And the way they dug out teaches a principle every builder needs, because our instinct is always to automate, to build systems, to scale. Here's the truth that flips it: **in the early days, the right move is often to do the slow, manual, un-scalable thing — because that's how you learn what actually makes the product work.** Watch.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MYSTERY." A map of New York with a cluster of listings, none of them booking. A founder frowns: "why isn't this working?"]

EV: So, the problem. They had listings in New York, but nobody was booking. And they couldn't figure out why from behind a screen. The data just said "not working." So instead of guessing, tweaking code, running another A/B test, they did something radical for engineers — they went to New York, in person, to look. And the moment they actually saw the listings, the answer was obvious. The photos were terrible. Dark, blurry phone pictures of unmade beds. Nobody wants to book a stay they can't even see properly. The barrier wasn't the technology. It was that the homes looked unappealing. You could stare at a dashboard forever and miss that.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE UN-SCALABLE FIX." The founders personally photograph apartment after apartment with a nice camera; the listings transform into warm, inviting spaces.]

EV: And here's the move everyone remembers. They didn't build a feature. They didn't write a help article telling hosts to take better photos. They rented a nice camera and went apartment to apartment, photographing the listings *themselves.* By hand. Does that scale? Absolutely not — you can't personally photograph every home on earth. But that was never the point. Bookings in New York jumped almost immediately. Suddenly the listings looked gorgeous, travelers could picture the stay, and they booked. The manual, "wasteful" act of grabbing a camera unlocked the single biggest lever in the business.

EV: If this is useful, subscribing to pmnorthstar helps the series keep going. Now — why the un-scalable move was actually the *smart* one.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY IT WAS GENIUS." A lightbulb: "great photos drive bookings." An arrow to a future scalable program: photographer network + host guidelines.]

EV: Here's why this wasn't a desperate hack — it was strategy. By doing it manually, they *learned the lesson* that professional-quality photos are a massive driver of bookings. You can't learn that from a distance. Once they knew it, *then* they could scale it the right way — building a program of professional photographers, giving hosts clear guidance, eventually tools to do it themselves. The sequence matters enormously. Do it by hand to discover what works. Then automate the thing you proved. If they'd tried to build a scalable photo *system* from day one, they wouldn't even have known photos were the problem. The manual phase was the research.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE DEEPER PATTERN — GET CLOSE." A founder sitting on a host's couch, talking, taking notes; insights pour out that no dashboard showed.]

EV: And there's a bigger pattern underneath the camera story. Doing the un-scalable thing forces you *close* to your users. Sitting in those apartments, talking to hosts, seeing the city — the founders absorbed a hundred little truths about their customers that no analytics tool would ever surface. What hosts were afraid of. What travelers looked for. The texture of the real problem. Early on, that closeness is worth more than any system. Scale creates distance from your users; in the beginning, distance is the enemy. Get your hands dirty, get in the room, and let reality teach you.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." The app redesigns itself into tabs — Homes, Experiences, Services; icons bloom for chefs, trainers, photographers, massages, local tours.]

EV: And look where that same instinct took them recently. In 2025 Airbnb relaunched with a big expansion beyond just places to stay — Experiences, things to do led by local hosts, and Services, where you can book a chef, a trainer, a photographer, a massage, right to your stay. They rebuilt the whole app around it. And here's the beautiful callback: the very first version of Experiences, years ago, actually struggled — so instead of forcing it, they went back, got close, learned what truly worked, and relaunched it properly. Same philosophy as the camera. They're trying to turn "a place to sleep" into "your whole trip," and they're doing it the Airbnb way — test it close and human first, then scale what proves out. The company that started with two founders and a borrowed camera is still running the very same playbook, just bigger.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Manual first — to learn. Scale second — what you proved."]

EV: So the principle, portable: early on, resist the urge to immediately automate and scale everything. Do the slow, manual, personal thing — because it gets you close enough to see what's really going on, and it lets you find the one lever that matters before you spend months building the wrong system. Then, once you've *proven* what works by hand, scale that. Manual to learn. Automated to grow. In that order. Flipping the order is how teams build beautiful systems for problems they never actually understood.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Your product has low engagement and the data isn't telling you why."]

EV: And in a case, this is a genuinely impressive instinct. When they hand you a struggling product and the metrics are murky, the junior move is to propose more experiments and features from the armchair. The strong move: "Before building anything, I'd get close to users directly — talk to them, watch them use it, even do the core job manually for a handful of them, to find the real barrier. Airbnb discovered bad photos were killing bookings only by showing up in person. I'd do the un-scalable thing first to *learn*, then build the scalable solution for whatever I found." That's maturity. That's a PM who knows data tells you *what*, but getting close tells you *why.*

[VISUAL: Recap: terrible photos → founders with a camera → beautiful listings → bookings climb → a scaled photo program.]

EV: So — Airbnb. A dying startup, saved not by a clever algorithm but by two founders humble enough to grab a camera and do the small, manual, un-scalable thing. It taught them the lever, got them close to their users, and gave them something real to scale. Sometimes the fastest way forward is the slow way. Do things that don't scale — then scale what you learned.
