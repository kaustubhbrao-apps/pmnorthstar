EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. You know Netflix as streaming — press play, watch anything. But it didn't start there. It started mailing you DVDs in little red envelopes. And the pivot from that to streaming is a masterclass in one of the hardest moves in all of strategy. Here's the principle: **if your product is going to be disrupted eventually, be the one who disrupts it.** Eat your own lunch before a competitor does. Most companies can't. Netflix did it twice. Watch.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE COMFORTABLE TRAP." A cozy chair labeled "DVD profits"; outside the window, a storm cloud labeled "the internet is getting faster."]

EV: Okay, so — the DVD business was genuinely good. Reliable revenue, loyal subscribers, a tidy operation. And this is exactly the trap. When something's working, every instinct says protect it, milk it, don't rock the boat. But Netflix's leadership saw the storm coming — internet speeds were climbing, and eventually you wouldn't need a disc in the mail, you'd just stream. The comfortable question was "how do we protect DVDs?" The dangerous, correct question was "what replaces DVDs — and can we be the ones who build it?" They chose the second question while the first one still paid the bills.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LEAP." The company pours resources into streaming while DVD revenue is still strong; a graph shows DVD dipping as streaming climbs past it.]

EV: So they built streaming — and here's the gutsy part — they pushed it hard *while the DVD business was still healthy.* They let the new thing eat the old thing. That takes real nerve, because in the short term you're hurting your own numbers, and plenty of people will tell you you're crazy. But the logic is ironclad: that disruption is coming either way. If you don't build the future, someone else builds it and takes your customers. Better the pain comes from your own hand, on your timeline, with your subscribers, than from a competitor on theirs. Self-disruption is just controlled demolition — you choose when the building comes down.

[VISUAL: Header: "DISRUPT AGAIN — ORIGINALS." Netflix stops only licensing others' shows and starts making its own; a trophy shelf fills up.]

EV: And they didn't stop at one pivot. Once they were a streaming service, they were renting content from the studios — the same studios who could yank it away or launch their own services. So Netflix disrupted *itself* again: it started making its own originals. Massive bet, huge upfront cost, no guarantee. But it turned them from a middleman who could be cut out into an owner of things people could only get from them. Twice now, they refused to sit still in a comfortable position. DVDs to streaming. Licensing to originals. Same courage, applied again.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE QUIET WEAPON — RECOMMENDATIONS." A user stares at infinite choice; an algorithm surfaces the perfect next show; "play" within seconds.]

EV: Now the quiet product weapon that made it all stick — recommendations. When you've got a near-infinite library, the hardest problem isn't content, it's *choice.* Scroll forever, find nothing, give up — that's how streaming dies. So Netflix obsessed over the algorithm that learns what you like and surfaces the thing you'll actually watch next. Every row, every thumbnail, personalized. Why does this matter so much? Because the faster they get you from "I'm bored" to "I'm watching," the more you value the subscription, and the less likely you are to cancel. Personalization wasn't a nice-to-have. It was the retention engine under the whole business.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." A shared password splits into separate paying accounts; a cheaper "with ads" plan appears; a "LIVE" badge lights up over a sports-and-events icon.]

EV: And true to form — they just did it *again.* For years Netflix quietly tolerated password sharing. Then, recently, they cracked down: freeloaders either leave or pay. Risky move, everyone predicted a revolt. Instead, paired with a new cheaper ad-supported plan, it converted huge numbers of those sharers into paying customers and pushed subscribers to record highs. Then they started moving into live events and advertising — brand-new models for them. Notice the pattern never stops. The moment an old way of making money looks tapped out, they willingly disrupt it for the next one. DVDs, streaming, originals, ad tiers, live. Same courage, over and over. That's not luck. That's a muscle.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Disrupt yourself on your timeline — or get disrupted on theirs."]

EV: So the principle, clearly: the scariest competitor to a successful product is usually the future version of that same product — and the winning move is to build that future yourself, even when it hurts today's numbers. The company that clings to its cash cow because it's comfortable is the company that gets blindsided. Netflix made discomfort a habit. And notice — self-disruption isn't recklessness. They jumped *toward* where the world was clearly going, with a product engine, recommendations, that made the new model stickier than the old one. Jump early, but jump toward something real.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Your core product is profitable but a new technology threatens it. What do you do?"]

EV: And this is a classic interview setup. "Your profitable product is threatened by a new technology — what do you do?" The weak answer defends the old product. The strong answer: "I'd assume the disruption is inevitable and ask whether we can be the ones to lead it, even if it cannibalizes current revenue. I'd rather we eat our own business on our timeline than let a competitor do it on theirs. And I'd make sure the new product has a retention engine — a reason customers stay — not just feature parity." That's the Netflix mindset. That's a candidate who isn't afraid of their own success.

[VISUAL: Recap: DVD → streaming → originals, with the recommendation engine humming underneath.]

EV: So — Netflix. Twice they tore down something that was working to build what was coming. DVDs to streaming. Licensing to originals. And underneath, a recommendation engine that turned an overwhelming library into "just one more episode." The lesson isn't "be reckless." It's "be brave enough to disrupt yourself before the world does it for you."
