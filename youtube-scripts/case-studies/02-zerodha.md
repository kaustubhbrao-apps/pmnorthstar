[VISUAL: Cold open. A split screen. Left: competitors' billboards, TV spots, celebrity ads, cash flying out. Right: a single clean app icon, silent, with a line counter ticking up: "users: 1M… 5M… 10M+." Right side wins.]

EV: Picture two companies chasing the same customers. One spends a fortune — ads everywhere, celebrity faces, the works. The other spends almost nothing on marketing. Barely advertises at all. Now guess which one became the biggest in its entire industry, and made its founders billionaires without taking a single rupee of outside funding. It's the quiet one. Zerodha. And how they did it is one of my favorite lessons in all of product. Let's go.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "ZERODHA — grow by not spending."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Gloss for the global crowd — Zerodha is India's largest stock brokerage, the app millions of people use to buy and sell shares. Bootstrapped. No venture money. And here's the principle I want to plant in your head today, because it's rare and it's powerful: **when your product is genuinely good and genuinely cheaper, the product becomes the marketing.** You don't buy growth. You earn it. Let me show you the three moves that made that work.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 1 — KILL THE FEE." A pricing table: competitors charge a percentage per trade; Zerodha's column flips to a bold "₹0 / flat tiny fee."]

EV: Move one — they attacked the thing everyone else quietly profited from. For decades, brokers charged you a percentage every time you traded. Trade more, pay more — it added up fast, and it was invisible friction eating your returns. Zerodha came in and said: we'll charge a tiny flat fee, and for a lot of trades, nothing at all. Think about what that does. It's not a discount. It's a reframe of the entire category. Suddenly the expensive incumbents look like they're overcharging — because they were. When your pricing is radically fairer, customers don't just switch. They tell people. Outrage at the old way is free distribution.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 2 — THE PRODUCT IS THE AD." A phone showing a famously clean, fast trading app called Kite; clutter from rival apps dissolves away.]

EV: Move two — they made the product itself so good it sold itself. Their trading app — it's called Kite — became known for being fast, clean, and genuinely pleasant to use in an industry full of clunky, confusing software. And here's the thing about a tool people use every single day: if it's delightful, they become walking billboards. A trader shows a friend their screen. "What's that? It's so clean." Boom — referral, and it cost Zerodha zero. This is the quiet superpower. Most companies treat product and marketing as separate departments. Zerodha collapsed them. The product *was* the growth team.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 3 — VARSITY." A free library of lessons opens up; a nervous first-timer reads, gains confidence, then opens an account. Caption "teach first, earn trust."]

EV: Move three — they gave away education, for free, at massive scale. They built something called Varsity — a huge, genuinely excellent, totally free library teaching ordinary people how markets work, how to invest, how not to lose their shirt. Now pause on that. A brokerage makes money when you trade. Teaching you to be careful and informed sounds almost against their interest. But look deeper. In a country where most people were scared of the stock market, the biggest barrier wasn't price — it was *fear and confusion.* By removing that fear, Zerodha created new customers who didn't exist before. And the person who teaches you something you were anxious about? You trust them. You open your account with them. Education wasn't charity. It was the smartest customer-acquisition engine in the business — and it built a moat of trust money can't buy.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THIS COMPOUNDS." A flywheel: fair pricing → happy users → word of mouth → more users → more scale → even lower costs → fairer pricing.]

EV: And here's why it all compounds instead of fizzling. Fair pricing brings people in. A delightful product keeps them and makes them refer others. Free education removes the fear that stopped the next wave. More users means more scale, which — because they're not burning it on ads — means they stay profitable and can keep prices low, which brings in *more* people. It's a flywheel that spins on trust and product quality instead of ad spend. The competitors were renting growth. Zerodha owned it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." A regulator's gavel labeled "SEBI" taps down; a revenue bar dips hard; a calm Zerodha stays standing while a VC-funded rival wobbles.]

EV: Now, honest current context — because it actually sharpens the lesson. India's market regulator, SEBI, cracked down hard on risky options trading across 2024 and 2025 — tighter rules, fewer weekly expiries. And since a big chunk of brokerage money came from exactly that frantic trading, revenues across the whole industry fell sharply, Zerodha's included — by something like forty percent. A fast-growing rival called Groww even passed them on sheer number of users. So — is the story broken? Watch closely, because this is the payoff of being bootstrapped. A company that had burned investor money to grow would be in crisis right now — scrambling, cutting, panicking to hit targets for anxious backers. Zerodha? Profitable, debt-free, no outside investors to please. They can absorb a brutal year *calmly*, because they never built on borrowed momentum. The discipline that looked boring in the good times is exactly what makes them unshakeable in the hard ones.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Earned growth beats bought growth — if the product earns it."]

EV: So the principle, clearly: paid marketing is renting attention. The moment you stop paying, it stops. But growth that comes from a product being genuinely better, fairer, and more trustworthy — that compounds and it's defensible, because a competitor can copy your ad but can't easily copy years of earned trust. Now, big honest caveat so you use this right: "just build a great product and they'll come" is a dangerous myth for *most* companies. This worked because Zerodha paired a great product with a radically better price *and* removed a real fear in the market. Great product, real wedge, genuine trust. All three. That's the recipe — not vibes.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "You have a tiny marketing budget. How do you grow?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a power move. If they constrain you — "you've got almost no marketing budget, how do you grow?" — don't shrink. Say: "I'd make the product the acquisition channel. One — is there a pricing or model change so bold that customers talk about it on their own? Two — can I make the core experience so good that daily users refer naturally? Three — can I give away genuine value upfront, like education, to remove the real barrier to adoption and build trust?" That's a founder's answer. That's Zerodha.

[VISUAL: Recap: the three moves — radical pricing, delightful product, free education — spinning the trust flywheel.]

EV: So — Zerodha. No war chest. No celebrity ads. Just a product fair enough to make rivals look greedy, clean enough to sell itself, and generous enough to turn scared beginners into loyal customers. They didn't buy a market. They earned one. And it made them one of the most profitable, independent companies in the game.
