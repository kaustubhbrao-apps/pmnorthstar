[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "AMAZON — the flywheel."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Amazon you know — the everything store. But the thing worth studying isn't the warehouses or the website. It's the *idea* underneath it all: a self-reinforcing growth loop where each part makes the next part stronger, around and around. Here's the principle: **the most powerful businesses aren't a straight line of features — they're a loop where success feeds more success.** Let me walk you around the wheel.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE WHEEL." A circular diagram, each arrow lighting up in turn as EV names it.]

EV: Okay, follow the loop with me. It starts with something customers always want — lower prices and a great experience. Lower prices and good service bring in more customers. Now, more customers — that's a crowd. And a crowd attracts sellers; every merchant wants to be where the buyers are. More sellers means more *selection* — more stuff available on Amazon than anywhere else. And more selection makes the customer experience even better, which brings in *even more* customers. See it? We've come all the way around. More customers, more sellers, more selection, better experience, more customers again. Each turn of the wheel makes the next turn easier. That's a flywheel.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY IT COMPOUNDS." The wheel spins faster with each loop; a straight-line "feature by feature" competitor falls behind.]

EV: And here's why this is so devastatingly effective. A heavy wheel is hard to push at first — the early days are a grind, it barely moves. But every push adds momentum, and momentum builds on momentum, so eventually the thing is spinning with enormous force and a tiny nudge keeps it roaring. Compare that to a competitor growing in a straight line, shipping one feature after another. Linear effort, linear results. Amazon built *compounding* effort — where the customers you won last year go to work recruiting the sellers who win you customers this year. Loops beat lines. Every time.

[VISUAL: Header: "FEEDING THE WHEEL — SCALE → LOWER COST." A giant warehouse; cost-per-item drops as volume rises; the savings get pushed back into lower prices.]

EV: Now here's the second, sneakier loop that powers the first. As Amazon got bigger, it got more *efficient* — gigantic scale meant lower costs per item, better logistics, cheaper operations. And what did they do with those savings? They didn't just pocket them. They pushed them *back into lower prices.* Which — look at the wheel — brings in more customers, which spins everything faster. So growth lowered their costs, and lower costs fueled more growth. That's a loop inside the loop. This is the discipline most companies miss: they'd take the savings as profit. Amazon fed them back into the flywheel to make it spin harder.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MASTERSTROKE — AWS." The warehouse of servers Amazon built for itself opens its doors and starts renting space to the whole internet.]

EV: And here's the masterstroke that ties this whole series together. To run its own massive operation, Amazon had to build enormous computing infrastructure — servers, data centers, the works. Most companies would treat that as a pure cost. Amazon looked at it and asked a founder's question: "we built this capability for ourselves — could we *rent it out?*" And that became Amazon Web Services, the business that rents computing power to much of the internet, and one of the most profitable things they do. Notice the pattern — it's the same instinct as everything we've studied. Zomato turning a rider network into groceries. Nike turning stores into data. Take a capability you built for one thing and find the next loop it can power. A cost became a flywheel of its own.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." A new arrow joins the wheel labeled "AI"; a shopping assistant answers a buyer's question; AWS data centers hum; a capex counter spins past $100 billion.]

EV: And right now, the newest spoke on the wheel is AI — same flywheel logic, brand-new fuel. Amazon's pouring staggering money into it: well over a hundred billion dollars a year on data centers and chips, including a multi-billion-dollar bet on the AI company Anthropic. Why? Watch the loop. That AI powers AWS, which sells computing to basically everyone building in the AI boom — a huge, growing engine. And it powers a shopping assistant on the retail side, called Rufus, that helps you find and decide what to buy — hundreds of millions of shoppers used it, and people who do tend to complete their purchases far more often, reportedly driving billions in extra sales. See it? Retail scale justifies the AI spend; the AI makes both AWS *and* shopping better; which drives more scale; which funds more AI. Decades in, they're still just... feeding the wheel. The fuel changes. The machine doesn't.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Build loops, not lines. Feed the wheel."]

EV: So the principle, the one I want you to carry out of this whole series: don't just think in features and straight lines — think in loops. Ask, "what's the wheel here? How does winning a customer today make it easier to win the next one? How does getting bigger make me *better*, not just larger?" And when you find savings or build a new capability, feed it back into the loop instead of just banking it. The companies that compound are the ones that build flywheels and then relentlessly feed them.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you build a durable, defensible business?"]

EV: And in an interview, ending on this makes you sound like a true strategist. If they ask how you'd build something durable and defensible, don't list features. Say: "I'd design a flywheel — a loop where each win makes the next one easier. For a marketplace, more buyers attract sellers, who add selection, which attracts more buyers. Then I'd look for reinforcing loops — like scale lowering costs that I reinvest into lower prices — and for capabilities I've built that could become their own engine, the way Amazon turned its own infrastructure into AWS." That's flywheel thinking. That's the answer that makes an interviewer lean in.

[VISUAL: Recap: the main wheel spinning, the cost-savings loop inside it, and AWS spinning off as its own wheel.]

EV: So — Amazon. Not a straight line of clever features, but a wheel — lower prices bring customers, customers bring sellers, sellers bring selection, selection brings more customers. Scale lowered costs, and they fed those savings right back in. And a capability built for themselves became AWS, a flywheel of its own. One simple loop, pushed relentlessly for decades, became the biggest store on earth.
