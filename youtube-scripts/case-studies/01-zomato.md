Quick gloss for anyone outside India — Zomato's one of the two giants that bring food from the restaurant to your door, think of it like DoorDash or Deliveroo, in a market of over a billion people. And here's the lesson I want you to walk away with, because it's one of the most useful ideas in all of product: **profitability isn't a finance thing you bolt on at the end. It's a product problem.** The teams that win learn to design the economics, not just the experience. Watch how they did it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TRAP." Three delivery bikes zig-zagging across a city, each dropping one order, caption "1 rider · 1 order · lots of petrol."]

EV: Okay, so — first, why was food delivery such a money pit? The math is brutal. You've got a rider, a bike, fuel, and time. If that rider picks up one order and drives it across town for a small fee, you lose money on the trip. Every single order is a tiny negative. Scale that to millions of orders a day and you're not building a business, you're building a very popular way to set money on fire. A lot of founders would've just kept raising cash and praying. Zomato did something sharper. They treated the loss on each order as a *design problem* — something the product could actually fix.

[VISUAL: Header: "LEVER 1 — DENSITY." Same map, but now one rider's path loops through three nearby drop-offs. Caption "1 rider · 3 orders."]

EV: Lever one — density. Here's the thing: the cost of a delivery barely changes whether the rider carries one order or three. So if you can get a rider to pick up three orders heading the same direction and drop them in one smart loop, your cost *per order* collapses. Suddenly the trip that lost money makes money. And how do you get that density? Product. Better batching algorithms, smarter dispatch, nudging restaurants to prep faster, concentrating demand in zones. None of that is glamorous. It's deeply unsexy backend optimization. And it's exactly where the profit was hiding.

[VISUAL: Header: "LEVER 2 — THE OTHER REVENUE." A restaurant dashboard lights up: "Promote your listing," "Ads," caption "the restaurants pay too."]

EV: Lever two — they stopped relying only on the customer to pay. Think about who's on this platform. Not just you, hungry at 9pm. Also thousands of restaurants, all desperate to be seen. So Zomato built an ads business — restaurants pay to show up higher, to run promotions, to get discovered. That's high-margin money that doesn't cost a rider or a drop of fuel. Then they added services for the restaurants themselves. The platform quietly became two businesses: a delivery business with thin margins, and an advertising-and-services business with fat ones, riding on the same traffic. That second one is the quiet hero.

[VISUAL: Header: "LEVER 3 — BLINKIT." A grocery bag appears next to the food bag; a clock spins to "10 minutes." Caption "same riders, new habit."]

EV: Lever three — and this is the bold one. They bought into quick-commerce. Blinkit — groceries and essentials at your door in about ten minutes. Now from the outside people went, "wait, you're not even profitable in food, why add a *second* hard business?" But look at it like a product strategist. They already had the hardest, most expensive thing in the world to build: a dense network of riders and dark stores and routing software across cities. Groceries ride on top of that same muscle. And here's the kicker — people order food a few times a week, but groceries? Constantly. Higher frequency means more orders flowing through the same network, which means more density, which — remember lever one — means better economics for *everything*. The bet was that quick-commerce wasn't a distraction from the core. It *was* the core, extended.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE FLIP." The red graph from the open, now redrawn: three stacked layers — delivery (thin green), ads (fat green), Blinkit (growing) — summing to a profit line above zero.]

EV: Stack those three and you see the flip. Delivery margins creep positive as density improves. A high-margin ads engine prints cash on top. And a frequency machine in quick-commerce feeds the whole network. The company that couldn't make a dollar on dinner turned its first real profits — not by charging you a fortune, but by *redesigning the economics underneath the product.* Same customers. Same craving at 9pm. Completely different business.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." The parent company's name flips from "Zomato" to "Eternal"; four tiles — Zomato, Blinkit, Hyperpure, District — with Blinkit glowing biggest.]

EV: And here's where it stands today, because the story kept moving. In 2025 the parent company renamed itself Eternal — Zomato's still the food app, but it now sits alongside Blinkit, the supply arm Hyperpure, and a going-out business called District. And the plot twist? Blinkit, the quick-commerce bet people once called a distraction, has become the *largest* consumer business in the whole group — roughly half of all the orders flowing through. They're even letting profit dip on purpose right now, pouring money into opening Blinkit dark stores as fast as humanly possible, because they've decided winning quick-commerce is worth a temporary hit to the bottom line. That's the exact same lesson playing out live — they're still treating the economics as something to engineer, not just report.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." A single card: "Unit economics is a product decision."]

EV: So here's the principle, and I want you to really hold onto this one. When something loses money per unit, weak teams say "that's just the cost of the business." Strong teams ask, "which product levers change the unit economics?" Batching changed the cost side. Ads changed the revenue side. Frequency changed the whole network. Every one of those is a product decision, not an accounting trick. Profit got *designed*.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." A mock prompt: "This feature loses money per user. What do you do?"]

EV: And this is gold in a case interview. When they hand you a business that's bleeding on every transaction and ask how you'd fix it, don't jump to "raise prices" or "cut costs" — everyone says that. Say this instead: "I'd attack the unit economics through the product. One — can I increase density or utilization so the fixed cost spreads over more value? Two — is there an adjacent, higher-margin revenue stream the same traffic unlocks, like ads? Three — can I raise frequency so the expensive network I've built gets used more often?" That's the Zomato answer. That's a candidate who thinks like an operator, not a spectator.

[VISUAL: Recap: three levers — density, ads, frequency — feeding one green profit line.]

EV: So — Zomato. The story everyone remembers is "startup burns cash." The story that matters is *how they stopped.* Density made each trip pay. Ads added margin the riders never touched. And quick-commerce turned an occasional habit into a daily one, feeding the whole machine. Profitability wasn't rescue. It was product.
