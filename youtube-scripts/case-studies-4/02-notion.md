[VISUAL: Cold open. A row of rigid single-purpose apps sits in little cages — a notes app, a to-do app, a database app — each locked to one shape. Then a blank white page appears, and a cursor starts dragging identical blocks onto it: a heading, a checklist, a table, a gallery — snapping together like LEGO into a custom tool that didn't exist a second ago. EV: Here's a different way to think about building a product. Most software makes a decision *for* you — this is a notes app, this is a to-do app, this is a spreadsheet, and you live inside the shape the maker chose. Notion did something stranger. Instead of building *an* app, it built a set of simple blocks — text, lists, tables, images — and let *you* snap them together into whatever tool you needed. A wiki, a project tracker, a personal planner, an entire company's knowledge base. Same blocks, infinite apps. It's less a product and more a box of LEGO for software. And that flexibility — plus a near-death moment where they threw it all away — is the whole story. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "NOTION — flexibility as the product."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Notion is the all-in-one workspace people use for notes, docs, wikis, databases — often replacing five separate apps at once. But the lesson worth studying isn't any single feature. It's a philosophy of product design. Here's the principle: **instead of building a rigid tool that does one thing your way, you can build flexible *building blocks* and let users assemble the exact tool they need. You hand them power and trust them to shape it — and in return you fit a thousand use cases one fixed app never could.** Let me show you how that works, and why it almost didn't.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE RIGID-APP TRAP." A grid of single-purpose apps, each a locked box. A user juggling eight of them, copy-pasting between, nothing talking to anything. Caption: "a different app for every little job."]

EV: First, the problem Notion set out to solve. Think about how most people's digital work *actually* looks: one app for notes, another for tasks, another for documents, a spreadsheet for tracking, a separate tool for the team wiki. Eight apps, none of them talking to each other, you copy-pasting between them all day. Each app is rigid — it does its one job its one way, and if your need is slightly different, tough. The software decided the shape; you contort to fit it. That fragmentation is friction, and it's everywhere. Notion's founders looked at that mess and asked a bigger question: what if, instead of *another* single-purpose app, we gave people raw materials flexible enough to build *any* of those tools themselves?

[VISUAL: Header: "THE UNLOCK — EVERYTHING IS A BLOCK." A single block appears, then morphs: text → checkbox → table row → image → toggle → linked page. The user drags blocks together; a custom CRM assembles itself, then a content calendar, then a habit tracker.]

EV: And here's the core idea, and it's genuinely clever: in Notion, *everything is a block.* A line of text is a block. A to-do is a block. A table, an image, a whole embedded page — blocks. And because they're all the same fundamental Lego-like unit, you can snap them together in any arrangement you can imagine. Want a project tracker? Stack a table, some status tags, and a few linked pages. Want a personal wiki, a reading list, a company handbook, a CRM? Same blocks, different arrangement. The maker didn't build a CRM or a wiki — the maker built the *blocks*, and the user builds the CRM. That's the flip: Notion shipped *capability and flexibility* instead of a fixed destination, and let a million users each build the exact tool their weird, specific workflow needed. One product, endless shapes.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE RISK — FLEXIBILITY CAN OVERWHELM." A blank page with infinite options; a new user frozen, unsure where to start. Caption: "freedom is scary when you're staring at nothing."]

EV: Now — and this is the honest tension — radical flexibility has a dark side, and good product people have to respect it. A blank, do-anything canvas can *paralyze* people. When software can be anything, a brand-new user often freezes: "okay… what do I even do with this?" A rigid app is limiting, but it's also *obvious* — it tells you exactly what to do. So Notion's real design challenge wasn't adding power; it was making all that power *approachable*. Their answer: templates. Ready-made setups — a planner, a tracker, a wiki — so you don't start from a terrifying blank page; you start from something that already works, then bend it to your needs. That's the balance every flexible product has to strike: give people power, but give them a *doorway* in, or the power scares them off. Flexibility without onboarding isn't freedom. It's a blank stare.

EV: If this is useful, subscribing to pmnorthstar helps the series keep going. Now — the part most people don't know: Notion almost died, and the fix was brutal.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE NEAR-DEATH REBUILD." A rickety early version buckling under its own weight, crashing. Then the team wipes the whiteboard completely clean, moves to a cheaper city to stretch their last money, and rebuilds the entire thing from scratch.]

EV: Here's the part that makes Notion a real founder story, not just a design essay. The early version was ambitious — and a buggy, bloated mess. It was slow, it crashed, it tried to do too much, and it was going nowhere. The company was nearly out of money and nearly out of road. And the founders made a decision most teams can't stomach: they *threw almost all of it away.* Laid the plans bare, cut the team down to a tiny core, moved somewhere cheaper to stretch their last runway, and spent roughly a year rebuilding the entire product from the ground up — simpler, faster, cleaner. It was a bet-the-company gamble: scrap years of work and rebuild, or slowly die shipping something broken. They rebuilt. And *that* version — the clean, fast, block-based one — is the Notion that took off. The lesson tucked inside: sometimes the bravest, most necessary product decision is to admit what you built is wrong and start over, before the market forces you to.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Notion everywhere — tens of millions of users, teams and students and solo creators; an AI layer built into the blocks that writes, summarizes, and answers questions across your workspace; a valuation in the billions.]

EV: And today? That rebuilt box of blocks became one of the most loved productivity tools in the world — tens of millions of users, from students running their whole lives in it to companies running their entire knowledge base on it, and a valuation in the billions. And watch how the flexibility keeps paying off: when AI arrived, Notion didn't have to bolt on a separate product — it wove AI *into the blocks*, so now the same canvas can write for you, summarize your notes, and answer questions across everything you've built. Because the foundation was flexible, it could absorb a whole new capability without breaking its identity. The near-death rebuild didn't just save the company; it gave it a foundation strong enough to keep evolving. Flexibility, it turns out, isn't just a feature for users — it's future-proofing for the company.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Ship the blocks, not just the building. Flexibility is a feature."]

EV: So the principle to carry: you don't always have to decide *exactly* how your product will be used. Sometimes the most powerful move is to build flexible building blocks and trust your users to assemble something you never imagined — because their needs are more varied than any fixed app can serve. But — and this is the discipline — pair that flexibility with a gentle doorway, templates and guidance, so the freedom empowers instead of paralyzes. And don't be afraid of the hard rebuild: if what you've built is fundamentally wrong, the bravest product decision can be to tear it down and start clean. Ask: "am I handing my users a cage, or a box of blocks?"

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "When would you build a flexible, configurable product versus an opinionated one?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a thoughtful answer that shows real judgment. If they ask flexible-versus-opinionated, don't just pick a side. Say: "It's a trade-off. A rigid, opinionated product is easier to learn but serves fewer needs; a flexible, block-based one — like Notion — serves endless use cases but risks overwhelming users. I'd choose flexibility when users' needs are genuinely varied, *and* I'd invest heavily in templates and onboarding so the freedom doesn't paralyze them." Then add the founder beat: "And I'd stay willing to rebuild — Notion nearly died shipping a bloated version and only won after scrapping it and starting clean." That shows you understand both design philosophy and the courage product sometimes demands.

[VISUAL: Recap: the caged single-purpose apps, the blank page, blocks snapping into any tool, the terrifying blank page softened by templates, the whiteboard wiped clean and rebuilt.]

EV: So — Notion. Instead of building one more rigid app, it built the blocks and let users build their own — flexibility *as* the product — then made that power approachable with templates, and survived only because it was brave enough to throw away a broken version and rebuild. Hand people blocks, not cages. Give them a doorway in. And never be too proud to start over. That's how you build a tool a million different people each make their own.
