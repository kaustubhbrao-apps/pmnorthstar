EV: Here's a product idea that sounds, at first, completely backwards. Every social app was built on *permanence* — you post a photo, it lives forever on your profile, racking up likes, part of your carefully curated highlight reel. Then Snapchat showed up and did the opposite: photos and messages that *delete themselves* after a few seconds. Gone. No permanent record, no like counter, no highlight reel. Critics thought it was pointless, even reckless — why would you build a product around things *disappearing*? But that "crazy" feature tapped into exactly how real people, especially young people, actually wanted to communicate. And it built a company worth tens of billions. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "SNAPCHAT — bet on a behavior, own a format."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Snapchat is the camera-first app where you send disappearing photos and videos, play with face-altering lenses, and share your day in "Stories." But the lesson is about something deeper than any feature. Here's the principle: **sometimes the biggest opportunity is a human behavior the incumbents find embarrassing or beneath them — and if you build for it unapologetically, you can own a whole new format so completely that the giants are forced to copy *you*.** Let me show you how Snapchat spotted the behavior, built for it, and flipped the whole game.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE PRESSURE OF PERMANENCE." A profile grid of flawless, filtered, posed photos; a user agonizing before posting, checking the like count, deleting a post that "didn't do well." Caption: "social media became a performance."]

EV: Start with the pain nobody was naming. Social media had turned into a *performance.* Because everything you posted was permanent and public and counted — likes, comments, followers — people felt enormous pressure to only share the *perfect* stuff: the posed photo, the flattering angle, the highlight. Your profile became a carefully managed personal brand, and posting became stressful — "will this get enough likes? does this fit my feed? what will people think *forever*?" But here's the thing: that's *not* how people actually communicate with their close friends. In real life, you send your friend a dumb face, a random moment, an ugly-funny selfie — stuff that's meant to be *in the moment*, not preserved for eternity and judged. The permanent, performative feed had no room for that. There was a huge gap between how people were *allowed* to share online and how they actually wanted to talk to their friends.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE UNLOCK — MAKE IT DISAPPEAR." A Snap sent, viewed, *poof* — gone. No like count. No profile grid. Just a fun moment between friends, then erased. Caption: "no record = no pressure."]

EV: And here's Snapchat's move, and once you see the *why*, it stops sounding crazy and starts sounding obvious. Make the photos and messages *disappear* after they're viewed. And look at what that one decision unlocks. No permanent record means *no pressure to be perfect* — if it's gone in seconds, who cares if it's a goofy, unflattering, silly moment? That's the *point.* It freed people to be real, spontaneous, and fun, the way they actually are with close friends. No public like-counter means no performance, no popularity contest — just communication. Disappearing messages felt *intimate and low-stakes*, like passing a note, not publishing to the world. The feature everyone mocked as pointless was actually removing the single biggest source of friction in social sharing: the fear of permanence. Snapchat didn't add a gimmick. It *deleted the pressure.*

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY INCUMBENTS MISSED IT." The big players, built entirely on permanent profiles and like counts, literally *can't* see the value in disappearing content — it threatens their whole model. Caption: "it looked like a flaw. it was the feature."]

EV: Now, *why* didn't the giants do this first? This is the strategic heart of it. The incumbents had built their *entire empires* on permanence — permanent profiles, permanent posts, the accumulated like-and-follower counts that kept you hooked and kept advertisers happy. To them, "content that disappears" didn't look like an opportunity; it looked like a *bug*, even a threat — it undermined everything their business was built on. So they dismissed it. And that's the pattern: the behaviors that create the biggest openings are often the ones incumbents find *embarrassing, trivial, or threatening* — "teenagers sending silly disappearing selfies" was easy to sneer at. But beneath the goofiness was a profound truth about how people want to communicate. Snapchat took the behavior seriously *because* the giants wouldn't. The thing that looks beneath a market leader's dignity is often exactly where the next leader is born.

[VISUAL: Header: "OWNING A FORMAT — STORIES." The "Stories" format appears: a day's moments strung together, vanishing after 24 hours. Then that exact format gets copied — into Instagram, WhatsApp, everywhere. Caption: "they invented a format the whole world adopted."]

EV: And here's where Snapchat went from "clever app" to "shaped the entire industry." It invented a new *format*: Stories — a string of photos and videos from your day, shown in sequence, that disappears after twenty-four hours. It was the perfect middle ground between a permanent post and a one-to-one disappearing snap: share your day with everyone, but with no pressure, because it's gone tomorrow. Stories were a *massive* hit. And here's the proof of how good the idea was: the biggest social companies in the world *copied it wholesale* — the Stories format got cloned into Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook, basically everywhere, and became a standard way billions of people now share. Now, the honest tension: the giants copying Stories also *hurt* Snapchat badly — a bigger network can clone your format and out-distribute you, and Snap has had a genuinely hard competitive road because of it. But make no mistake: Snapchat *invented the format the entire industry adopted.* It didn't just build a product. It changed how the whole world shares.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Snapchat still huge with young users — hundreds of millions daily, a leader in augmented-reality lenses and camera tech, a real advertising business — even as it fights permanent pressure from far larger rivals who copied its best ideas. The disappearing-photo app that reshaped social media.]

EV: And today? Snapchat is still a major platform, especially beloved by younger users — hundreds of millions of people use it every day, it's a genuine leader in augmented-reality camera tech and those face-altering lenses, and it built a real advertising business. It fights a tough, ongoing battle against far larger rivals who copied its formats — that's the honest part of the story. But its *influence* is undeniable: the disappearing message, the Stories format, the camera-first interface, the AR lens — Snapchat pioneered features now woven into how *everyone* shares online. It spotted a behavior the giants were too proud to serve, built for it without apology, and ended up defining formats the entire industry runs on. That's a legacy most far-bigger companies never achieve.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Serve the behavior incumbents won't. Own the format."]

EV: So the principle to carry: the next big opportunity is often hiding in a human behavior that the current leaders find trivial, embarrassing, or threatening — something they won't touch because it doesn't fit their model or their dignity. Pay attention to how people *actually* behave, especially the messy, unglamorous, in-the-moment stuff, not how the current products *make* them behave. If there's a real human need going unserved because the incumbents can't or won't see it, build for it unapologetically — and if you can turn that behavior into a new *format*, you can shape the whole industry, even force the giants to follow you. Ask: "what do people genuinely want to do that the market leaders are too proud to build for?"

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Where do you look for a disruptive product opportunity?"]

EV: And in an interview, this shows real product vision. If they ask where you'd look for a disruptive opportunity, don't say "a feature competitors don't have." Say: "I'd look for a genuine human behavior the incumbents *won't* serve — often because it looks trivial or threatens their model. Snapchat built on disappearing messages, which the giants dismissed as pointless, but it removed the pressure of permanence that real friends don't want — and then owned the Stories format the whole industry copied." Then land it: "The biggest openings are often the behaviors market leaders are too proud to build for." That tells an interviewer you find opportunities in human truth, not just feature gaps.

EV: So — Snapchat. It bet on a behavior everyone else mocked — messages that disappear — because it saw the truth underneath: people wanted to share without the pressure of permanence. It served that behavior without apology, invented the Stories format, and forced the entire industry to copy it. Watch how people really behave, serve the need the giants are too proud to touch, and own the format. That's the disappearing act that reshaped social media.
