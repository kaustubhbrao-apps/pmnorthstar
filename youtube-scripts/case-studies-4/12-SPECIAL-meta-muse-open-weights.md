[VISUAL: Header: "OPEN vs. CLOSED." Left: a closed model — locked box, pay-per-use, you can't see inside or run it yourself. Right: an open-weight model — you download it, run it on your own machines, inspect it, customize it, free. Caption: "one you rent. the other you own."]

EV: First, the difference between "closed" and "open" AI, because the whole move hinges on it. Most top models are *closed* — you access them over the internet and pay per use, you can't see how they work inside, and you definitely can't download and run your own copy. It's a product you *rent*, forever, from the company that owns it. An *open-weight* model is the opposite: the company publishes the actual trained model — the "weights" — so anyone can download it, run it on their *own* computers, look inside it, and customize it freely, usually at no cost. You *own* a copy; you're not renting access. Now ask the obvious question: if your competitors are making money *renting* access to their models, and you release one that's nearly as good that people can just *download and own for free*… what happens to the thing your competitors are selling? It gets a lot harder to charge for. That's the whole idea.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE WEAPON — COMMODITIZE YOUR RIVAL'S PRODUCT." A dial labeled 'price of a good AI model' being dragged toward $0 by Meta's free release; the closed-model companies' revenue stream thinning as it drops. Caption: "make their cash cow free."]

EV: And here's the weapon, stated plainly. For companies whose *entire business* is selling access to a top AI model, Meta releasing a comparably powerful model *for free* is a direct attack on their revenue. Why would a developer or company pay ongoing fees for a closed model when there's a free, open one that's nearly as capable, that they can run and control themselves? Meta is effectively dragging the *price of a good AI model toward zero* — "commoditizing" it. And here's the key: Meta can *afford* to do this, because selling AI-model access was never Meta's main business. Meta makes its money elsewhere — mostly advertising across its apps. So the powerful AI model isn't Meta's *product* to protect; it's a *complement* — something that makes Meta's actual business better. And there's a famous principle: **"commoditize your complement."** Make the thing *next to* your business cheap or free, so more of it gets used, which drives more value to the business you *actually* monetize — while quietly wrecking the economics of rivals who were trying to sell that very thing. Free, aimed precisely.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHAT META ACTUALLY GAINS." Three arrows out of the free release: developers build on Meta's model (mindshare + standard), Meta learns from the whole ecosystem, and closed rivals' pricing power erodes. Caption: "give the model, win the ecosystem."]

EV: So what does Meta actually *get* out of giving away something this expensive to build? Several things, and they're big. One: *mindshare and standardization.* When the best free model is yours, developers, researchers, and companies worldwide build on *your* model — it becomes a default, a standard, and all that activity, tooling, and talent orbits Meta. Two: *ecosystem and goodwill* — a huge community improving, extending, and stress-testing your model, work you didn't have to pay for, plus serious credibility with the developers who shape the future. Three — the competitive one — it *erodes the pricing power* of closed-model rivals, keeping any single competitor from getting too dominant or too rich selling what Meta just made free. Meta doesn't need to win by *selling* the model. It wins by making powerful AI *abundant and Meta-flavored*, so the whole field tilts its way while rivals who bet on *scarcity* find the ground giving out under them. You give away the model to win the *ecosystem* — and the ecosystem is worth far more.

EV: But let's be honest about the complications, because "open" gets romanticized. First, *open-weight* isn't the same as "anyone can easily use it" — a model this enormous needs *serious* hardware to run, so in practice it's mostly companies and serious developers, not you on your laptop. Second, the license usually has *strings* — "open" here comes with terms that limit some commercial uses, so it's open on Meta's conditions, not a total free-for-all. Third, there are genuine *safety* debates: a powerful model anyone can download and modify is harder to control, and can be used for harm as well as good — reasonable people disagree hard about whether releasing these openly is wise. And fourth, strategically, "free" is a position, not a promise — a company giving away models to win the ecosystem *today* has a lot of influence over that ecosystem *tomorrow.* So the open-source weapon is powerful, but it's not pure generosity, and it's not without real risks. Smart product people hold both: it's a brilliant strategic move *and* a genuinely complicated one.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Attack a rival by making what they sell free — and win where they can't follow."]

EV: So the principle to carry from this one: when you're competing against someone whose whole business is *selling* a particular thing, one of the most powerful moves is to make that thing *free* — *if* you make your money somewhere else. Commoditize your rival's product, so their advantage evaporates, while you build your strength in a business they can't easily attack. Meta gave away a world-class AI model because models aren't how Meta makes money — so free models hurt its rivals far more than they hurt Meta, and win it the whole developer ecosystem. Ask, when you're the underdog: "what is my competitor *selling* that I could make *free* — because I profit somewhere they can't follow?" Sometimes the sharpest weapon in business isn't a better product. It's a free one.
