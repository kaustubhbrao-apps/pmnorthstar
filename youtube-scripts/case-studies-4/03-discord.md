EV: Here's a lesson about who your product is *really* for. A team built a chat-and-voice app for one very specific group: gamers who needed to talk to each other, clearly and instantly, while playing together. They nailed it — low lag, crystal voice, easy to organize. And then something they didn't plan for happened. Study groups started using it. Then clubs, artists, crypto communities, fan groups, even companies. People who had never touched a video game moved into an app built *for* gamers — because the thing it did well, letting any group hang out and talk in real time, turned out to be something *everyone* wanted. Discord's whole arc is about recognizing that, and leaning in. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "DISCORD — serve the adjacent audience."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Discord is the app of "servers" — communities built around voice channels and text chat, where any group can gather and hang out. It started laser-focused on gamers. But the lesson is one every product person needs: **the users who adopt your product are often not the ones you built it for. When an unexpected audience shows up, that's not noise — it's a signal. The winning move is to notice who else wants what you made, and expand the definition of who your product is for.** Let me walk through how Discord did exactly that.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE FOCUSED START." A single clear job: gamers talking while they play. Rivals are clunky, laggy, ugly. Discord is fast, clean, free. Caption: "nail one audience, completely."]

EV: Start with why Discord won its first audience so decisively, because focus was the foundation. Before it, gamers used voice tools that were clunky, laggy, or a pain to set up. Discord showed up and did that one job beautifully — crisp voice with almost no lag, dead-simple servers and channels, works on any device, free. It wasn't trying to be everything. It was trying to be the *best possible tool for one specific group doing one specific thing*: playing games together and needing to talk. And that focus is exactly why it got so good, so fast — a sharp tool for a sharp need. Gamers adopted it in droves, and importantly, gamers are intense, communal, word-of-mouth users. Nail a passionate niche, and they'll carry you. That's lesson one, and it's the setup for everything: Discord earned the right to expand by first dominating a focused beachhead.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE UNEXPECTED SIGNAL." Server names drifting in that have nothing to do with gaming: "Bio Study Group," "Watercolor Club," "Local Runners," "Startup Team." Caption: "wait… who ARE these people?"]

EV: And here's the pivotal moment — and it's easy to miss if you're not paying attention. Non-gamers started showing up. Study groups realized Discord was a perfect place to meet and work together. Hobby communities — artists, musicians, runners, readers — set up servers to hang out. Then online communities of every kind, and eventually even work teams. These people weren't using Discord for gaming at all. They just wanted a good place for their *group* to talk, in voice and text, organized into channels. Now, here's the fork a lot of companies get wrong: when users you didn't design for start pouring in, you can dismiss them as "not our target," or you can *listen*. Discord listened. It recognized that the core thing it had built — a great home for any community to gather and talk — was valuable *far* beyond games. The unexpected users weren't a distraction from the mission. They were revealing a bigger one.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE REPOSITION — FROM GAMERS TO EVERYONE." The tagline literally rewrites itself on screen: "chat for gamers" → "a place to talk and hang out." The blurple world expands to hold every kind of community.]

EV: So Discord made a deliberate, gutsy move: it *repositioned.* It stopped describing itself as an app for gamers and started describing itself as a place for *any* community to belong — "your place to talk." It updated its messaging, its onboarding, its features to welcome everyone, not just raiders and shooters. And notice how careful that is: repositioning is risky. Your original, loyal audience can feel abandoned if you chase new users badly. So Discord expanded the *definition* of who it was for without abandoning the gamers who built it — the gaming features stayed great; the doors just opened wider. It expanded the tent instead of moving it. That's the craft of serving an adjacent audience: you grow the circle of who belongs, while keeping the people already inside feeling at home.

EV: Quick one — if this is landing, subscribe to pmnorthstar so these keep coming. Now — why the *same* product worked for such different people.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY ONE PRODUCT FIT THEM ALL." Under gaming clans, study groups, and fan communities sits the same engine: real-time voice, organized channels, a sense of belonging. Caption: "the need was never 'gaming.' it was 'community.'"]

EV: And here's the deeper insight, the thing that made the expansion *work* rather than just hopeful. Discord could serve gamers and book clubs and startups with basically the same product because the real underlying need was never "gaming." It was *community* — the human desire for a group of people to have a shared space to talk, in real time, and feel like they belong. Gamers happened to be the first group that need showed up in most intensely. But that need is universal. So the product didn't have to change much to serve everyone, because it was solving a human need, not a gaming need. That's the lesson under the lesson: when you understand the *fundamental human need* your product serves, you often discover your market is far bigger than the first audience you found it in. Discord was never really a gaming app. It was a belonging app that started with gamers.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Hundreds of millions of users across every imaginable community — gaming still huge, but also education, creators, crypto, fandoms, businesses; a valuation in the billions; "Discord server" now a default for any online community.]

EV: And today? Discord is home to *hundreds of millions* of people across every kind of community imaginable — yes, still gaming, hugely, but also classrooms, creator fandoms, local clubs, professional groups, and more, with a valuation in the billions. "Set up a Discord" has become the default move for anyone starting an online community, about anything. The app built for gamers to coordinate raids became the general-purpose home for human belonging on the internet — and it got there not by abandoning its first audience, but by *listening* when a bigger one walked through the door. The niche was the launchpad. The adjacent audiences were the destination.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Your real users may not be the ones you built for. Expand the tent."]

EV: So the principle to carry: start focused — nail one specific audience completely, because focus is how you get *good*. But then *watch who actually shows up.* When unexpected users adopt your product, don't dismiss them — study them, because they're telling you the real, often bigger need your product serves. And when the signal is strong, have the courage to expand the definition of who you're for, carefully, without abandoning the people who got you here. Ask constantly: "who's using this that I didn't expect — and what does that reveal about what I've *actually* built?" Your biggest market might be standing right next to the one you aimed at.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Users outside your target market are adopting your product. What do you do?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a great signal of product instinct. If they say "people outside your target are using the product," never say "ignore them, they're not our segment." Say: "That's one of the most valuable signals you can get. I'd dig into *why* they're adopting it — usually it means the core value I built serves a broader need than I realized. Discord was built for gamers and found study groups, clubs, and whole communities wanted the same thing: a place to belong." Then land it: "I'd consider expanding the definition of my audience to welcome them — carefully, without abandoning my core users — because that adjacent audience might be a far bigger market." That shows you treat users as teachers, not just targets.

[VISUAL: Recap: the gamer room, the walls dissolving, the flood of new communities, the tagline rewriting itself, the shared engine of belonging underneath.]

EV: So — Discord. Built perfectly for one audience, it had the wisdom to notice when everyone else moved in, and the courage to open the doors wider without kicking out the people who built it. It understood it was never really a gaming app — it was a belonging app. Start focused. Watch who shows up. And when a bigger need reveals itself, expand the tent. That's how a tool for gamers became home for the whole internet.
