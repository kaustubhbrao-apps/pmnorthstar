A general-purpose home robot has to handle an almost infinite variety of tasks in a wildly *unpredictable, chaotic* environment — every home is different, every task is fuzzy, things move, kids and pets wander through, and "fold the laundry" actually hides a thousand sub-problems. That is *staggeringly* hard — it's basically unsolved, and chasing it head-on means you build something that does a little of everything *badly* and isn't reliable enough for anyone to actually use or pay for. [VISUAL: Header: "THE SMART MOVE — ONE NARROW, CONTROLLED JOB." A factory: structured, predictable, the same task repeated; the robot does *one* thing — move parts into trolleys — reliably, all day. Caption: "pick a slice you can actually nail."]

EV: And here's the smart move Figure and BMW made — go *narrow* and go *controlled.* Instead of a robot that does everything in your chaotic home, deploy a robot that does *one specific job* in a *structured, predictable* place: a factory. Think about why a factory is the *perfect* starting point. The environment is controlled and consistent — same layout, same lighting, same conditions every day, unlike the chaos of a home. The task is well-defined and repetitive — "pick these parts, put them in this trolley, in this order" — the kind of narrow, repeatable job a robot can actually learn to do *reliably.* And the value is *immediate and measurable* — a factory has clear, paid work that needs doing, so if the robot does it well, there's real money and real demand *today*, not someday. By picking one narrow, valuable, *achievable* task in a *controlled* environment, Figure gets its robots doing real work *now* — succeeding where it counts instead of failing at everything at once. Narrow isn't a compromise. It's the whole strategy.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY NARROW WINS — THE EXPANSION PATH." The robot masters task one → earns revenue + real-world data → learns task two → task three → slowly generalizes. A ladder, not a leap. Caption: "each rung funds and teaches the next."]

EV: And here's *why* starting narrow is so powerful — it's not just easier, it *compounds* into the big goal. Watch the ladder. The robot masters *one* task reliably. That does three things. One: it earns *revenue* — real paid work — which funds the next stage of development, instead of burning investor money with nothing deployed. Two: it generates priceless *real-world data* — every hour working in a real factory teaches the robot and its makers things no lab ever could, about how the machine actually performs in the messy real world. Three: once it's nailed task one, you add task two, then task three, each a bit harder, *gradually* building toward a more capable, general robot — but now funded by revenue and trained on real data at every step. So "start narrow" isn't abandoning the big dream of a general robot; it's the *realistic path to it.* You climb to the ambitious goal one achievable, *paid*, data-rich rung at a time — instead of trying to leap to the top and falling. The narrow job is rung one of the ladder to the dream.
