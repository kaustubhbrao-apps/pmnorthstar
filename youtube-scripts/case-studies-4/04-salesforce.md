EV: Here's how you win a market you're late to: you rename the game. Back when business software meant buying physical discs, installing them on your own servers for months, and paying a fortune to maintain them, a new company showed up selling something radical — business software you just *log into*, in a web browser, like a website. No discs. No servers. No install. And instead of quietly pitching "a more convenient option," they did something bolder: they declared the *entire old way* obsolete, marched under a banner that literally said "No Software," and turned a product into a *movement*. That company is Salesforce, and the way it created a category is one of the great go-to-market stories ever. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "SALESFORCE — create the category, name the enemy."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Salesforce sells CRM — software to manage a company's customers and sales. But the lesson isn't about CRM. It's about *how you enter and win a market*, especially one with entrenched giants. Here's the principle: **you don't have to compete inside the existing category on the existing terms. You can create a *new* category, give it a name, cast the old way as the villain, and turn your product into a cause people want to join. Don't sell a product — start a movement.** Let me show you how Salesforce pulled that off.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE OLD WORLD — SOFTWARE AS A PAINFUL PRODUCT." A company buys software: huge upfront license, discs shipped in, months of installation on in-house servers, expensive IT to maintain, painful upgrades. Caption: "you didn't use software. you suffered it."]

EV: Picture how business software worked before Salesforce, because the pain is the whole opportunity. If a company wanted a tool to manage its sales, it *bought* the software — a massive upfront license, hundreds of thousands of dollars. Then it shipped in on discs and got *installed* on the company's own servers, a project that could take months and an army of consultants. Then you needed IT staff to maintain it, and every upgrade was its own painful project. It was slow, hugely expensive, and risky — plenty of these installs failed outright. For most businesses, especially smaller ones, powerful software was simply out of reach, buried under cost and complexity. That was the status quo everyone accepted. And an accepted, painful status quo is exactly what a category-creator feasts on.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE NEW MODEL — SOFTWARE AS A SERVICE." The discs and servers vanish. The software lives in the cloud; you just open a browser and log in. A simple monthly subscription meter ticks instead of a giant upfront check. Caption: "rent it. log in. done."]

EV: And here's Salesforce's move, which seems obvious now only because they won. What if, instead of *buying and installing* software, you just *accessed* it over the internet, through your web browser, like visiting a website? The software lives on the provider's servers in the cloud; you simply log in. No discs, no installation, no in-house servers, no IT army. And instead of a crushing upfront license, you pay a manageable *subscription* — monthly or yearly, per user. This is the model we now call **Software as a Service**, SaaS — and Salesforce was its most important pioneer. Look at what it did to the pain: install time went from months to *minutes*. Upfront cost went from a fortune to an affordable subscription. Maintenance became the provider's problem, not yours. It didn't make the software a little better. It demolished the entire cost-and-complexity wall around it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE GENIUS MOVE — 'NO SOFTWARE.'" The red circle-slash "NO SOFTWARE" logo. A staged protest outside a rival's conference with the banner. Caption: "they made the old way the enemy."]

EV: Now here's the part that turns a good product into a legendary *strategy* — the marketing, and it was audacious. Salesforce could have said, politely, "we're a more convenient alternative." Instead, it planted a flag and started a *fight*. Its rallying cry was "No Software" — a red circle with a slash through the word "software," worn as a badge, plastered everywhere. They famously staged mock protests outside competitors' events. The message was deliberately provocative: the *old way* — buying and installing traditional software — is dead, painful, obsolete, and we're here to end it. They didn't position as a product in the software category. They positioned as the *end* of it. By naming an enemy — the old model — and rallying customers to a cause, they turned buyers into *believers*. People didn't just purchase Salesforce; they *joined* it. That's the difference between selling a thing and starting a movement.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY CATEGORY CREATION WINS." A crowded old arena labeled "CRM" with giants fighting — versus a brand-new arena labeled "CLOUD / SaaS" with Salesforce standing alone at the center, defining the rules. Caption: "don't win the race. build a new track."]

EV: And here's why this is such a powerful play, strategically. When you compete *inside* an existing category, you're fighting established giants on *their* terms, on a field they built — brutal, uphill, and you're always "the alternative to" the leader. But when you *create* a new category, you get to define it, and you start as its undisputed leader because you *are* the category. Salesforce didn't try to be a better traditional-software company. It created the cloud-software category and became its face. For years, "cloud CRM" basically *meant* Salesforce. When you own the category, you own the conversation — the terms, the expectations, the default choice. New customers entering the space come to *you* first, because you're not an option in the category; you're the one who made it. Creating a category is harder than entering one. But if you pull it off, you don't just get market share. You get to be the market.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Salesforce a giant in its own right — tens of billions in revenue, a sprawling cloud empire across sales, service, marketing, data, and AI; the SaaS model it pioneered now simply how *all* software works. And, fittingly, newer players now doing to Salesforce what it once did to the old guard.]

EV: And today? Salesforce is one of the largest software companies on earth — tens of billions in revenue, a whole empire of cloud products, and an annual gathering that's one of the biggest tech events anywhere. But the real monument is this: the model it championed, SaaS, *won so completely* that it's now simply how software works. Almost every tool you use for work today — the ones in this very series — is a cloud service you log into and subscribe to. Salesforce didn't just build a big company; it *changed the default*, permanently. And here's the humbling, honest coda: the category-creator eventually becomes the incumbent, and now nimble new players use the exact same playbook — name Salesforce's complexity as the enemy, pitch a simpler way — against *it*. The movement always comes for the movement's founder. Which is the clearest proof the play works.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Don't compete in the category. Create one — and name the enemy."]

EV: So the principle to carry: when you're facing entrenched competition, ask whether you're doomed to fight on their terms — or whether you could *redefine the game*. Can you create a new category that makes the old way look obsolete? And if you can, don't be shy about it: give the new category a name, cast the painful status quo as the enemy, and rally customers to a cause, not just a purchase. People will fight for a movement they believe in far harder than they'll shop for a product. The boldest go-to-market isn't "we're better." It's "the old way is over, and here's what's next." Own the new category, and you own the future of the market.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you launch against entrenched, dominant competitors?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a standout, strategic answer. If they ask how you'd launch against dominant incumbents, don't say "we'll build more features." Say: "Competing on their terms is usually a losing fight, so I'd try to *create a new category* rather than win the old one. Salesforce didn't sell 'better CRM' — it declared traditional software obsolete, named it the enemy with 'No Software,' and pioneered the cloud model, so it led a category it defined." Then land it: "I'd frame my product as a movement against a painful status quo, not just a product in a crowded field — because owning a new category beats being 'the alternative' in an old one." That signals you think like a strategist, not just a builder.

[VISUAL: Recap: the tower of software boxes, the browser login, the "No Software" banner march, the new empty arena Salesforce stands alone in, SaaS becoming the default everywhere.]

EV: So — Salesforce. It took a painful old model — buy, install, maintain — and replaced it with something radical: software you just log into. But the masterstroke was refusing to compete inside the old category at all. It created a new one, named the old way as the villain with "No Software," and turned a product into a movement people joined. Don't fight for share in a crowded market. Build a new one, name the enemy, and lead the cause. That's how you win a game by renaming it.
