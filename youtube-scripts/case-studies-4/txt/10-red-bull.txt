EV: Here's a company that figured out a completely different way to market — so different that it barely looks like marketing at all. Red Bull sells an energy drink. But if you look at what the company actually *does* and *spends money on*, it looks far more like a *media and entertainment* empire — producing extreme-sports events, films, stunts, a Formula 1 team, even sponsoring a man jumping from the *edge of space.* The product, the can, is almost in the background. And that's the point. Red Bull realized that instead of *interrupting* great content with ads for its drink, it could *become* the source of great content people seek out — and build a brand so powerful the drink sells itself. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "RED BULL — sell meaning, not the commodity."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Red Bull is the energy drink — "gives you wings." But the lesson is about *branding and marketing strategy*, and it's one of the most powerful ideas in all of business. Here's the principle: **when your actual product is a commodity — something others can copy — you don't win by talking about the product. You win by selling a *feeling, an identity, a world* people want to belong to, and by becoming a source of content and experiences so good that people seek you out instead of skipping your ads.** Red Bull doesn't sell caffeine and sugar. It sells *energy, adventure, pushing limits* — a whole identity. Let me show you how.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE COMMODITY PROBLEM." A shelf of near-identical energy drinks; blind taste tests where nobody can tell them apart. Caption: "the liquid isn't the differentiator. it can't be."]

EV: Start with the honest truth about the product, because it's the root of the whole strategy. An energy drink is, functionally, a *commodity.* It's caffeine, sugar, some other ingredients, in a can. Competitors can make a drink that tastes similar and does the same thing — there's nothing about the *liquid itself* that's defensible or truly unique. In blind taste tests, people often can't even tell these drinks apart. So if Red Bull tried to compete on the *product* — "ours tastes slightly better, has slightly different ingredients" — it would be a losing, boring race to the bottom on flavor and price, against endless copycats. When your product is a commodity, competing on the product is a dead end. You need to make people choose you for a reason that has *nothing to do with the liquid.* And that reason is the *brand* — the meaning, the identity, the feeling attached to it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MOVE — BECOME A MEDIA COMPANY." Instead of ads interrupting content, Red Bull *produces* the content: extreme sports films, live events, athlete sponsorships, a space jump watched by millions. The can appears only in the corner. Caption: "don't buy ad space. BE the show."]

EV: And here's Red Bull's radical move. Most companies market by *interrupting* content — you're watching a video or a game, and an ad for a product barges in, and you wait to skip it. Red Bull flipped that entirely. Instead of *buying* ad space inside other people's content, it decided to *create its own content* — content so thrilling that people *seek it out and love it.* It produces extreme-sports films and competitions, sponsors daredevil athletes, runs its own events, fields a championship-winning Formula 1 team, and famously funded a man free-falling from the *stratosphere*, live, watched by millions around the world. All of it embodies the brand's spirit — energy, adrenaline, pushing human limits. The drink is barely *in* most of it. Red Bull essentially became a *media company that happens to sell a drink.* And people don't skip Red Bull's content — they *search for it*, share it, love it. That's the dream: marketing people actively *want* to consume.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THIS IS GENIUS — SELL THE IDENTITY." A young person watching a Red Bull stunt feels the energy and adventure — then reaches for the can not for the taste, but to feel part of *that world.* Caption: "you're not buying a drink. you're buying who you want to be."]

EV: And here's *why* this works so profoundly — it's about what people are *actually* buying. When someone picks up a Red Bull, in a real sense they're not buying caffeine. They're buying into an *identity and a feeling*: energy, adventure, excitement, being the kind of person who pushes limits. All that incredible content builds a powerful *association* in your mind — Red Bull *means* adrenaline, action, living boldly. So choosing Red Bull becomes a way to feel connected to that world, to signal something about who you are. And *that* is something competitors genuinely can't copy, because they can copy the liquid but not the *meaning* Red Bull spent decades building. This is the deepest lesson of branding: for a commodity, you're not really competing on the product — you're competing on the *identity and emotion* attached to it. Red Bull sells a *feeling*, and the drink is just how you buy a sip of it.

[VISUAL: Header: "CONTENT BEATS ADS." An ad: costs money, interrupts, gets skipped, disappears. A piece of great content: gets sought out, shared, lives forever, builds the brand every time it's watched. Caption: "an ad is a cost. great content is an asset."]

EV: And let's name *why* this approach is structurally superior to normal advertising, because it's a lesson any product or marketing person can use. A traditional ad is an *interruption* — people's instinct is to skip or ignore it, you have to keep paying to run it, and the moment you stop paying, it vanishes. But a great piece of *content* — an incredible film, an unforgettable stunt — is something people *choose* to watch, *share* with friends, and *remember.* It doesn't feel like marketing, so people don't resist it; it spreads on its own because it's genuinely good; and it keeps building the brand every single time someone watches it, for years. An ad is a *cost* that disappears. Great content is an *asset* that compounds. Red Bull understood that in a world where everyone's drowning in ads and getting better at ignoring them, the way to win attention isn't to shout louder — it's to make something people actually *want* to watch, with your brand's spirit baked into it. Be the show, not the ad break.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Red Bull a global giant — billions of cans sold a year across the world, the dominant energy-drink brand, a Formula 1 championship team, a sprawling media arm producing content watched by millions. The "media company that sells a drink" model studied and copied everywhere.]

EV: And today? Red Bull sells *billions* of cans a year and is the dominant energy-drink brand on the planet — in a category it largely *created* and still defines, decades on. It has a championship Formula 1 team, a huge roster of sponsored athletes, and a genuine media arm producing films, shows, and events consumed by millions who often don't even think of it as advertising. And the *strategy* has become a model studied and imitated across industries — "content marketing," "brand as media," companies acting like studios. Red Bull proved you can take a plain commodity and, through pure brand-building and content, turn it into a global icon people pay a premium for and feel loyal to. Not because of the liquid. Because of the *world* it built around it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "When the product is a commodity, sell the meaning. Be the content, not the ad."]

EV: So the principle to carry: when your product is a commodity — something competitors can copy — stop trying to win on the product itself; that's a dead-end race. Win on the *brand* — the identity, the feeling, the world people want to belong to — because that's the thing rivals *can't* copy. And to build that brand, don't just run ads people skip: become a *source of content and experiences* people actively seek out, with your spirit woven in, so your marketing is something they *enjoy* rather than endure. Ask: "beyond the functional product, what *feeling or identity* am I really selling — and could I create content so good that people come to *me* instead of me interrupting *them*?" Sell the meaning, and the commodity sells itself.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How do you differentiate a commodity product?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a sharp branding answer. If they ask how to differentiate a commodity, don't say "improve the product or cut the price." Say: "If the product's a commodity, competing on the product is a losing race — so I'd compete on *brand and meaning* instead. Red Bull sells a commodity drink, but it really sells an *identity* — energy and adventure — by becoming a media company that produces content people seek out, from extreme sports to a jump from space." Then land it: "I'd build an emotional identity competitors can't copy, and market through content people *want* to watch rather than ads they skip — because the meaning is the moat, not the liquid." That shows you understand brand as a genuine competitive advantage.

[VISUAL: Recap: the space jump, the F1 car, the wingsuit, the tiny can in the corner, a young fan reaching for the drink to feel part of that world, the content spreading everywhere.]

EV: So — Red Bull. Selling a commodity drink, it refused to compete on the liquid. It built a *brand* — an identity of energy and adventure — by becoming a media company that produces content people seek out instead of ads they skip, and sold a *feeling* competitors could never copy. When the product's a commodity, sell the meaning, and be the show, not the ad break. That's how an energy drink became a global icon.
