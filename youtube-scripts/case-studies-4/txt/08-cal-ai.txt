EV: Here's a story that rewrites what you think you need to win. A *teenager* built a calorie-tracking app — point your phone at your food, and AI estimates the calories and nutrition. Useful, sure. But here's the thing: the core technology wasn't some unbeatable secret. Other apps did similar things. What made Cal AI explode to millions of users and tens of millions in revenue — before the founder turned twenty — wasn't a technical moat. It was a *distribution* masterclass: he realized that getting the app in front of the right people, at scale, through creators, *was the whole game.* He paid and partnered with hundreds of influencers to flood social media with it. The lesson: in a crowded market, how you *reach* people can matter more than what's under the hood. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "CAL AI — distribution IS the product."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Cal AI is a health app that uses your phone camera and AI to log what you eat, built by a young founder, Zach Yadegari, who'd been coding since childhood and had already sold a project as a teen. But the lesson isn't "AI calorie counter." It's one of the most important and *underrated* truths in building products. Here's the principle: **a great product that nobody discovers loses to a decent product everybody sees. Distribution — how you get your product in front of people — is not an afterthought to the product. It can *be* the strategy, and in a crowded market it's often the real moat.** Let me show you how a teenager out-distributed everyone.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BUILDER'S TRAP." A beautiful app sitting in the app store with zero downloads, while the founder waits for people to "find it." Caption: "build it and they will come… is a lie."]

EV: Let's start with the trap almost every builder falls into, because it's the setup for why Cal AI stands out. Most people who build a product — especially technical founders — pour *everything* into making the product great and then quietly assume that if it's good enough, people will just *find* it. Build it and they will come. But that is mostly a myth, and a dangerous one. The app stores and the internet are *flooded* — there are countless calorie-tracking apps, countless of everything. Being good is not enough, because nobody can choose you if nobody *knows you exist.* A brilliant product with no distribution is a tree falling in an empty forest. The hard, humbling truth is that the best product does *not* automatically win — the best-*distributed* product very often does. And most builders under-invest wildly in distribution, treating it as "marketing we'll figure out later." Cal AI's founder treated it as the main event.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE INSIGHT — GO WHERE ATTENTION ALREADY IS." Fitness and food creators on short-form video, each with a devoted, perfectly-matched audience hanging on their every recommendation. Caption: "the right messenger beats the loudest ad."]

EV: And here's the insight that made the difference. Zach understood *where the attention of his exact audience already lived*: with creators. Think about who wants a calorie-tracking app — people into fitness, dieting, health, eating better. And where do those people spend their time and get their recommendations? Watching fitness and food *influencers* on short-form video. Those creators had already done the hard work — they'd assembled large, engaged, *perfectly-matched* audiences who *trust their recommendations.* So instead of shouting into the void with generic ads, Cal AI went straight to those creators. A trusted creator showing their audience "here's the app I use to track my food" is worth a hundred faceless banner ads, because it comes with built-in trust and it's aimed at *exactly* the right people. Don't try to build an audience from scratch. Borrow the trust of people who already have the one you want.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MACHINE — 250 INFLUENCERS ON RETAINER." The founder personally DMing thousands of creators; a system forms — hundreds of influencers posting on retainer, a steady drumbeat of videos, downloads flowing in. Half the tiny team focused on marketing. Caption: "they turned influencer marketing into a factory."]

EV: Now here's where it goes from "nice idea" to "relentless execution," and this is the part to really study. Zach didn't just casually ask a few influencers. He turned it into a *machine.* He personally direct-messaged *thousands* of creators, testing and refining the pitch, treating outreach as a numbers game to be optimized. The result: Cal AI had hundreds of influencers — reportedly around *250 creators on retainer*, posting about the app regularly, with the biggest ones earning serious money per post. Roughly *half* of the small team was focused on marketing and managing those creator relationships. Read that again: a tiny company, and half of it was dedicated not to building the product but to *distributing* it. They built a content *engine* — a constant, high-volume stream of creators showing the app to their audiences, driving a steady flood of downloads. That's the difference between "we should do influencer marketing" and "influencer marketing *is our company.*"

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CATCH — DISTRIBUTION GETS THEM IN, PRODUCT KEEPS THEM." A flood of downloads pours into a bucket; if the product is leaky (bad), they drain right out; if it's solid (good enough), they stay and pay. Caption: "a great funnel into a bad product just loses money faster."]

EV: Let me be honest about the nuance, because it's where people misread this story. "Distribution can matter more than the product" does *not* mean the product doesn't matter. Here's the real relationship: distribution gets people *in the door* — but the product is what makes them *stay and pay.* If Cal AI had been garbage, all those downloads would have churned straight out, and he'd just have been *paying to lose users faster* — a leaky bucket. The app had to be genuinely good *enough* — easy, actually useful, worth keeping — for the distribution to convert into real retained, paying customers. So the right way to hold it is: you need a product that's *good enough to retain*, and distribution *good enough to win.* Most builders obsess over making the product a ten and leave distribution at a two. Cal AI made the product a solid *eight* and the distribution a *ten* — and in a crowded market, that combination crushes a perfect product nobody can find.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Cal AI past 15 million downloads and tens of millions in annual revenue, built by a founder barely out of his teens with a tiny team; acquired by a major health-app company. Proof that in the modern era, distribution leverage lets a handful of people reach millions.]

EV: And where did it land? Cal AI blew past *fifteen million downloads* and *tens of millions of dollars* in annual revenue — built by a founder barely out of his teens, with a team you could count on two hands, and it was ultimately *acquired* by a major established health-app company. Sit with how wild that is: a teenager, a small team, and a product whose core tech wasn't unique, beat far more established players — because he understood that in a crowded market, *distribution is the battlefield*, and he out-executed everyone on it. It's a defining lesson of the modern era: the tools to *build* are now cheap and everywhere, which means the product is rarely the scarce thing. *Attention* is scarce. And whoever masters distribution — getting the product in front of the right people at scale — wins, even against bigger, more technical competitors.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Build a product good enough to keep them. Build distribution good enough to win."]

EV: So the principle to carry: stop assuming a great product sells itself — it doesn't. Treat *distribution* as a first-class part of your strategy, maybe *the* strategy, especially in a crowded market where being good isn't rare. Figure out where your exact audience's attention already lives — which creators, platforms, and communities they already trust — and go borrow that trust instead of building an audience from zero. And build distribution into a *system*, not a one-off. But keep the product good enough to retain the people distribution brings, or you're just filling a leaky bucket. Ask, as early as you ask about features: "how, specifically, will the right people *discover* this — and is that plan as good as the product?"

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Your product is great but growth is flat. What's your move?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a very current, credible answer. If they say "the product's great but growth is flat," don't say "add more features." Say: "Flat growth with a good product is usually a *distribution* problem, not a product one. I'd find where my exact audience's attention already lives — often specific creators or communities they trust — and build a repeatable distribution engine there, the way Cal AI reached millions through hundreds of influencers rather than ads." Then show balance: "I'd make sure the product retains the users that brings in — distribution fills the bucket, the product has to hold them." That signals you understand that in a crowded market, distribution is often the real moat.

[VISUAL: Recap: the empty app store, the matched creator audiences, the 250-influencer machine, the downloads flooding in, the bucket that holds because the product is good enough.]

EV: So — Cal AI. A teenager with a good-enough app beat established players by understanding what most builders miss: distribution isn't an afterthought, it's the strategy. He went where his audience's attention already lived — creators — and turned influencer marketing into a relentless machine, while keeping the product solid enough to retain everyone it brought in. Don't just build it and hope. Engineer how the right people discover it. In a crowded world, distribution is the moat.
