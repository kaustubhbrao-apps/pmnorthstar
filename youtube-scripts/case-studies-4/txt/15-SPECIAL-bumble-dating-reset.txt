But in 2026, the mood shifted hard. Growth stalled, users got *tired* — "dating app fatigue" became a real, widely-felt thing — and the number of people willing to *pay* started *falling.* And then Bumble, one of the biggest names in the space, agreed to be acquired for around *1.3 billion dollars.* The deal itself matters, but the bigger story is what it *signals*: a whole category moving from booming growth into maturity and decline. And recognizing that moment — the top of the curve — is one of the most important and hardest skills in business. Let's get into it.

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar — special episode, because this is a revealing moment and the lesson is one too many people learn too late. The headline: Bumble agreed to a roughly 1.3-billion-dollar acquisition, against a backdrop of the whole online-dating category slowing — fewer new users, fatigued existing ones, declining paying subscribers across the board. Here's the principle I want you to take: **every product and market follows a lifecycle — an S-curve — that rises, plateaus, and eventually declines. The crucial, hard skill is recognizing *when* you've hit the top, and reinventing *before* the decline, not after.** Let me break it down.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE S-CURVE." A curve: slow start → explosive growth → plateau → decline. A marker sliding from the steep growth part toward the bend at the top. Caption: "slow… then fast… then flat… then down."]

EV: First, the shape every product lives inside, because it's the whole frame. Think of a product or market's life as an *S-curve.* At the start, slow growth — a new thing, few users, figuring it out. Then, if it catches on, *explosive* growth — the steep, thrilling part where everyone's talking about it, users pour in, it feels unstoppable. Then, inevitably, the curve *flattens* — a plateau, where most people who'll ever adopt it already have, and growth slows to a crawl. And eventually, often, *decline* — the thing goes out of fashion, gets replaced, or people simply tire of it, and usage actually *falls.* Online dating rode that curve beautifully — the explosive-growth years were real and huge. But every S-curve bends. And dating apps appear to have hit the *plateau-tipping-toward-decline* part: market mostly saturated, users fatigued, fewer willing to pay. The exciting growth phase is *over*, and a different, harder phase has begun.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY DATING APPS HIT THE WALL." Three forces: the market is saturated (most singles already tried them), fatigue (swiping feels like a tiring chore, not fun), and the awkward incentive (an app that 'succeeds' removes its own paying user). Caption: "the model has a built-in ceiling."]

EV: And it's worth understanding *why* dating apps specifically hit the wall, because the reasons are a great product lesson in themselves. Three forces. One: *saturation* — pretty much every single person who wanted to try online dating already has, so there's almost no one *new* to acquire; growth from new users basically stops. Two: *fatigue* — endless swiping, which felt exciting and novel at first, started to feel like a tiring, disheartening *chore*; the experience itself wore people down, and burned-out users stop paying or leave. And three — the quietly brutal one — dating apps have an *awkward built-in incentive problem*: if the app *actually works* and you find a partner, you *leave* and stop paying. The product's success *removes* its own customer. So the model has a ceiling baked right into it. Put saturation, fatigue, and that perverse incentive together, and you get a category that was *always* going to hit a wall once the easy growth ran out. The wall wasn't a surprise. It was structural.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE REAL LESSON — REINVENT BEFORE THE DECLINE." A company near the top of the curve launching a *second* new S-curve upward, *before* the first one drops. Versus a company that waits, and rides the first curve down. Caption: "jump to the next curve while you're still high."]

EV: And here's the crucial, hard lesson for *anyone* building anything — the thing the smartest companies do and most companies miss. Because every S-curve eventually bends down, you can't just ride one product's growth forever and assume it'll last. The winning move is to *recognize when you're approaching the top* and *reinvent yourself before the decline* — launch a *new* S-curve while you're still riding high on the old one. Find a new product, a new model, a new source of growth, *before* the current one stalls. The companies that endure for decades are the ones that keep *jumping to new curves* — reinventing while they're still strong, not waiting until they're desperate. The painfully common mistake is the opposite: assume the good times will last forever, miss the plateau, and only try to change *after* decline has set in — when you're weaker, have less money, and fewer options. For the dating category, the hard question is exactly this: can the players reinvent the experience — new models, genuinely better ways to connect — and start a *new* curve? Or did they wait too long, and now the only move left is to consolidate and sell, like Bumble? Reinvent near the top, or get sold near the bottom.

EV: But let me be honest about the nuance, because "the category is declining" is too simple. First, a mature or plateaued business is *not* worthless — dating apps still have millions of users and make real, steady money; "not growing fast" is very different from "dead," and plenty of great businesses live profitably on a plateau for years. Second, S-curves *can* be *restarted*: a genuinely new innovation — a smarter, AI-assisted way to match, a new format that fixes the fatigue, a different model that doesn't punish success — could kick off a *fresh* growth curve and revive the whole category. Decline isn't always permanent; sometimes it's just a signal that the *current* approach is tapped out and it's time for the *next* one. And third, the Bumble sale isn't simply "failure" — consolidating in a maturing market can be a rational, even smart, move (remember the last episode). So the honest read: the easy-growth era of dating apps is over, the warning signs are real, but whether this is terminal decline or the pause before a reinvention depends entirely on whether someone starts the next curve. A plateau is a warning, not a death certificate.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Nothing grows forever. Spot the top of the curve — and reinvent before the fall."]

EV: So the principle to carry from this one: internalize that *nothing grows forever.* Every product and market rides an S-curve that eventually flattens and can decline — and the single hardest, most valuable skill is *honestly recognizing when you're near the top*, rather than assuming the boom will last. When you see the plateau coming — growth slowing, users fatiguing, the model's ceiling showing — that's the signal to *reinvent*: start a new growth curve *while you're still strong*, instead of riding the old one down and scrambling only once you're weak. Dating apps rode a huge curve and are now hitting its bend; the ones that survive and thrive will be the ones that start a genuinely new curve in time. Ask, even when things are going great: "where am I on the S-curve — and what's my *next* curve, before this one bends?"

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Your flagship product's growth is slowing. What do you do?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a mature, forward-looking answer. If they say "your flagship's growth is slowing," don't say "we'll optimize the funnel and push harder." Say: "Slowing growth usually means the product is hitting the plateau of its S-curve — like online dating right now, with saturation and user fatigue driving the whole category into a reset, and players like Bumble getting acquired." Then make the strategic move: "So rather than just squeezing the maturing product, I'd focus on starting the *next* curve — a genuinely new product, model, or experience to reignite growth — and do it *while we're still strong*, not after decline sets in. The companies that last are the ones that keep jumping to new curves." That shows you think in lifecycles and plan for reinvention, not just optimization.

The lesson is universal: nothing grows forever. The skill that separates the companies that last is spotting the top of the curve and reinventing *before* the fall — jumping to the next curve while you're still high. Watch your S-curve. And always be building the next one.
