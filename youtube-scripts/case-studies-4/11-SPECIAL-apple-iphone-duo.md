They were exciting, futuristic… and early on, kind of a mess: creased displays, fragile hinges, bugs, high prices for a rough experience. And through all of it, the biggest phone company in the world — Apple — did *nothing.* No foldable. Critics said it was falling behind. Then, in October 2026, Apple finally unveiled its first foldable, the iPhone Duo — starting around two thousand dollars — and the years of waiting turn out to be the whole strategy. EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar — special episode, because this just dropped and the lesson behind it is bigger than one phone. Here's the principle I want you to take: **being first to a new category is often *not* the advantage people think it is. There's enormous power in being a *well-timed follower* — letting others prove the category and make the early mistakes, then entering when the technology is finally ready and you can deliver the *best* version, at a premium.** Apple just ran that playbook, again. Let me break it down.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE COST OF BEING FIRST." Early foldables: creased screens, hinge failures, buggy software, sky-high prices, frustrated early adopters doing the market's R&D the hard way. Caption: "pioneers take the arrows."]

EV: First, let's be honest about what the *pioneers* of foldable phones actually went through — because this is the part people forget. The early foldables were genuinely impressive *attempts*, but the first generations had real problems: visible creases down the middle of the screen, hinges that wore out or broke, software that didn't quite know what to do with a folding display, and premium prices for an experience that was still rough. The companies that went first spent years and fortunes *figuring out the hard problems* — the engineering, the durability, how the software should even work — and their early customers were, effectively, beta testers. That's the hidden tax of being first: you do the expensive R&D, you take the arrows, you train the market, and you absorb the reputation hit for the rough early versions. Being first *looks* like leadership. Often it's just *paying for everyone else's education.*

[VISUAL: Header: "THE WELL-TIMED ENTRANCE." Apple steps in only now — the folding tech mature, the problems mostly solved — with a thin, polished iPhone Duo and 'Split View,' plugged straight into the iPhone ecosystem. Caption: "enter when it's ready, and make it the best."]

EV: And here's Apple's move, which is the opposite of first — it's *well-timed.* Apple waited until the foldable technology was mature enough to do it *right*: thinner, more polished, more durable, with the software actually built for the folding experience — things like Split View, running two apps side by side on the bigger screen. It let *others* spend years solving the hard engineering and proving people even *want* a foldable, and then walked in with a more refined version — and, crucially, plugged it straight into the thing only Apple has: a massive, loyal ecosystem of users whose apps, photos, watch, and habits already live in Apple's world. So the iPhone Duo isn't a risky experiment for Apple. It's a mature product, entering a *proven* category, for an audience already waiting. That's the entire advantage of patience: you skip the painful pioneering phase and show up when you can win.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY LATE-BUT-BEST BEATS FIRST — WHEN YOU CAN." A chart: first-movers often lose to a better-timed, better-resourced follower who learns from their mistakes and out-executes. Caption: "first isn't the prize. BEST-at-the-right-moment is."]

EV: Now let's name *why* this works, because it's a genuine strategic principle, not just "Apple can do what it wants." In lots of markets, the first mover does *not* ultimately win. A well-timed follower can watch the pioneers, learn exactly what works and what doesn't, skip their expensive mistakes, and arrive with a better product right as the market is ready to go mainstream — and out-execute everyone. History is full of this: the famous version of a product is frequently *not* the first one, but the one that showed up later and got it *right.* Apple itself is the master of this — it wasn't first to the smartphone, the music player, the smartwatch, or the tablet, yet it defined and dominated each, because it entered when it could deliver the best, most polished version to an ecosystem that trusted it. "First" is a vanity metric. "Best, at the moment the market is ready" is the one that actually wins — *if* you have the patience to wait and the strength to execute when you move.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE PREMIUM PLAY + THE FLAGSHIP HALO." A ~$1,999 price tag; a small group of enthusiasts paying top dollar; and the 'wow' of the foldable lifting the shine on the *whole* iPhone line. Caption: "the halo product sells the ordinary ones too."]

EV: And notice the *pricing*, because it's deliberate. Starting around two thousand dollars and climbing past three, the iPhone Duo is a *premium*, aspirational product — Apple isn't trying to sell one to everyone. And it does *two* jobs at that price. One: it earns high margins from the enthusiasts and early adopters who'll happily pay top dollar for the newest, most impressive thing. Two — and this is subtle — it acts as a *halo* for the entire iPhone lineup. A jaw-dropping flagship makes the *whole brand* feel more innovative and desirable, which lifts sales of the regular, cheaper iPhones that most people actually buy. The foldable doesn't have to sell in huge numbers to be a win; it has to make Apple look like the leader in phone innovation again, and re-rate the desirability of the entire franchise. The flashy product at the top pulls up everything beneath it. That's what a flagship is *for.*

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CATCH — LATE ONLY WINS IF YOU EXECUTE." A reminder: waiting is only smart if the final product is genuinely better and the moment is right; wait too long, or ship something merely 'fine,' and the strategy fails. Question marks over durability and real-world demand. Caption: "patience isn't a strategy by itself."]

EV: But let me be honest about the risks, because "be a late follower" is *not* automatically safe. Waiting only wins if two things are true. First, your late product has to be *genuinely better* — if Apple's foldable is merely *fine*, or has its own first-generation problems (and folding phones are genuinely hard to get durable and crease-free), then waiting years bought nothing and the pioneers got a long head start for free. Second, the *demand* has to be real — the bet assumes enough people actually want a foldable at a premium price, and if foldables turn out to be a niche most people don't need, even a perfect one won't move the needle. And waiting has a cost: competitors got years to build loyalty and learn. So "late but best" is powerful, but only when paired with genuinely superior execution and good timing. Patience without a better product isn't strategy. It's just being slow.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Don't rush to be first. Time your entry — and make it the best."]

EV: So the principle to carry from this one: don't assume you have to be *first* to win a new category — and don't panic when competitors beat you to one. There's real power in being a *well-timed follower*: let others prove the market and solve the hard early problems, then enter when the technology's ready and you can deliver the *best* version — ideally into an audience that already trusts you. Apple just did it again with the iPhone Duo. Ask of your own market: "is being first here actually an advantage, or am I better off letting others take the arrows and entering when I can win?" Sometimes the smartest move in product isn't to race. It's to *wait, watch, and then out-execute.*

EV: And in an interview, this is a calm, senior answer. If they say "a competitor beat us to a new category," don't panic and say "we need to rush ours out." Say: "Being first isn't always the advantage — first movers often do the expensive R&D and take the arrows, and a well-timed follower can learn from their mistakes and ship a better version when the market's actually ready. Apple just entered foldables *years* after rivals with the iPhone Duo, and that patience is the strategy." Then add the discipline: "But late only wins if our version is genuinely better and the demand is real — so I'd use the time to build something clearly superior, not just a catch-up clone." That shows you understand timing as a real strategic lever.
