[VISUAL: Header: "MOSCOW — THE 4 BUCKETS." Four buckets: Must, Should, Could, Won't.]

EV: First, MoSCoW. The name is just the four bucket labels with some O's thrown in to make it pronounceable — Must, Should, Could, and Won't. Instead of ranking everything in a precise order, you sort each item into one of four priority buckets. It's fast, it's intuitive, and it's especially great when you're scoping *what goes into a specific release* and need to align a team quickly. Let's define the buckets.

[VISUAL: Each bucket enlarges as EV describes it.]

EV: Bucket one — *Must have*. Non-negotiable. If these don't ship, the release *fails* or can't launch at all. The login has to work; the payment has to process. No Musts, no launch. Bucket two — *Should have*. Important and painful to leave out, but the release could *technically* survive without them for now. You really want these, but they're not launch-blockers. Bucket three — *Could have*. Nice-to-haves. Desirable if there's time and capacity, but easily dropped without much harm. These are the first things to cut when the deadline looms. And bucket four — *Won't have* — and this is the clever, underused one. These are things you're *explicitly deciding not to do this time*. Not "maybe later" vaguely — a clear, out-loud "not now." Naming your Won'ts prevents scope creep and sets expectations. It's saying no, formally.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THE 'WON'T' BUCKET IS SECRETLY THE BEST." The Won't bucket glows; scope-creep monsters bounce off it.]

EV: And let me zoom in on that Won't bucket, because it's secretly the most valuable one. Most people prioritize by only talking about what they *will* do. But scope creep — the silent killer of every project — happens because nobody clearly said what they *won't* do, so things keep sneaking in. By explicitly listing your Won'ts, you build a wall against that. In an interview, saying "and I'd put these three firmly in the Won't-have bucket for this release, because..." shows discipline and the courage to say no — the exact trait we keep coming back to. The Won't bucket is where prioritization becomes *real*, because it's where you actually give something up.

[VISUAL: Header: "COST OF DELAY — THE HIDDEN PRICE." A valuable feature sitting un-built while money/users leak away each week.]

EV: Second tool — cost of delay. And this one reframes prioritization in a way most people miss. Every framework so far asks "how valuable is this feature?" Cost of delay asks a different, sharper question: "how much does it cost us *every week we DON'T have it*?" Because a feature isn't just worth its value — it's worth its value *times time*. If a feature would earn a hundred thousand dollars a month, then every month you delay it, you're *losing* a hundred thousand dollars. That loss is invisible on most prioritization lists, but it's real money walking out the door. Cost of delay drags that hidden cost into the light.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY IT CHANGES YOUR ORDER." Two features: one high-value-but-patient, one lower-value-but-urgent; order flips.]

EV: And here's why cost of delay can *flip* your priorities. Imagine two features. Feature A is super valuable, but its value is *patient* — it'll be just as valuable next quarter. Feature B is a bit less valuable overall, but it's tied to a seasonal window, or a competitor's about to launch the same thing — so every week you wait, you lose a chunk of its value *permanently*. Pure value says build A first. But cost of delay says — wait — B is *bleeding value right now*, and A can wait without losing anything. So do B first. This is huge for anything time-sensitive: seasonal features, competitive responses, compliance deadlines. Cost of delay is what tells you that *urgency* is its own form of value. Sometimes the less-valuable thing should go first simply because waiting on it is so expensive.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CLASSES OF URGENCY." Three patterns: steady loss, deadline cliff, seasonal peak.]

EV: And a sharp detail — cost of delay comes in different *shapes*, and naming them impresses. Some features lose value *steadily* — a flat amount every week forever, like our hundred-thousand-a-month example. Some have a *deadline cliff* — they're hugely valuable right up until a specific date, then nearly worthless, like a feature for a holiday or a regulatory deadline. And some are *seasonal* — valuable only during a window that comes and goes. Knowing the shape tells you *how hard* to rush. A deadline-cliff feature with the deadline next week? Drop everything. A steady-loss feature? Important, but you've got flexibility. Matching your urgency to the *shape* of the delay cost is genuinely senior thinking.

[VISUAL: Header: "PUTTING IT TOGETHER." MoSCoW buckets + cost-of-delay urgency combining.]

EV: So how do these two play together, and with everything else? MoSCoW gives you clean buckets for *what's in versus out* of a release. Cost of delay adjusts the *order* within your plan by surfacing urgency. Use them alongside value-effort and Kano: the grid finds your quick wins, Kano tells you the *type* of value, MoSCoW scopes the release, and cost of delay sequences by urgency. They're not competitors — they're different lenses, and a great PM reaches for whichever one the situation needs. In an interview, name the tool that fits: "Let me bucket these with MoSCoW... and this one jumps up because its cost of delay is high — the competitor launches next month."

Cost of delay: ask not just "how valuable" but "how much do we lose every week we wait?" — and let urgency, in all its shapes, reorder your plan. Together they handle scope and sequencing, rounding out your whole prioritization toolkit.
