Let me set the scene. We're a PM at a growing food-delivery app, planning next quarter. Here's our chaotic backlog of six items.

[VISUAL: Six sticky notes appear and get read out, each with a tag of who wants it.]

EV: Item one — fix a bug where the app crashes during payment on older Android phones. Item two — a brand-new loyalty rewards program, which the CEO is excited about. Item three — redesign the app's color scheme, which the design team really wants. Item four — add support for a popular local payment method that users keep requesting. Item five — a full rewrite of our backend to a trendy new framework, which some engineers want. And item six — a referral feature, which the growth team is pushing, timed before a competitor launches theirs. Six items, four different groups lobbying. Let's bring order.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 1 · CLARIFY THE GOAL." A north-star anchor drops.]

EV: Step one — before I rank anything, I anchor on the goal, because priorities only make sense relative to a goal. "What are we trying to achieve this quarter? Let's say our North Star is active orders per month, and the company's focus is *growth* while keeping the experience solid." Good. Now every item gets judged against *that*. Watch how the goal instantly starts sorting things.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 2 · THE VALUE-EFFORT PASS." The six notes drop onto a 2x2 grid.]

EV: Step two — a fast value-effort pass to get rough positions. The payment crash bug — massive value, people literally can't pay, that's lost orders *right now* — and low effort, it's a known bug. Top-left, screaming quick win. The local payment method — high value, users keep asking and it unlocks orders from people who can't currently pay — medium effort. High-value, worth it. The referral feature — high value for growth, medium effort. The loyalty program — potentially high value, but *high* effort, it's a big build. Top-right, a big bet. The color redesign — low value for our growth goal, honestly users won't order more because of new colors — low-ish effort. Bottom-left. And the backend rewrite — near-zero *user* value, users won't notice, and *enormous* effort. Bottom-right, the money pit.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 3 · COST OF DELAY BREAKS THE TIE." The referral note starts leaking value with a ticking clock.]

EV: Step three — now I've got several good items, so I use cost of delay to sequence them, and this is where it gets interesting. Look at the referral feature. On pure value it's strong but not clearly first. *But* — it's timed before a competitor launches theirs. That's a deadline-cliff cost of delay. Every week we wait, we lose the chance to lock in users *before* the competitor does — value that may never come back. So cost of delay *bumps the referral feature up* in urgency, above things that are equally valuable but *patient*. The payment bug and the local payment method are also urgent — they're bleeding orders every single day. The loyalty program? Valuable, but patient — it'll be just as valuable next quarter. So it can wait.

EV: Quick one — if watching this come together is helping, subscribe to pmnorthstar so you can run it yourself. Now, the Kano and saying-no layers.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 4 · KANO & SAYING NO." Items get tagged basic/performance/delighter; two get a "NO" stamp.]

EV: Step four — a quick Kano and no-saying pass. The payment crash? That's a *basic expectation* we're currently *failing* — paying has to work, full stop. Failing a basic is actively hurting us, which confirms it's priority number one. The loyalty and referral features lean toward *performance and delighter* territory — real growth drivers. Now the hard part — saying no. The color redesign? I'd tell the design team: "I hear you, and I love that you care about craft — but against a growth goal, a color change won't move orders, so it's a *Could-have*, not this quarter. Let's revisit when we have slack." And the backend rewrite? A firmer no to the engineers: "Totally understand the appeal, but it's huge effort with no user-facing value right now — that's a *Won't-have* this quarter. If it starts blocking us technically, we'll reassess with data." Notice — I said no *with reasons and trade-offs*, and left doors open.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE FINAL RANKED PLAN." The six notes snap into a clean ordered list.]

EV: So — pulling it all together, here's my prioritized plan, and I'd present it exactly like this. Number one — fix the payment crash. It's a failing basic bleeding orders daily: highest value, lowest effort, urgent. Number two — add the local payment method. High value, unlocks new orders, users are asking. Number three — the referral feature. Strong growth value, and critically, high cost of delay with the competitor looming. Number four — the loyalty program. Genuinely valuable, but a big bet and *patient*, so it's planned but later. And explicitly *not* this quarter — the color redesign, parked as a Could-have, and the backend rewrite, a firm Won't-have. Clean, ranked, and every call defended.

[VISUAL: All six items resolved — four ordered, two parked — with reasons glowing beside each.]

EV: And look at what just happened. I turned six items and four lobbying groups into a clear, confident, *defensible* plan. And I never once said "because I feel like it." Every decision traced to the goal, to value versus effort, to cost of delay, to Kano, or to an honest no. If the CEO pushes back on loyalty being fourth, I can *show* them the trade-off. That's the difference between a PM who has opinions and a PM who has *priorities*.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE RHYTHM TO INTERNALIZE." The step sequence pulses.]

EV: Here's the rhythm to internalize. Anchor on the goal first — it does half the sorting for you. Do a fast value-effort pass for rough positions. Use cost of delay to sequence the urgent against the patient. Layer Kano for *why* things matter. And have the courage to explicitly park and reject things, with reasons. Run that sequence on any backlog and you'll produce a plan you can defend to anyone in the room.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHEN THE CEO PUSHES BACK." A CEO figure crosses arms; EV stays calm with the trade-off.]

EV: And let me prep you for the pushback, because it *always* comes — especially from the person whose pet feature you ranked low. Say the CEO frowns: "I don't love that the loyalty program is only fourth. I really want that this quarter." Here's where everything we learned pays off — you don't cave, and you don't dig in stubbornly. You go to the trade-off and the goal. "Totally hear you — and I'm excited about loyalty too. Here's my thinking: loyalty is a big, high-effort build, and critically, its value is *patient* — it'll be just as powerful next quarter. Meanwhile the payment bug is bleeding orders *today*, and the referral feature has a hard cost of delay because our competitor launches theirs next month. So if we pull the team onto loyalty now, we lose that referral window *permanently* and keep bleeding orders. If you feel loyalty genuinely beats locking in users before the competitor, I'm open — but that's the trade-off we'd be making. What do you think?" Notice — I defended my ranking with *reasoning and trade-offs*, I stayed warm and collaborative, and I left the final call as a shared decision. That's how you hold your ground with a powerful stakeholder without either caving or fighting. The frameworks gave me the *ammunition* to make the trade-off visible — and that's what wins the room.
