EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Last video we did RICE, which is powerful but takes some number-crunching. Today's tool is RICE's fast, scrappy cousin — the value-versus-effort grid. It's the single most useful prioritization tool in an interview, because you can sketch it instantly, talk through it out loud, and reach a confident decision in under a minute. Let me show you how it works and how to use it like a pro.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TWO AXES." A grid: vertical axis "VALUE" (low to high), horizontal axis "EFFORT" (low to high).]

EV: The whole tool is just two axes. The vertical axis is Value — how much good does this do, for users and the business? Low at the bottom, high at the top. The horizontal axis is Effort — how much work to build it? Low on the left, high on the right. That's it. Every idea you're considering gets a rough spot on this grid based on those two judgments. You don't need exact numbers — "high value, low effort" is enough to place a dot. And once your ideas are placed, the four corners tell you exactly what to do.

[VISUAL: The four quadrants light up and label themselves as EV names them, with example dots.]

EV: Let's walk the four quadrants, because each one is a different *verdict*. Top-left — high value, low effort. These are your *quick wins*, and they're pure gold. Maximum impact, minimal work. You do these *first*, always, no debate. Top-right — high value, high effort — your *big bets* or major projects. Genuinely worth doing, but they're expensive, so you plan them carefully and do them after the quick wins. Bottom-left — low value, low effort — the *maybes* or fill-ins. Cheap, but they don't do much. Fine to slot in when you have spare capacity, but never a priority. And bottom-right — low value, high effort — the *money pit*. Expensive *and* pointless. You avoid these like the plague. The corner names *are* your prioritization.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE ORDER OF OPERATIONS." The quadrants number themselves 1-2-3-and-avoid.]

EV: So the order practically writes itself. One — do the quick wins immediately; they build momentum and deliver value fast. Two — plan the big bets; these are often your most important long-term work, just resource-intensive. Three — sprinkle in the low-effort maybes when there's slack. And the money pit? You don't do it at all — and a *great* move in an interview is explicitly calling out "and these down here I'd consciously *not* build, because they cost a lot and deliver little." Naming what you'd *drop* shows the saying-no courage we talked about. Don't just rank what you'd do — point at what you'd kill.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY IT BEATS RICE IN AN INTERVIEW." A stopwatch; 2x2 drawn fast vs RICE math slow.]

EV: Now you might ask — when do I use this versus RICE? Here's the honest answer. In an interview, the two-by-two usually wins, because it's *fast* and *visual* and keeps the conversation flowing — you don't want to burn five minutes doing RICE arithmetic out loud. On the *job*, with real data and many stakeholders, RICE's rigor and its comparable scores are often worth the extra work. So — rough-and-fast, reach for the grid; precise-and-defensible with real numbers, reach for RICE. And honestly? They agree most of the time, because they're asking similar questions. Pick the tool that fits the moment.

[VISUAL: Header, cautionary: "TWO TRAPS." Two warning icons.]

EV: Two traps with the grid. Trap one — being vague about *value*. "High value" is lazy if you can't say *why*. Push yourself: valuable to *whom*, and *how* — more revenue? happier users? unblocks future work? The grid is only as good as the thinking behind each placement. Trap two — underestimating effort, which everyone does. That exciting "quick win" might actually be a big bet in disguise once you account for edge cases and testing. When unsure, place it slightly *more* effortful than your optimistic gut says. Honest axes make an honest grid.

[VISUAL: Recap: the clean 2x2 with the four quadrant verdicts labeled, quick-wins corner glowing.]

EV: So — the value-versus-effort grid. Two axes: value up, effort right. Four verdicts: quick wins first, big bets planned, maybes as fill-ins, money pits avoided. It's fast, visual, needs no math, and it's your best friend in an interview. Just be specific about value, honest about effort, and don't forget to point at what you'd *not* build. Master this, and you can sort any chaos in under a minute.

[VISUAL: Header: "GET SPECIFIC ABOUT 'VALUE'." The value axis explodes into sub-factors: revenue, user delight, strategic, unblocks-future.]

EV: Let me go deeper on the hardest part of this tool — being honest about *value* — because "high value" is where lazy thinking hides. When you place a dot high on the value axis, force yourself to say *what kind* of value and *to whom*. Is it *revenue* value — does it directly make money? Is it *user* value — does it make people's lives better, even if the money's indirect? Is it *strategic* value — does it open a new market or build a moat? Or is it *enabling* value — does it unblock a bunch of *future* work? That last one is sneaky-important, because a feature can look low-value on its own but actually be high-value because five other things depend on it. A boring piece of infrastructure might be your real top priority because nothing else can ship until it's done. So when you place a dot, narrate the value out loud: "high value, specifically because it unblocks our whole payments roadmap." That specificity is what turns a vague grid into a *defensible* decision — and it's exactly what interviewers are listening for.
