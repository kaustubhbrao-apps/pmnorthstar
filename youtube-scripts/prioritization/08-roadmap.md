A great roadmap turns your private priorities into shared momentum. Let's build one the right way.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE #1 RULE — THEMES, NOT A FEATURE LIST." A long feature checklist vs a few bold outcome-themes.]

EV: Rule number one, and it's the biggest mindset shift — a great roadmap is organized around *themes and outcomes*, not a long list of features with dates. The rookie roadmap is a giant checklist: "Feature A in January, Feature B in February," like a factory schedule. The problem? It's rigid, it's boring, and it makes promises you'll inevitably break. A strong roadmap instead groups work under *themes* tied to goals: "This quarter's theme is *reducing friction in checkout*," and under it sit the specific bets. Themes tell everyone the *why*, survive inevitable changes in the *what*, and keep the team focused on *outcomes* — the problems you're solving — rather than a brittle list of outputs. Lead with outcomes, not outputs.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 3 HORIZONS — NOW / NEXT / LATER." Three bands stretch out, getting fuzzier further out.]

EV: Rule number two — use time *horizons*, not precise dates, and let the future get *fuzzier*. The cleanest format is three horizons: Now, Next, and Later. *Now* — what we're actively building, fairly committed and specific. *Next* — what's coming after, likely but not locked. *Later* — directions we're considering, deliberately vague. Notice the genius of this: the near term is detailed and confident, and the further out you go, the *fuzzier* it gets — which is *honest*, because you genuinely don't know exactly what you'll build in nine months, and pretending you do just sets you up to break promises. Now, Next, Later communicates direction without lying about certainty. It's the format that's become standard for a reason.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY YOU AVOID HARD DATES." A dated roadmap cracking under a missed deadline vs a flexible horizon roadmap bending gracefully.]

EV: And let me stress *why* we avoid hard dates on everything, because new PMs resist this. The moment you write "Feature X ships March 15th" on a roadmap, you've made a *promise*. And in product, things change — you learn something, priorities shift, an estimate was wrong. When March 15th comes and goes, you've lost trust, even if you made the right call to change course. Horizons protect you *and* keep you honest. Now — this doesn't mean *never* commit to a date; for a specific launch with real external dependencies, you absolutely might. It means your *roadmap* communicates direction and intent, while *specific* commitments are made carefully and separately. Flexibility where you can, commitment where you must.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE ROADMAP, MANY AUDIENCES." The same roadmap shown through three different lenses: team, exec, customer.]

EV: Rule number three — communicate the roadmap *differently* to different audiences, because they care about different things. For your *engineering and design team*, emphasize the *why* and the problems to solve, plus enough detail to start work — they want context and clarity. For *leadership and executives*, connect everything to *business goals and strategy* — "this theme drives our growth North Star" — they want to see the roadmap ladders up to company objectives. And for *customers*, keep it high-level and benefit-focused — "we're making checkout faster" — and be *careful* with promises, because customers remember commitments and get angry when they slip. Same underlying roadmap, three different framings. Knowing your audience is half of communicating well.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE ROADMAP IS A LIVING DOCUMENT." A roadmap breathing/updating as new info arrives.]

EV: Rule number four — a roadmap is *alive*, not carved in stone. This trips people up. A roadmap is your *best current thinking*, and it *should* change as you learn — a competitor moves, an experiment surprises you, priorities shift. That's not failure, that's good product management responding to reality. The key is to *communicate changes clearly and with reasoning* when they happen — "we're moving this up because the data showed X." A roadmap that never changes means you're ignoring new information; a roadmap that changes chaotically means you had no conviction. The sweet spot: steady direction, updated thoughtfully as you learn. Treat it as a living document, and say *why* when it breathes.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SHOWS UP IN INTERVIEWS." A candidate presenting a themed, horizon-based plan.]

EV: How does this land in an interview? Often as the *finish* to a prioritization question — after you've ranked things, present them as a roadmap. "So I'd structure this as: *Now*, we fix the payment bug and ship the local payment method — our theme is unblocking revenue. *Next*, the referral feature and loyalty program — the theme shifts to growth. *Later*, we'd explore the redesign. And I'd frame it for leadership around our growth North Star, and keep customer promises vague until committed." That answer shows you don't just *decide* priorities — you can *mobilize an organization* around them. That's the leap from "good analyst" to "product leader," and it's a fantastic note to end a prioritization answer on.

[VISUAL: Header: "ROADMAP RED FLAGS TO AVOID." A few bad-roadmap patterns get crossed out.]

EV: Before we close, let me give you the quick red-flags list — the roadmap mistakes that scream "junior PM," so you can avoid every one. Red flag one — the *dated feature checklist*: a rigid list of features with exact dates, which is brittle and sets you up to break promises. Lead with themes and horizons instead. Red flag two — the *everything roadmap*: so stuffed with items it has no real priorities, which just tells everyone you couldn't say no. A good roadmap is as notable for what it *leaves off*. Red flag three — the *output obsession*: listing features — the *what* — with no connection to outcomes or goals — the *why*. Always tie work to the problem it solves. Red flag four — the *set-it-and-forget-it* roadmap that never updates, meaning you're ignoring new information; or its evil twin, the *chaos* roadmap that changes every week, meaning you have no conviction. And red flag five — *same roadmap for every audience*, boring your execs with technical detail or scaring your customers with internal uncertainty. Dodge these five, and your roadmap already looks senior. The best roadmap is themed, horizon-based, outcome-driven, alive, and audience-aware — the exact opposite of all five flags.
