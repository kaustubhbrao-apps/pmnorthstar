[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "THE KANO MODEL."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. So far we've prioritized by value and effort — but today's model adds a crucial new lens: not all "value" is the same *type*. The Kano model, from Professor Noriaki Kano, says features fall into distinct categories based on *how user satisfaction responds* to them. And understanding those categories stops you from making a classic, expensive prioritization mistake. Let's meet the three types that matter most.

[VISUAL: Header: "TYPE 1 · BASIC EXPECTATIONS." A graph: no matter how much you deliver, satisfaction caps at neutral; absence causes anger.]

EV: Type one — Basic Expectations, sometimes called must-haves. These are the features users simply *assume* will be there. A hotel room has a working door lock. An app lets you log in. Here's the key, and it's counterintuitive: doing these *well* earns you almost zero extra credit — nobody praises a hotel for having a door that locks. But doing them *badly*, or missing them, makes users *furious*. So basics are asymmetric — they can only ever *lose* you points, never win them. You absolutely must nail them... but pouring *extra* effort into making a basic "amazing" is wasted. A door lock that's *extra* locky doesn't delight anyone.

[VISUAL: Header: "TYPE 2 · PERFORMANCE FEATURES." A clean straight line: more delivered = more satisfaction, linearly.]

EV: Type two — Performance features, the "more is better" category. Here, satisfaction scales *linearly* with how much you deliver. Faster load times, more storage, better battery life, lower price. The more you give, the happier users get, in direct proportion. These are the features users actively compare between products — "this phone has more battery than that one." Performance features are where you compete head-to-head, and investing more genuinely pays off in more satisfaction. This is the category most people intuitively understand — do more, get more.

[VISUAL: Header: "TYPE 3 · DELIGHTERS." A graph shooting up steeply: their presence thrills; their absence isn't even missed.]

EV: And type three — Delighters, the exciting ones. These are features users don't expect and wouldn't even miss if absent — but when present, they produce *disproportionate* joy. The surprise free upgrade. The playful animation. The feature that makes people go "whoa, I didn't know I needed this." Delighters are asymmetric the *opposite* way from basics — their absence costs you nothing, but their presence can create real love and word-of-mouth. They're how you turn a merely *acceptable* product into one people *rave* about. The catch: they're often where the magic and differentiation live, but they're easy to skip because nobody's *asking* for them.

EV: Quick nudge — if the three types are clicking, subscribe to pmnorthstar so you catch the rest. Now, the prioritization payoff.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW KANO DRIVES PRIORITIZATION." A funding split across the three types.]

EV: So here's how Kano actually changes your prioritization — and this is the whole point. You must fund the three types *differently*. Rule one — nail all your Basics *first*, but don't over-invest in them. They're table stakes; get them solid and *stop*. Gilding a basic is wasted money. Rule two — invest in Performance features where you want to *compete*, because more genuinely means more satisfaction. And rule three — sprinkle in a *few* Delighters to create love and stand out, but don't rely only on them — a product of pure delighters with broken basics is a disaster. The mistake Kano saves you from: pouring effort into making a *basic* feature "world-class" when users won't care, while ignoring the delighter that would've won their hearts. Fund each type according to how satisfaction actually responds.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TWIST — TYPES DECAY OVER TIME." A delighter slides down into performance, then into basic.]

EV: And here's the fascinating twist that makes Kano feel alive — categories *migrate over time*. Today's delighter becomes tomorrow's basic expectation. Think about it: a camera on a phone was once an exciting delighter. Then it became a performance feature — "whose camera is better?" And now? A phone *without* a camera would be unthinkable — it's a basic. The delight wore off and expectations rose. This means you can *never stop innovating* — the thing that delights users today will be the bare minimum they demand in a few years. Mentioning this in an interview — "and remember, today's delighter is tomorrow's basic, so we have to keep finding new ones" — is a genuinely sophisticated insight that makes you stand out.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SHOWS UP IN INTERVIEWS." A prioritization question answered with Kano language.]

EV: How does Kano help you in an interview? It gives you richer language for *why* you're prioritizing something. Instead of flat "this is valuable," you can say: "This is a basic expectation we're currently failing, so it's hurting us — fix it first. This other one is a performance feature where we're behind competitors, so investing here wins comparisons. And this third one is a delighter that could really differentiate us and earn word-of-mouth." That framing shows you understand *types* of value, not just amounts. It's a layer of sophistication most candidates never bring, and it pairs beautifully with the value-effort grid from last video.

[VISUAL: Recap: the three Kano curves on one graph — basic (flattening low), performance (linear), delighter (soaring).]

EV: So — the Kano model. Three types. Basics: assumed, only lose you points, nail them but don't over-invest. Performance: more is linearly better, compete here. Delighters: unexpected joy, differentiate with a few. Fund each differently, and remember — delighters decay into basics over time, so keep innovating. Layer this onto value-versus-effort, and your prioritization gets a whole new dimension of insight.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CLASSIC KANO BLUNDER." A team gold-plating a basic while a competitor ships a delighter and wins.]

EV: Let me show you the exact expensive mistake Kano exists to prevent, with a story you'll recognize. Imagine two competing apps. Team A decides to make their *login* absolutely world-class — biometrics, fancy animations, six ways to sign in. Months of work on... logging in. A *basic*. And how do users react? A collective shrug. "Cool, I guess?" Nobody switches apps or tells a friend because the login was gorgeous — because logging in is just *expected*. Meanwhile Team B spent those same months on a *delighter* — say, a feature that automatically remembers your usual order and lets you reorder in one tap. Users went "whoa, that's amazing." and told their friends. Team B wins — not because they worked harder, but because they invested in the *right type* of feature. Team A poured delighter-level effort into a basic and got basic-level reward. *That's* the Kano blunder — over-investing where satisfaction is capped, under-investing where it soars. Knowing the three types is what keeps you from pouring your best months into something users will never even notice.
