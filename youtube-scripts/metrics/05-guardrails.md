[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "COUNTER-METRICS & GUARDRAILS."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. This video is about the single move that makes you sound genuinely senior in any metrics discussion — the guardrail metric. Most candidates only think about the number they want to *grow*. The mature PM also thinks about the number they must *protect*. Because here's the hard truth — almost any metric, pushed hard enough, can be grown in ways that secretly damage your product. Guardrails are how you catch that before it's too late.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CORE PROBLEM — METRICS GET GAMED." A metric being inflated by a sketchy shortcut, with hidden damage underneath.]

EV: Here's the core problem. When a whole team optimizes one metric relentlessly, they'll often find a shortcut that pumps that number while hurting something you weren't watching. Classic example: you want to grow "time spent in app," so the team adds aggressive, addictive notifications and autoplay everything. Time spent soars. But user *satisfaction* tanks, people feel manipulated, and six months later they churn in droves. You won the battle for your metric and lost the war for your product. A guardrail metric is the alarm that would've caught this early.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHAT IS A GUARDRAIL?" A primary metric climbing, with a protective floor/ceiling line that must not be crossed.]

EV: So what exactly is a guardrail metric? It's a metric you watch to make sure that, while you chase your primary goal, you're not breaking something important. It's not the thing you're trying to *grow* — it's the thing you're trying not to *harm*. You set a line: "we'll push our main metric, but *not* if this guardrail crosses a danger threshold." Think of it like guardrails on a mountain road — you're driving forward fast, but they stop you from flying off the cliff. The primary metric is your speed; the guardrail is your safety.

[VISUAL: Header: "COMMON GUARDRAIL PAIRS." Pairs flip in: a primary metric next to its guardrail.]

EV: Let me give you the classic pairings, because patterns help. If your primary is "time spent" or "engagement," your guardrail is user satisfaction or complaint rate — are you engaging them or annoying them? If your primary is "revenue" or "conversion," your guardrail is refund rate or churn — did you sell them something they regret? If your primary is "content posted," your guardrail is content *quality* or report rate — are you getting great posts or spam? If your primary is "sign-up speed," your guardrail is fraud or fake-account rate — did you let the bots in? See the pattern? For every growth metric, there's a shadow it can cast — and the guardrail watches that shadow.

EV: Quick one — if guardrails are opening your eyes, subscribe to pmnorthstar, this is exactly the senior-level stuff that gets you hired. Now, how to find your guardrail.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO FIND YOURS — ASK THE DARK QUESTION." A metric with a thought bubble: "if I pushed this ruthlessly... what breaks?"]

EV: Here's the simple technique to find your guardrail for *any* metric. Ask the dark question: "If a ruthless team optimized this number at all costs, with no conscience, what would they break?" Really imagine the worst-case path to growing your metric. Want more orders? A ruthless team might spam discounts that destroy margins — so guardrail your profitability. Want more daily active users? They might send annoying daily notifications — so guardrail opt-outs and satisfaction. Want faster onboarding? They might cut important safety steps — so guardrail fraud. Whatever the dark path would damage — *that's* your guardrail. It's a surprisingly fun thought experiment, and it instantly reveals what to protect.

[VISUAL: Header: "GOODHART'S LAW." Kinetic text: "When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure."]

EV: And there's a famous principle behind all of this, worth knowing by name — Goodhart's Law. It says: "when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure." The moment you tell a team "hit this number," they'll optimize the number itself, sometimes losing sight of the real goal it was supposed to represent. Call centers told to minimize call *time* start hanging up on people. Teams told to maximize *lines of code* write bloated garbage. Naming Goodhart's Law in an interview is a serious credibility signal — it shows you understand the deep risk of metrics, and *why* guardrails exist in the first place.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SOUNDS IN THE ROOM." A clean spoken addition.]

EV: In an interview, adding a guardrail is a ten-second move with huge payoff. After you name your primary metric, just add: "and I'd pair that with a guardrail metric — because if we pushed this too hard, we might accidentally harm X, so I'd watch Y to make sure we're growing *responsibly*." That single sentence instantly separates you from every candidate who only thought about growth. It signals you think about the *health* of the product, not just the vanity of a rising line. Very few people do this — which is exactly why it makes you memorable.

[VISUAL: Header: "GROW RESPONSIBLY." The metric climbs smoothly between two guardrail lines, product intact and glowing.]

EV: And that's the real mindset here — responsible growth. A junior PM asks "how do I make this number go up?" A senior PM asks "how do I make this number go up *without breaking anything that matters*?" That second question is the whole job at scale. Anyone can inflate a metric with a cheap trick. The craft is growing it sustainably, keeping the product healthy and users genuinely happy while the line climbs. Guardrails are how you prove you can do that.

[VISUAL: Recap: a primary metric climbing between guardrail lines, with common pairings listed.]

EV: So — guardrail metrics. Almost any metric can be gamed in ways that hurt the product, thanks to Goodhart's Law. So for every metric you grow, name a guardrail you protect. Find it by asking the dark question — what would a ruthless team break? Pair engagement with satisfaction, revenue with churn, speed with fraud. And always frame it as growing *responsibly*. Master this, and you'll sound like the most thoughtful product person in the room.

[VISUAL: Header: "A REAL CAUTIONARY TALE." A social feed optimizing "engagement" while a user doom-scrolls unhappily.]

EV: Let me ground this in the most famous cautionary tale in all of tech, because it shows guardrails aren't academic — they're how products avoid doing real harm. For years, social media companies optimized hard for "engagement" — time spent, clicks, scrolls. The number went up and up and up. Victory, right? Except the ruthless path to more engagement turned out to be outrage, addiction, and endless doom-scrolling — because what keeps people glued isn't always what's *good* for them. Engagement soared while user well-being, trust, and satisfaction quietly cratered, and it became a society-level problem. That is Goodhart's Law at planetary scale. And it's *exactly* what a guardrail metric is designed to prevent. If those teams had treated "user-reported well-being" or "regret rate" as a hard guardrail — a line engagement wasn't allowed to cross — the story could've gone differently. So when you name a guardrail in an interview, you're not doing a party trick. You're showing you understand that growth without guardrails can build something genuinely harmful. That's the mark of a product leader worth trusting.
