EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. This video is about a distinction that sounds academic but completely changes how you steer a product — the difference between leading and lagging indicators. Get this, and you'll know not just *what* to measure, but *which* metrics let you act *before* it's too late versus which only confirm what already happened. It's the difference between a windshield and a rear-view mirror.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE DEFINITIONS." Two cards: a lagging metric (result, past) and a leading metric (predictor, future).]

EV: Let's define them cleanly. A *lagging* indicator measures a *result* that has already happened. It's the outcome, the final score — revenue, churn, retention. It tells you how you did, but by the time you see it, it's done; you can't change it anymore. A *leading* indicator measures something *earlier* in the chain that *predicts* that future result. It's an early signal you can still act on. Lagging is the rear-view mirror — accurate, but backward-looking. Leading is the windshield — you see what's coming while you can still steer.

[VISUAL: Header: "AN EXAMPLE THAT CLICKS." A chain: "onboarding completion → week-1 activity → 6-month retention."]

EV: Here's an example that makes it click. Say your big lagging metric is *six-month retention* — do users stick around half a year later? Super important... but you have to wait six months to measure it. Useless for steering *today*. So you find leading indicators that *predict* it. Maybe you discover that users who complete onboarding and take three key actions in week one are way more likely to still be around at six months. Now "week-one actions" is your leading indicator — you can measure it in *days*, and if it dips, you can fix onboarding *now*, long before the six-month retention number would've revealed the damage. Leading indicators let you catch problems while there's still time to act.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY YOU NEED BOTH." A scale balancing the rear-view mirror and the windshield.]

EV: Now — crucial point — you need *both*, and candidates who understand this stand out. Lagging indicators are your *truth*: they confirm what actually happened, they're what the business ultimately cares about, revenue and retention are real. But they're slow. Leading indicators are your *steering wheel*: fast, actionable, predictive — but they're only *proxies*, and a bad one can mislead you. So the art is: use lagging indicators to define real success, and use leading indicators to *manage toward* that success day to day. The windshield to drive, the rear-view to confirm you went the right way. You don't pick one — you use them together.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO FIND A LEADING INDICATOR." Data detective: compare users who later succeeded vs churned, find the early difference.]

EV: So how do you actually find a good leading indicator for a lagging result you care about? You look backward at your data and ask: "what did users who *eventually succeeded* do *early on* that users who *eventually failed* didn't?" Find the early behavior that most separates the two groups. That early behavior is your leading indicator. This is exactly how the famous ones were discovered — a social network found that users who added a certain number of friends in their first days almost always stuck around, while those who didn't, churned. That early action became the leading indicator the whole growth team drove toward. Find the early behavior that predicts the late result — that's the recipe.

[VISUAL: Header: "A WARNING — LEADING ≠ CAUSING." A correlation arrow with a "⚠ test it" tag.]

EV: One important warning, because it's a classic mistake. A leading indicator that *predicts* a result isn't automatically *causing* it — that's correlation, not causation. Maybe users who add seven friends retain better not because the friends cause retention, but because engaged people naturally do both. So before you bet the whole roadmap on driving a leading indicator, you want to *test* that pushing it actually moves the lagging result — ideally with an experiment. Mentioning this in an interview — "I'd confirm the leading indicator actually *drives* retention, not just correlates with it" — is a beautiful signal of rigor. Don't just find the predictor; verify it's a real lever.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SHOWS UP IN INTERVIEWS." A metric question with a layered answer.]

EV: How does this show up in interviews? Often subtly. When asked for a metric, a strong answer layers both: "My ultimate measure of success is retention — that's the lagging truth. But since that takes months, I'd steer day to day using a leading indicator, like week-one activation, which predicts retention and lets me act fast." And just like that, you've shown you understand measuring *results* versus measuring *what you can steer by*. It also shines in root-cause questions: if a lagging metric drops, a sharp PM checks the leading indicators to understand *why* it's about to get worse or better. This concept quietly makes every other metrics answer stronger.

[VISUAL: Recap: the windshield (leading) and rear-view (lagging) with the example chain between them.]

EV: So — leading versus lagging. Lagging indicators measure results that already happened — true, but slow; your rear-view mirror. Leading indicators predict those results early enough to act — fast, but proxies; your windshield. Find leading indicators by seeing what early behavior separates future winners from future churners, and verify they actually *drive* the result, not just correlate. Use both: lagging to define success, leading to steer toward it. Do that, and you'll never drive your product looking only backward.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE WEIGHT-LOSS ANALOGY." A scale (lagging) vs daily habits: calories, steps, sleep (leading).]

EV: Let me give you an analogy that locks this in forever, because it's one everyone feels. Say your goal is to lose weight. The number on the *scale* is your lagging indicator — it's the real result, the truth... but it moves slowly, it's noisy day to day, and by the time it's clearly wrong, you've already gained the weight. You can't *directly* control the scale. So what do you actually manage day to day? Your *leading* indicators — calories eaten, steps walked, hours slept. Those you control *today*, and those *predict* where the scale goes next month. If you only ever stared at the scale, you'd be frustrated and powerless. But by managing the daily leading habits, you steer the lagging result. Products work *exactly* the same way. Retention and revenue are your scale — the slow truth. Activation, engagement, daily habits — those are your calories and steps, the things you actually act on now to move the scale later. Manage the leading inputs, confirm with the lagging output. Simple as that.
