[VISUAL: The bright star morphs into the pmnorthstar logo. Title card: "THE NORTH STAR METRIC."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar — and yes, the name is literally this concept, so you know I love it. The North Star metric is the single number that best captures the core value your product delivers to users. It's the one metric that, if it's going up, means you're genuinely winning — not faking it. Get this one right and every other decision gets clearer. So let's learn how to pick it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE GOLDEN RULE — IT MUST CAPTURE *VALUE*." A product icon with its core value pulsing; an arrow to the metric.]

EV: Here's the golden rule of a North Star: it must capture the value your users actually get, not the value *you* get. That distinction is everything. Revenue is value for the *company*. A North Star should reflect value for the *user* — because when users win, revenue follows, but not the other way around. So the question to ask is always: "what's the core thing a user comes to this product to get — and how do I measure them getting it?" Answer that, and you've found your North Star.

[VISUAL: Header: "FAMOUS NORTH STARS." Example cards flip in.]

EV: Let me make it concrete with the classics. A messaging app: *messages sent between friends* — because the value is connection. A music streaming service: *time spent listening* — because the value is enjoyment. A ride-hailing app: *completed rides* — the value is getting somewhere. A collaboration tool: *weekly active teams*, not individual users — because the value shows up when a whole team adopts it. Notice the pattern — every one of these measures the user *actually experiencing the core value*, not just showing up or signing up. That's the signature of a great North Star.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 3 TESTS OF A GOOD NORTH STAR." Three checkmarks appear.]

EV: So how do you know if a candidate metric is actually North-Star worthy? Run it through three tests. Test one — does it reflect real *value delivered*? If the number goes up, are users genuinely better off? Test two — does it predict long-term success? A good North Star today should correlate with a healthy business tomorrow. Test three — can the whole team actually influence it? A North Star nobody can move is useless. If your metric passes all three — real value, predicts success, team can move it — you've got a winner.

[VISUAL: Header: "WATCH ME PICK ONE." A food-delivery app icon; candidate metrics appear and get tested.]

EV: Let me pick one live so you see the reasoning. Product: a food-delivery app. Candidate metrics? "Downloads" — no, a download isn't value, someone can download and never order. "Registered users" — same problem. "Orders delivered" — getting warmer, that's real value, a user got fed. But let me sharpen it: "orders delivered per active user per month" — now it captures both that people order *and* that they keep coming back, which is the true heart of the value. Run the tests: real value? Yes, a completed order is the whole point. Predicts success? Yes, repeat ordering drives the business. Team can move it? Yes — through selection, speed, reliability. That's my North Star. Watch how I reasoned from *value*, not from what's easy to count.

EV: Quick nudge — if this is clicking, subscribe to pmnorthstar, and honestly the channel name will remind you of this concept forever. Now, a few traps.

[VISUAL: Header: "NORTH STAR TRAPS." Warning icons.]

EV: Three traps when picking a North Star. Trap one — picking a vanity metric. Total downloads, total sign-ups, total registered users. Big, impressive, and meaningless, because they count people who may never get any value. Always pick a metric tied to *active, ongoing* value. Trap two — picking pure revenue as your North Star. Revenue is critical, but it's a *lagging* result of delivering user value, and optimizing it directly can push you to squeeze users in ways that hurt long-term. Measure the user value that *drives* revenue. Trap three — picking something nobody can influence. "Total market size" isn't a North Star — your team can't move it. Pick a number your product work actually changes.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE NUMBER, NOT TEN." A cluttered dashboard collapses to a single glowing star with a few small supporting dots.]

EV: And the discipline that trips people up most — it's called the North *Star*, singular, for a reason. Pick ONE. Candidates love to hedge: "I'd track engagement and retention and revenue and satisfaction and..." No. Commit to the one number that best captures core value, and name it with conviction. You can — and should — have a few *supporting* metrics beneath it, which we'll cover in the metric tree next video. But the North Star itself is one number the whole company can rally behind. A team with five North Stars has none. Choose one, defend it, and you'll sound like a leader.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SOUNDS IN THE ROOM." A clean spoken sequence.]

EV: In the interview this sounds like: "The core value of this product is X. So my North Star would be *this specific metric*, because it directly captures users experiencing that value — not just showing up. It passes the key tests: it reflects real value, it predicts long-term success, and the team can actually move it." That's a confident, reasoned answer that shows you understand the product at its core. That's what wins the question.

[VISUAL: Recap: the single bright star with the three tests orbiting it.]

EV: So — the North Star. One number that captures the *user's* core value. Find it by asking what users really come for. Test it three ways: real value, predicts success, team can move it. Dodge vanity metrics, raw revenue, and un-influenceable numbers. And commit to *one*. Nail your North Star, and everything downstream gets dramatically clearer.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE MORE REP — A LEARNING APP." Candidate metrics appear and get tested against the three tests.]

EV: Let me do one more rep on a trickier product, because the reasoning muscle matters more than any single example — a language-learning app. What's the core value? Not "opening the app" — it's actually *learning the language*. So let's test candidates. "Daily active users"? It tells us people show up, but not that they're learning — someone can open the app and bounce. "Lessons completed"? Better — that's real effort toward the value. But let me push to the true heart: "learning days" — days where a user completes a meaningful lesson — or even better, a metric tied to actual *progress*, like lessons completed per active user per week. Run the three tests: real value? Yes, completing lessons is literally learning. Predicts success? Yes, people who keep learning keep subscribing. Team can move it? Absolutely — through better lessons, reminders, motivation. Notice how I kept rejecting the shallow options and pushing toward the metric that captures *learning actually happening*. That relentless "but does this capture the real value?" instinct — that's the whole skill, and now it's yours.
