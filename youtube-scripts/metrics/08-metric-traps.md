[VISUAL: Header: "TRAP 1 · VANITY METRICS." A giant impressive number with nothing behind it.]

EV: Trap one — the vanity metric. The big, impressive-looking number that makes everyone feel great and means almost nothing. Total downloads. Total registered users. Total page views. Cumulative sign-ups. They only ever go *up* — they literally can't go down — so they always look like success. But a download isn't a happy user, and a registration isn't someone getting value. The test to catch a vanity metric: ask "can this number ever go *down*, and does it reflect *current* value?" If it only climbs and counts dead weight, it's vanity. Swap it for an *active* metric — weekly active users, not total users. Current value, not cumulative counts.

[VISUAL: Header: "TRAP 2 · GOODHART'S LAW." Kinetic text: "When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure."]

EV: Trap two — Goodhart's Law, which we met with guardrails. "When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure." The moment you make a number *the* goal, people optimize the number itself, often destroying the real thing it stood for. Support team measured on *tickets closed per hour*? They'll close tickets without actually helping anyone. Newsroom measured on *clicks*? You get clickbait headlines and garbage articles. The metric goes up, the mission goes down. The defense is everything we built: pair targets with guardrails, and never let a single number become so sacred that people game it. Measure the goal, not just a proxy for the goal.

[VISUAL: Header: "TRAP 3 · THE AVERAGE THAT LIES." A flat average line hiding a bimodal split underneath.]

EV: Trap three — the average that lies. Averages hide more than they reveal. "Average session time is five minutes." Sounds healthy. But maybe half your users bounce in ten seconds and the other half stay ten minutes — nobody actually experiences "five minutes." The average smeared two totally different realities into one misleading number. The fix: look at the *distribution*, not just the average. Use medians, use percentiles, segment your users. Whenever someone quotes you an average in an interview, a great instinct is to ask "what does the *distribution* look like? Is that an average of similar users, or two very different groups blended together?" That question alone signals real analytical maturity.

[VISUAL: Header: "TRAP 4 · SURVIVORSHIP BIAS." A chart built only from survivors; the ones who left are invisible/greyed out.]

EV: Trap four — survivorship bias. You measure only the users who *stuck around*, and draw happy conclusions, while completely ignoring everyone who already left. "Our active users love feature X." Sure — but the people who *churned because of feature X* aren't in your data anymore, so you never see them. You're studying the survivors and missing the casualties entirely. The fix: deliberately look at the users who *left* — churn analysis, exit surveys, the cohorts that didn't make it. The most important lesson is often hiding in the data you *don't* have because those users already walked out the door.

[VISUAL: Header: "TRAP 5 · CORRELATION ≠ CAUSATION." Two lines moving together; a "⚠" over the assumption that one causes the other.]

EV: Trap five — mistaking correlation for causation. We touched this with leading indicators, but it's everywhere. Two numbers move together, so you assume one *causes* the other. "Users who use feature Y retain better, so let's push everyone to feature Y." But maybe engaged users naturally do both — the feature isn't *causing* retention, it's just *correlated* with being an engaged person. Push feature Y on disengaged users and nothing improves. The fix: before betting on a lever, *test* it — ideally an experiment where you actually change the one thing and watch the result. Saying "I'd confirm causation with a test, not just assume it from correlation" is a hallmark of a rigorous thinker.

[VISUAL: Header: "TRAP 6 · THE WRONG TIME FRAME." A metric up this week, down this quarter — two charts telling opposite stories.]

EV: And trap six — the wrong time frame. The same metric can tell opposite stories depending on the window you look at. A change might boost engagement *this week* — a shiny new thing, everyone pokes at it — but *hurt* it next quarter once the novelty dies and the annoyance sets in. Aggressive monetization might spike revenue this month and crater it next year through churn. The fix: always ask "over what time frame?" and especially watch for short-term wins that are long-term losses. The best products optimize for the long game. Checking whether a "win" survives a longer window is a mark of real product wisdom.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MASTER DEFENSE." A single question glows: "WHAT IS THIS NUMBER *REALLY* TELLING ME?"]

EV: Now here's the beautiful thing — all six traps share one master defense, one question that disarms every single one of them. Whenever you look at a metric, ask: "what is this number *really* telling me — and what might it be *hiding*?" Vanity? It's hiding inactive users. Goodhart? It's hiding a gamed proxy. Averages? Hiding the distribution. Survivorship? Hiding the people who left. Correlation? Hiding the real cause. Time frame? Hiding the long-term story. That one skeptical question — what's hiding behind this pretty number — turns you from someone who gets fooled by metrics into someone who sees *through* them.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT WINS INTERVIEWS." An interviewer shows a rising chart; the candidate asks the sharp questions.]

EV: And this is gold in interviews, because interviewers *love* to show you a metric and ask "this went up — good news, right?" The weak candidate says "yes, great." The strong candidate gets *appropriately* skeptical: "It could be — but let me check. Is it a vanity metric or real active value? What's the distribution? Over what time frame? And are we sure nothing else is being harmed?" You've just demonstrated you can't be fooled by a pretty chart — which is *exactly* the person they want owning their product's numbers.
