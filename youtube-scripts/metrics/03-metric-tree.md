EV: Your North Star is one beautiful number. But here's the problem — you can't *directly* grab it and move it. So you break it into branches you *can* move. That's the metric tree, and it turns a goal into a to-do list. Let's build it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "THE METRIC TREE."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Last video we found our North Star — the one number that captures core value. But a North Star has a catch: it's often too big and too high-level to act on directly. You can't just walk in and "increase messages sent" — you have to pull *levers* that add up to it. The metric tree is how you find those levers. It breaks your one North Star into the smaller input metrics your team actually works on day to day. It's maybe the most practical tool in this whole series.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BIG IDEA — OUTPUT vs INPUTS." A faucet (output) fed by several pipes (inputs).]

EV: Here's the core idea. Your North Star is an *output* — a result. And every output is produced by a set of *inputs* — the things that feed it. Picture a faucet: the water coming out is your North Star, and the pipes feeding it are your input metrics. You can't make more water appear by staring at the faucet — you have to work the pipes. The metric tree maps those pipes, so instead of a vague "grow the North Star," you get a clear set of specific levers to pull.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO BUILD IT — BREAK INTO DRIVERS." A North Star node splits into 2-4 driver branches.]

EV: So how do you build the tree? You ask, over and over: "what drives this number?" Start with your North Star at the top, and break it into the two to four big things that directly produce it. Let me show you with a classic. Say our North Star is "total orders per month" for a delivery app. What drives it? Well — total orders equals the *number of active users*, times the *average orders per user*. Two clean branches. More users, or each user ordering more. Already you've gone from one vague goal to two concrete directions.

[VISUAL: The two branches each split again into sub-nodes.]

EV: Now you break each branch down further. What drives *number of active users*? New users acquired, plus existing users retained, minus users churning. What drives *orders per user*? How often they order — frequency — and maybe how many items, but let's focus on frequency. So now the tree has leaves like: new user acquisition, retention rate, churn rate, and order frequency. And here's the payoff — *those* are things a team can actually own and move. One team works acquisition, another works retention, another works frequency. The giant abstract North Star just became a set of concrete, assignable jobs. That's the magic of the tree.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THIS IS SO POWERFUL." Three benefit icons pop.]

EV: Why does this matter so much? Three reasons. One — it makes a huge goal *actionable*. Instead of "grow orders," you get "improve retention by two points and lift frequency." Two — it *diagnoses* problems. If your North Star is flat, you walk the tree to find which branch is sagging — retention dropping? frequency falling? The tree is a map for root-cause analysis too. And three — it *aligns teams*. Each team owns a branch, and everyone can see how their branch rolls up into the one North Star. No more teams optimizing random disconnected numbers. The tree connects every daily task to the one thing that matters.

EV: Quick one — if the tree is clicking, subscribe to pmnorthstar so you catch the rest. Now, how to use it in an interview.

[VISUAL: Header: "USING IT IN THE INTERVIEW." A tree drawing itself as EV narrates.]

EV: In an interview, the metric tree is a flex move that makes you look structured and senior. When asked "how would you grow this metric?" don't just rattle off tactics. Say: "Let me break the metric into its drivers first." Then build the tree out loud. "Orders equal active users times orders per user. Active users come from acquisition, retention, and churn. So to grow orders, I can pull any of these levers — and I'd prioritize based on which is weakest right now." See what happened? You turned a vague question into a structured map, and you set yourself up to prioritize. Interviewers love it because it shows you think in systems, not random ideas.

[VISUAL: Header: "KEEP IT CLEAN — 2 LEVELS, MECE-ish." A tidy 2-level tree vs a tangled messy one.]

EV: A couple of rules to keep your tree clean. First — don't go too deep. Two, maybe three levels is plenty in an interview. A tree with seven layers is a mess nobody can follow. Second — try to make each split roughly *complete and non-overlapping* — the pieces should add up to the whole without double-counting. "Active users equals new plus retained" — clean. Don't have overlapping branches that count the same users twice. You don't need to be perfect here, just logical. A clean two-level tree beats a sprawling tangled one every time.

[VISUAL: Header: "ADDITIVE vs MULTIPLICATIVE." A "+" branch and a "×" branch shown side by side.]

EV: And one sharp detail that impresses — notice some branches *add* and some *multiply*. Orders equals active users *times* orders-per-user — that's multiplicative. Active users equals new *plus* retained *minus* churned — that's additive. Knowing which is which matters, because multiplicative levers compound — improving two multiplied inputs by ten percent each gives you more than twenty percent. Mentioning this shows real numerical fluency. You don't need to belabor it, but dropping "and since these multiply, improving both compounds" is a lovely senior touch.

[VISUAL: Recap: the clean upside-down tree, North Star on top, movable leaves at the bottom glowing.]

EV: So — the metric tree. Your North Star is the output; break it into the inputs that feed it. Ask "what drives this?" repeatedly, two levels deep, keeping branches clean and non-overlapping. It makes big goals actionable, diagnoses what's sagging, and aligns teams. And in an interview, building one out loud instantly makes you look like a systems thinker. One North Star up top, a set of movable levers below — that's how goals actually get done.

[VISUAL: Header: "USE THE TREE TO *DIAGNOSE*." A North Star flat-lines; EV walks the branches to find the sagging one.]

EV: Let me show you a second superpower of the tree that most people miss — using it to *diagnose* a problem, not just grow. Say your North Star, orders per month, suddenly goes flat. Instead of panicking, you walk your tree. "Orders equals active users times orders-per-user. Which branch moved? Let me check — active users are actually *up*. So the problem is on the other branch: orders-per-user, the frequency, must be *down*." Now walk that branch: "frequency is driven by how often people have a reason to come back. Did something hurt reorder rate — did we remove reminders, slow down delivery, raise prices?" See what happened? The tree turned a vague "orders are flat" into a precise "frequency dropped, here's where to look" — in about thirty seconds. This is why the metric tree quietly powers root-cause analysis too. A good tree isn't just a growth map — it's a diagnostic map. It tells you where to grow *and* where to look when something breaks.
