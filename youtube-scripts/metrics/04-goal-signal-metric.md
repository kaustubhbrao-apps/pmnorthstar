[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "GOAL → SIGNAL → METRIC."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. So far we've worked at the product level — North Stars, metric trees. But a huge share of metrics questions are about a *single feature*: "We're adding X. How would you measure success?" And for that, there's a beautifully simple three-step tool: goal, to signal, to metric. Three links in a chain that take you from "we built a thing" to "here's the exact number that proves it worked." Let's build it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CHAIN." Three boxes link with arrows: GOAL → SIGNAL → METRIC.]

EV: Here's the chain. Step one — the Goal: what are you actually trying to achieve with this feature? Step two — the Signal: what user *behavior* would show you're achieving that goal? And step three — the Metric: how do you turn that behavior into a specific, trackable number? Goal, signal, metric. Each link flows logically from the one before, and that logical flow is exactly what interviewers want to see. You're not pulling a metric from nowhere — you're *deriving* it from the goal. Let's walk each link.

[VISUAL: Box 1 "GOAL" enlarges. A target with the feature's purpose.]

EV: Link one — the Goal. Before anything, ask: *why* are we building this feature? What's the intended outcome? And be specific about *whose* outcome. "We're adding a dark mode." Okay, but why? The goal might be "reduce eye strain for users who browse at night, so they stay in the app longer and more comfortably." Notice — the goal isn't "ship dark mode," it's the *outcome* dark mode is supposed to create. If you can't articulate the goal clearly, you can't measure it. The goal is the anchor for everything downstream.

[VISUAL: Box 2 "SIGNAL" enlarges. A user figure doing an observable behavior; a little radar ping.]

EV: Link two — the Signal. Now ask: if we achieved that goal, what would users *do* differently? What observable behavior would show it? For dark mode: if we truly reduced nighttime eye strain and made the app more comfortable, the signal might be "users enable dark mode and then spend more time in the app during evening hours." The signal is the behavioral bridge between the abstract goal and a concrete number. It answers "what does success look like in terms of what people actually do?" Nail the signal, and the metric almost writes itself.

[VISUAL: Box 3 "METRIC" enlarges. The behavior turns into a clean number on a chart.]

EV: Link three — the Metric. Finally, turn that signal into a specific number you can track. The signal was "users enable dark mode and spend more evening time in-app." The metrics: "percentage of users who enable dark mode" and "average evening session length for dark-mode users versus everyone else." Now you have concrete, trackable numbers that connect straight back to the goal. See the chain? Goal: reduce nighttime strain, keep users comfortable. Signal: they use it and stay longer at night. Metric: adoption rate plus evening session length. Airtight logic, start to finish.

EV: Quick nudge — if this chain is clicking, subscribe to pmnorthstar, it genuinely helps. Now, picking the *primary* metric.

[VISUAL: Header: "PICK ONE PRIMARY — THEN SUPPORT." One bold metric, a few smaller ones beneath.]

EV: Just like with the North Star, even for a feature you want ONE primary success metric — the single number that best proves the goal was hit — plus a couple of supporting ones. For dark mode, the primary might be "evening session length for dark-mode users," because that most directly reflects the actual goal of comfortable nighttime use. Supporting metrics round it out: adoption rate tells you if people even found it; retention of dark-mode users tells you if it kept them. Lead with the primary, name it with conviction, then layer support. One clear answer beats a scattered list.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE ADOPTION → ENGAGEMENT → RETENTION LADDER." Three rungs climb upward.]

EV: And here's a handy mental checklist for feature metrics — think in three rungs. Adoption: are people even *using* the feature? Engagement: are they using it *meaningfully and repeatedly*? And retention: does it make them *stick with the product* overall? A great feature answer touches all three. "First I'd check adoption — do people turn it on? Then engagement — do they keep using it? Then the big one — retention — do dark-mode users stick around longer than those who don't?" That ladder ensures you measure not just whether people *tried* the feature, but whether it delivered *lasting* value. Lots of features get adopted and then abandoned — the ladder catches that.

[VISUAL: Header: "WATCH IT ON A SOCIAL FEATURE." The chain rebuilds for a "Stories" feature.]

EV: Let me run the chain once more on a different feature so it really sticks — a "Stories" feature on a social app. Goal: "give users a low-pressure way to share daily moments, so they post more often and engage more." Signal: "users post stories regularly and others view and react to them." Metric — primary: "stories posted per active user per week"; supporting: "story view rate" and "does posting stories increase overall time in app?" Same three links, completely different feature, under a minute. Once this chain is automatic, you can measure *any* feature on the spot.

[VISUAL: Recap: the goal→signal→metric chain with the adoption/engagement/retention ladder beside it.]

EV: So — measuring a feature. Walk the chain: Goal, what outcome are we after; Signal, what behavior shows it; Metric, what number captures that behavior. Pick one primary metric plus supporting ones, and sanity-check across the adoption-engagement-retention ladder. Do this, and you'll never again be caught flat when someone asks "but did it actually work?"

[VISUAL: Header: "THE #1 MISTAKE — MEASURING USAGE, NOT VALUE." A feature with high clicks but a sad user.]

EV: Let me save you from the single most common mistake on feature metrics, because almost everyone makes it — measuring *usage* instead of *value*. Here's the trap. You build a feature and proudly measure "number of clicks on the feature." It's high. Success? Not necessarily. What if people are clicking it because they're *confused* and can't find what they want? High usage can mean high *frustration*, not high value. Picture a search feature where "searches per user" keeps climbing — sounds great, until you realize it's climbing because the *first* search never gives people what they need, so they search again and again. The usage went up because the feature is *failing*. So always connect your metric back to the *goal*, which is about user value, not activity. Ask "does more of this number mean users are better off, or just busier?" If a rising number could secretly mean rising frustration, you're measuring usage, not value — go back to the goal and fix your metric.
