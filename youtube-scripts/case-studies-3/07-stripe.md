EV: Picture it's around 2010 and you're a developer who just wants to accept a credit card payment on your website. Sounds simple. It was a nightmare. You had to apply to a bank for a "merchant account," wait weeks for approval, decipher ancient documentation, wire together clunky systems, and wrestle with endless compliance paperwork. It could take *weeks* and make you want to quit. Then two brothers looked at that misery and asked, "why isn't this just a few lines of code?" They made it exactly that — and built one of the most valuable startups in the world by obsessing over one very specific person. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "STRIPE — obsess over your real user."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Stripe — it's the thing that lets websites and apps accept payments; a huge chunk of the internet's checkout runs on it, quietly, in the background. But the thing worth studying isn't payments. It's *who* Stripe decided to serve, and how fanatically. Here's the principle: **find out who your real user actually is — not who you assume — and obsess over removing every scrap of friction for *them.* The company that makes a painful thing effortless for a specific user wins that user completely.** Let me show you how Patrick and John Collison did it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHO IS THE REAL CUSTOMER?" A business owner wants payments — but the person actually *implementing* it, suffering the pain, is the developer at the keyboard.]

EV: First, the crucial insight about *who* to serve. When you think "online payments," you might picture the business owner who wants money to come in. But Stripe saw something sharper: the person who actually *feels the pain*, who has to make payments work, who will *choose* the tool, is the *developer* — the engineer at the keyboard wiring it up. The old payment companies treated developers as an afterthought; they sold to executives in boardrooms and handed engineers terrible tools to suffer through. Stripe flipped it: the developer is the real customer. Win the developer's heart, and the business follows. Identifying your *true* user — the one who feels the pain and makes the choice — is step one, and most companies get it wrong.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE OBSESSION — RADICAL SIMPLICITY FOR DEVELOPERS." The famous seven lines of code glow on screen; a payment works instantly; beautiful documentation unfurls like a welcome mat.]

EV: And once they knew the developer was the customer, Stripe obsessed — almost fanatically — over the developer's experience. The legendary example: they got accepting a payment down to about *seven lines of code* you could copy, paste, and have working in minutes, versus the weeks it used to take. But it went deeper than that. Stripe wrote documentation so clear and beautiful that developers actually *enjoyed* reading it — famously treating the docs as a core product, not an afterthought. Clean tools, instant setup, everything designed to make a developer's life easy and even delightful. They sweated details most payment companies didn't even know existed, because they understood that for a developer, friction is the enemy and elegance is love. They didn't just reduce the pain. They made the painful thing a *pleasure.*

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY DELIGHTING DEVELOPERS WAS GENIUS." A developer tries Stripe, loves it, tells other developers, uses it at their next company and the next — spreading it bottom-up.]

EV: Now here's why targeting developers was strategic genius, not just nice. Developers *talk.* They trust each other far more than they trust ads or salespeople, and when an engineer finds a tool that saves them from weeks of pain, they evangelize it — to their team, on forums, at the next startup they join. So by delighting developers, Stripe got something priceless: bottom-up, word-of-mouth growth through the most trusted channel in tech. A developer would pick Stripe for a weekend project, love it, and then bring it into their company — flipping the old model where you had to sell to the boss from the top down. The product spread *because* the real user loved it. Delight the right user deeply, and they become your sales force.

[VISUAL: Header: "FROM 7 LINES TO THE BACKBONE." Stripe expands outward: subscriptions, fraud protection, payouts, business banking — a whole financial toolkit, all as easy as the first seven lines.]

EV: And here's how that beachhead became an empire. Once Stripe owned the developer's trust and became the easy default for *taking a payment*, it expanded outward — adding everything else a business needs: handling subscriptions, fighting fraud, paying out to sellers, even tools to start and run a company. And it built each new thing with the *same* obsession: make it radically simple for the developer. So Stripe went from "the easy way to accept a card" to the financial backbone that a huge portion of internet businesses run on — quietly powering checkout for startups and giants alike. The seven lines of code were the wedge. The relentless focus on developer simplicity was the thing that let them keep expanding without ever losing the magic that won the user in the first place.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Stripe one of the most valuable private companies on earth, processing trillions in payments; pushing into AI tools for commerce and into stablecoins/crypto rails; still privately held, still developer-obsessed.]

EV: And now? Stripe is one of the most valuable private companies in the world, processing *trillions* of dollars a year for millions of businesses — and still, notably, privately held, doing things its own patient way. It's moved into powering payments for the new wave of AI companies, and made big bets on stablecoins and crypto rails as the next form of moving money, acquiring its way into that future. But through all of it, the founding obsession holds: make moving money as simple as possible for the developer. The whole trillion-dollar machine traces straight back to two brothers who refused to accept that accepting a payment had to be miserable, and decided to serve the one user everyone else ignored. Developer-first was the whole strategy, and it still is.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Find your real user. Remove every ounce of their friction."]

EV: So the principle to carry: first, identify your *true* user — not who writes the check, but who actually feels the pain and makes the choice. Stripe saw it was the developer, not the executive. Then obsess, fanatically, over removing every ounce of friction for that user — make the painful thing not just easy but *delightful*, down to the documentation. Because when you win the heart of the person who actually chooses, two things happen: they stay, and they tell everyone. Serve your real user so well they can't help but spread you. That's a growth engine no ad budget can buy.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you drive adoption for a technical / developer product?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a sharp, modern answer. If they ask how you'd grow a technical or developer product, don't say "run ads" or "hire salespeople." Say: "I'd identify the real decision-maker — for developer tools, that's the developer who feels the pain and chooses the tool — and obsess over their experience: radical simplicity, instant setup, documentation so good it's a product in itself. Stripe got accepting payments down to seven lines of code and treated docs as core." Then land it: "Delight the real user deeply and you get bottom-up, word-of-mouth adoption through the most trusted channel there is — users telling other users." That shows you understand developer-led, product-led growth.

Find who truly feels the pain and makes the choice, and remove every scrap of their friction. Serve the right user that well, and they'll build your company for you.
