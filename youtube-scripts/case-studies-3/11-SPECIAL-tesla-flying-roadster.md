EV: Two days ago, a car flew. Not a render, not a promise — a real Tesla Roadster prototype, on Thursday night at a SpaceX facility in Texas, drove up a ramp, flipped upside down, fired rocket thrusters built with SpaceX, launched into the air, flipped, hovered for a second, and landed on its wheels — leaping clean over a row of other Teslas. The internet lost its mind. Now, is it *actually* a flying car you'll buy next year? No. But the *strategy* behind this stunt is one of the smartest and most important moves in product, and it has nothing to do with flying. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "TESLA ROADSTER — the halo product."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar — and this one's a special, because it just happened and it's too good a lesson to wait on. What you're looking at is what's called a *halo product.* And here's the principle I want you to take: **a spectacular, barely-real "halo" product isn't built to sell units — it's built to sell the *vision*, re-rate the whole brand, and remind the world who you are. The product is marketing. The discipline is not crossing from vision into broken promises.** Let me break down what actually happened and why it's genius.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED." A clean spec card builds: SpaceX cold-gas thrusters, ~10 jets, ~600 liters of compressed air, 0–60 in under 1 second, 230+ mph claimed, no driver on board.]

EV: Here's the real breakdown. Tesla unveiled the next-generation Roadster at SpaceX's test site. The "flight" came from SpaceX technology bolted into a car — around ten cold-gas thrusters, fed by roughly six hundred liters of ultra-high-pressure compressed air, giving it just enough thrust to launch off a ramp, flip, hover briefly, and set back down. Musk claims some versions will do zero to sixty in *under one second* and top over two hundred and thirty miles per hour. And importantly — there was *no driver* in the car during the flip, done, in Musk's words, "to make sure we don't kill anyone." So: a genuinely jaw-dropping piece of engineering theater. Now watch what it's *for.*

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY: THE HALO EFFECT." The flying Roadster glows at the top; its light pours down onto the ordinary cars below — Model 3, Model Y — making the whole lineup look advanced and desirable.]

EV: This is the halo effect, and every great brand uses some version of it. You build one insane, headline-grabbing product at the very top — a flagship that most people will never buy — and its glamour pours *down* over your entire ordinary lineup. A flying Roadster makes the regular Model 3 in the showroom feel like it was made by the company that builds *flying cars.* It doesn't need to sell in volume; it needs to make the whole brand feel futuristic, daring, and alive. Sports-car makers do this with hypercars. Phone makers do it with the one impossible foldable. The halo product's job isn't revenue — it's *meaning.* It redefines, in one spectacle, what people believe your brand is capable of.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TIMING IS THE TELL." A backdrop of tough headlines — softer EV demand, fierce rivals, a bruised brand — and then the flying car bursts through it, flipping the conversation.]

EV: And here's the part that reveals the real strategy — the *timing.* Tesla, lately, has been under pressure: softer demand, brutal competition from rivals like BYD, and a brand that's taken some hits. The story had turned negative. Then — this. Overnight, the conversation flips from "is Tesla in trouble?" to "did you see the Tesla that *flies?*" Tesla's stock jumped on the reveal. That's the halo product as a *narrative weapon*: when the story about you turns sour, a bold enough vision can seize the narrative back and remind everyone — customers, investors, employees — why they believed in you in the first place. It's not a distraction. It's a deliberate reframe of the entire story.

[VISUAL: Header: "SELLING THE FUTURE — $50K DEPOSITS." A "Reserve Now" screen; deposits flowing in for a car that won't ship until ~2028, funding the dream in advance.]

EV: Because here's the next move: Tesla is taking fifty-thousand-dollar deposits, right now, for a car it says will enter production around 2028. Think about what that does. It funds the future with customers' money, it creates a public list of true believers, and it manufactures scarcity and desire years before the thing exists. This is selling the *vision* and letting the most passionate buyers pre-commit to it. It's a powerful tool — Tesla has done it before with earlier Roadster and Cybertruck reservations. Casting a bold, concrete vision of the future gets people to fund it, rally behind it, and wait for it. You're not selling a product. You're selling *belief in a future*, and belief is something people will literally pre-pay for.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CATCH — VISION vs. OVERPROMISE." A tightrope: on one side 'inspiring vision,' on the other 'broken promise.' The flying car balances on the wire.]

EV: But — and this is the honest, crucial part — there's a razor's edge here, and it's the whole lesson. What was shown was a *remotely-controlled, unoccupied prototype* on a private SpaceX site. It is *not* a street-legal car cleared to fly over public roads, and there's no confirmed price, no firm ship date, and no guarantee the flight hardware ever makes it onto a car you can buy. Flying cars face enormous regulatory and safety hurdles. So the same move that's brilliant marketing is dangerous if the gap between the dazzling demo and the shipped reality grows too wide, or stretches too long. Vision inspires; *overpromising* erodes trust. Tesla has a real history of thrilling demos and timelines that then slip. A halo product rallies people — but every bold promise you make is a debt you'll eventually be asked to pay. Cast the vision; just know the bill comes due.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THIS SITS (RIGHT NOW)." The flying Roadster as the loud, shiny top of a strategy stack; beneath it, the real business — the robotaxi, the mass-market cars — quietly carrying the weight.]

EV: So where does this sit, as of right now? The flying Roadster is the spectacle at the top — the thing that grabs the world's attention and re-rates the brand. Underneath it, the actual business still has to deliver: the mass-market cars, the robotaxi bet, the real revenue. The halo creates belief and buys attention and goodwill; the ordinary products have to convert that belief into a company that works. That's the balance every brand using a halo product walks: let the flagship *inspire*, but make sure the real lineup *delivers*, so the vision and the reality eventually meet. A flying car that re-energizes the brand is only a win if the cars people actually buy live up to the excitement it created.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Build a halo to sell the vision — but don't let the promise outrun the product."]

EV: So the principle to carry from this one: you don't win attention and belief with a spreadsheet of incremental improvements. Sometimes you need a *halo* — one bold, almost unreasonable thing at the top that redefines what people believe your brand can do, and makes your whole ordinary lineup glow. Use it to seize the narrative, to rally believers, even to fund the future. But respect the tightrope: a vision inspires only as long as people trust you'll eventually deliver it. The art is dreaming big enough to move people, without promising so big, or so soon, that the gap breaks their faith in you.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you re-energize a brand that's lost its momentum?"]

EV: And in an interview — especially if this is still in the news, this is a killer, current answer. If they ask how you'd re-energize a brand that's lost momentum, don't say "new ad campaign." Say: "I'd consider a *halo* product — one bold flagship, even one most customers won't buy, whose job is to redefine what people believe the brand can do and let that glow lift the whole lineup. Tesla just did exactly this with the flying Roadster to seize the narrative back." Then show judgment: "But I'd be disciplined about the gap between the demo and what ships — a halo inspires, but overpromising erodes trust, so the vision has to stay tethered to something we can actually deliver." Citing a two-day-old example *and* naming its risk makes you sound sharp, current, and mature.
