EV: In the video game console business, there's a war that's raged for decades, and it's almost always fought on one axis: *power.* Better graphics, faster processors, more horsepower — each giant trying to out-muscle the other, and gamers comparing spec sheets like boxers at a weigh-in. Now, one company — Nintendo — was often "losing" that power race; its consoles were frequently *less* powerful than the competition. And yet, again and again, Nintendo produced some of the best-selling machines in history. How do you win a war you're losing? Simple — you stop fighting it, and start a different one. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "NINTENDO — blue ocean strategy."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Nintendo you know — Mario, Zelda, the Switch, the Wii. But the thing worth studying isn't the games. It's the *strategic choice* to refuse the fight everyone else was having. Here's the principle, and it has a name — "blue ocean strategy": **instead of competing in a bloody, crowded market on the dimension everyone's fighting over, create a new, uncontested space by competing on a *different* dimension entirely.** Don't fight for a bigger slice of the red ocean. Go find a blue one. Let me show you how Nintendo did this, twice.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE RED OCEAN — THE POWER WAR." Two rival consoles locked in an endless arms race: more teraflops, sharper pixels, aimed at the same hardcore gamers, margins bleeding red.]

EV: First, the trap Nintendo refused to enter. The console war between the big power players was a classic "red ocean" — bloody, brutal, everyone competing on the *same* thing (raw power) for the *same* customer (the hardcore gamer who wanted the best graphics). In that fight, you win only by out-spending and out-muscling, margins get crushed, and there's a real ceiling: you're all chasing the same limited pool of serious gamers. Nintendo looked at that bloodbath and made a radical call: *we're not going to win a power contest, so we won't enter one.* Instead of asking "how do we build a more powerful console?", they asked a completely different question: "who is *not* playing games at all, and why?"

[VISUAL: Header: "THE WII — A NEW OCEAN OF PLAYERS." A living room fills with people who never game — grandparents, little kids, moms — all standing up, swinging Wii remotes, laughing together.]

EV: That question led to the Wii. Nintendo noticed a *huge* group of people the power war completely ignored: everyone who found games intimidating or irrelevant — grandparents, young kids, parents, non-gamers. Why didn't they play? Because games were complex, with controllers covered in buttons, and graphics-obsessed worlds that didn't appeal to them. So the Wii wasn't more powerful — it was *less* powerful, deliberately. Instead, it introduced simple *motion controls* — you swing the remote like a tennis racket, roll it like a bowling ball. Suddenly your grandma could play. Games became social, physical, something a whole family did together in the living room. Nintendo didn't fight for the hardcore gamer. It created a *brand-new ocean* of millions of people who'd never bought a console in their lives. The Wii became a global phenomenon, outselling the "superior" machines.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY LESS POWER WAS THE POINT." A chart: competing on power = crowded, expensive, capped. Competing on 'fun and accessible for everyone' = wide open, cheaper to build, huge new audience.]

EV: And here's why going *less* powerful was genius, not a compromise. By not chasing cutting-edge power, Nintendo's hardware was cheaper to build, which meant they could sell it at a healthy profit — while the rivals often *lost* money on each powerful console, hoping to make it back later. So Nintendo had better economics *and* a bigger audience *and* no direct competition in its new space, because nobody else was even trying to reach non-gamers. Think about how complete that win is: they escaped the price war, escaped the spec war, and opened a market many times larger than the one everyone was fighting over. Less power wasn't a weakness they overcame. It was the *strategy* — the thing that freed them to compete on fun and accessibility instead of horsepower.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE SWITCH — A NEW DIMENSION AGAIN." A console docked to a TV at home, then lifted out and carried onto a train, into a park — the same games, now anywhere.]

EV: And Nintendo proved it wasn't a fluke by doing it *again* with the Switch. Once more, they didn't try to out-power the rivals. They found a *new* dimension: *flexibility.* The Switch is a console you play on your big TV at home — and then lift out of its dock and carry with you, instantly becoming a handheld you play on the train, in bed, anywhere. One device, two worlds, seamlessly. Nobody else was competing on "play the same great games at home *and* on the go." Again, not the most powerful machine — but it opened a new space between home consoles and mobile gaming that was uniquely Nintendo's. The Switch became one of the best-selling consoles of all time. Same playbook: don't fight on power, change the dimension of the game.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." The Switch 2 launches to massive demand; Nintendo expanding its worlds into movies, theme parks, and mobile — Mario everywhere, still refusing the raw-power race.]

EV: And now? Nintendo launched the Switch 2 to enormous demand — a careful evolution of that same play-anywhere idea, still not trying to be the most powerful box in the room, still winning. And it's taken its *other* great asset — beloved characters like Mario and Zelda — far beyond consoles: blockbuster movies, theme-park worlds, mobile games, merchandise everywhere. So Nintendo competes on things its rivals *can't* easily copy: unique, delightful experiences and characters people love across generations, rather than raw specs anyone can buy. Decades in, the discipline holds — while others fight over teraflops, Nintendo keeps finding and owning blue oceans. It never won the power war. It just kept refusing to fight it, and kept winning anyway.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Don't fight the crowded war. Change the dimension and own a new ocean."]

EV: So the principle to carry: when you're in a crowded market where everyone competes on the *same* thing — price, power, speed, features — you don't have to win that fight. In fact, if you're not the biggest, you probably *can't.* Instead, ask the blue-ocean questions: "who is this market *ignoring*? What dimension is *nobody* competing on? What if I made this simpler, more accessible, more fun, more flexible — instead of just more powerful?" The goal isn't a bigger slice of the bloody red ocean. It's to create a new, uncontested blue ocean where you're the only one, and the audience is far larger than the one everyone's fighting over.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "You're entering a market dominated by bigger, better-resourced competitors. What do you do?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a sophisticated answer. If they say "you're entering a market with bigger, stronger competitors," do NOT say "we'll build a better version." Say: "I wouldn't compete on the dimension the giants dominate — I'd lose that. I'd use blue-ocean strategy: find a *new* dimension they're ignoring and an audience they're not serving, and compete there. Nintendo was 'losing' the console power war, so it stopped competing on power and won on accessibility with the Wii, reaching millions of non-gamers nobody else wanted." Then land it: "Against a stronger incumbent, you don't want a fairer fight on their turf — you want a different fight on *yours.*" That shows you understand differentiation and market creation, not just competition.

[VISUAL: Recap: the two giants flexing specs, Nintendo walking off the battlefield, the Wii filling a living room, the Switch going portable, the characters spreading into movies and parks.]

EV: So — Nintendo. It was "losing" the war everyone was fighting — the race for raw power — so it refused to fight it, and instead created new, uncontested oceans: the Wii reaching millions who'd never gamed, the Switch blending home and portable. Less powerful, on purpose, and a bigger winner for it. Don't fight the crowded war on the enemy's terms. Change the dimension, and go find your blue ocean. That's how you win by refusing to compete.
