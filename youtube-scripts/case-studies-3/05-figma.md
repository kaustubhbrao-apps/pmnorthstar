EV: Here's a David-and-Goliath story. For decades, professional design software was owned by one giant — Adobe — and it was single-player. One designer, one computer, one file, which you'd then export and email around, drowning in versions called "final-final-v7." Everyone accepted that too. Then a startup showed up and beat the giant, not by building better design tools — Adobe's were excellent — but by changing something far more fundamental: they made design *multiplayer*, a whole team working on the same thing at once, live, in a web browser. Everyone said that was technically impossible. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "FIGMA — collaboration as a moat."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Figma — if you've seen a design tool where a bunch of people's cursors move around the same screen like a Google Doc, that's it. But the thing worth studying isn't the features. It's the *strategic insight* about where the real pain was. Here's the principle: **sometimes you don't beat an incumbent by being better at what they're good at — you win by changing the dimension of competition entirely.** Adobe competed on tools. Figma competed on *collaboration.* Let me show you why that was unbeatable.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE REAL PAIN WASN'T DRAWING — IT WAS SHARING." A designer's actual day: 20% drawing, 80% exporting, emailing, version-chaos, feedback lost in comments, handoff to engineers breaking.]

EV: First, the insight. Adobe obsessed over the *drawing* — more brushes, more filters, more power for the lone designer. And they were great at it. But Figma's founders noticed something: the designer's biggest *pain* wasn't the drawing. It was everything *around* it. Design is a team sport — designers, product managers, engineers, all need to see and shape the work. And the old single-player model made that agony: export, email, version confusion, feedback scattered everywhere, the engineer building from an outdated file. Most of the frustration lived in the *collaboration*, not the creation. Adobe was polishing the 20% that worked and ignoring the 80% that hurt. Figma aimed straight at the pain everyone else had stopped seeing because they assumed it was just how design worked.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BET — DESIGN IN THE BROWSER." Experts shaking their heads: 'serious design can't run in a browser.' Behind them, Figma's team quietly building exactly that.]

EV: And here's the part that took enormous courage: to make design truly multiplayer and instantly shareable, Figma bet on building it inside a *web browser* — a link, not an install. At the time, this was considered borderline impossible. Browsers were thought to be far too slow and weak to run heavy, professional design work; real tools *had* to be installed desktop software. Figma spent *years* — quietly, with little to show — solving incredibly hard technical problems to make high-performance design run smoothly in a browser. It was a huge, patient, unglamorous bet. But that browser foundation was the *only* way to deliver the real prize: open a link, and you're instantly in the same live file as your whole team. The hard technical bet existed to serve the strategic insight.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MAGIC — MANY CURSORS, ONE FILE." A link opens; suddenly five named cursors are moving on the same canvas, editing live, commenting in place, no exporting, no versions.]

EV: And when it worked, the magic was obvious the second you saw it. You send a link. Your teammate clicks it and is *instantly* in the exact same file as you — no download, no "which version is this," no export. You see their cursor moving. They see yours. A product manager drops a comment right on the button that's wrong. An engineer inspects the exact design to build from, always current. Design stopped being a lonely file you passed around and became a shared, living space the whole team inhabited together — exactly like Google Docs did for writing. That wasn't a better *feature* than Adobe. It was a better *model* of how design actually happens. And once a team worked that way, going back to emailing files felt prehistoric.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY IT BECAME A MOAT." A whole org's files, teams, and shared workflow living inside Figma; pulling it out would mean ripping out how everyone works together.]

EV: Here's why "multiplayer" wasn't just nice — it was a *moat.* A single-player tool is a personal choice; you can switch anytime. But once an entire *organization* collaborates in Figma — all the files, the shared libraries, the whole team's way of working together living in one place — switching away means disrupting how *everyone* works, not just one person. The value isn't in any one user; it's in the *network* of the whole team being there. That's a collaboration network effect, and it's incredibly sticky. It also drove growth for free: to work with someone in Figma, you invite them *into* Figma, so the product spread team by team, company by company, on its own. Adobe could copy Figma's features. It could not easily copy the fact that your entire team already lived and worked inside Figma together.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Adobe tries to buy Figma for ~$20B; regulators block it; Figma stays independent and goes public; now adding AI design tools and racing new rivals.]

EV: And now? The ultimate validation: Adobe, the giant, agreed to *buy* Figma for around twenty billion dollars — a stunning price that basically admitted Figma had out-strategized it. Regulators blocked the deal over competition concerns, so Figma stayed independent and went public on its own, one of the most anticipated tech listings in years. Today it's pushing into AI-assisted design — tools that generate and edit designs from prompts — while new competitors try the same "change the game" move on *it.* The story's lesson is sealed: a startup beat one of software's great giants not by winning the feature war, but by spotting that the real pain was collaboration and making a hard, patient bet to own it. Change the dimension of competition, and size stops protecting the incumbent.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Don't beat them at their game. Change the game."]

EV: So the principle to carry: when you're facing a strong incumbent, don't line up to beat them at the thing they're already great at — you'll usually lose. Instead, find the pain *around* the product that everyone has accepted as normal, and compete on a *new* dimension entirely. For Adobe it was tools; Figma changed the game to collaboration. Ask: "what does everyone in this category assume is just 'how it works' — and what if I challenged *that* instead of the features?" The way to beat a giant is rarely a better version of their game. It's a different game where their strength doesn't count.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you compete against a dominant, established incumbent?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a genuinely impressive answer. If they ask how you'd take on a dominant incumbent, don't say "build better features." Say: "I wouldn't fight them on their strength — I'd change the dimension of competition. I'd look for the pain *around* the core product that everyone's accepted as normal and win there. Figma beat Adobe not with better drawing tools but by making design collaborative and browser-based — a new game where Adobe's lead didn't transfer." Then land it: "And I'd pick a dimension that creates a network effect, like team collaboration, so it becomes a moat, not just a feature." That shows you think about *reframing* competition, not just out-executing it.

[VISUAL: Recap: the lonely designer and version chaos, the pain-around-the-product insight, the hard browser bet, the swarm of live cursors, the collaboration moat, the $20B offer.]

EV: So — Figma. It beat a giant not by building better tools, but by noticing that the real pain was collaboration, making a hard, years-long bet to run design live in the browser, and turning "multiplayer" into a moat an entire organization lived inside. Don't beat the incumbent at their game. Find the pain they ignore, and change the game. That's how David takes down Goliath.
