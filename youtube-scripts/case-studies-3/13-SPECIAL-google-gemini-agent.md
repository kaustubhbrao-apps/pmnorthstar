Write and debug code. Draft and format documents. Generate images. Google pitched it, essentially, as an AI that acts like a *colleague* rather than a chatbot. [VISUAL: Header: "INSTRUCTIONS vs. OUTCOMES." Left: a human micro-managing every click of a tool. Right: a human stating a goal and reviewing the finished result. The arrow moves right.]

EV: So let's name the shift precisely, because it's the whole lesson. Old model: you operate a *tool.* You know the steps, and the software just makes each step faster — you're still the worker, driving every action. New model: you delegate to an *agent.* You describe the *outcome* you want, and it figures out and executes the steps. Think of the difference between *driving* a car and *telling a driver where to go.* One, you control every input. The other, you specify the destination and judge whether you arrived. That's the agentic shift: from managing *inputs* to specifying *outputs.* And it quietly flips your role from *operator* to *manager* — you're now directing and reviewing work, not performing every step of it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY THIS REWIRES PRODUCT DESIGN." Old product: lots of buttons and screens to operate. New product: a way to state a goal, watch the agent work, and approve or correct — trust and oversight become the interface.]

EV: And here's why this rewires how you build products. If users are *operating* your software, you design lots of buttons, screens, and controls for every step. But if an *agent* is doing the work, the human doesn't need all those controls — they need something else entirely: a way to clearly state the goal, *visibility* into what the agent is doing, and the ability to *trust, check, and correct* it. The interface stops being a cockpit full of controls and becomes more like a *manager's dashboard* — set the objective, watch progress, approve or intervene. So the key product questions change from "is this easy to operate?" to "do users trust it to act on their behalf, and can they supervise it well?" Designing for *delegation* is a completely different craft than designing for *operation.*

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CATCH — TRUST AND THE COST OF A MISTAKE." An agent confidently completes a task — but one step is wrong, and because it ran autonomously, the error propagated through everything after it.]

EV: But let's be honest about the hard part, because delegation raises the stakes. When you *operate* a tool, you catch mistakes as you go — you're in every step. When you *delegate* to an agent, it runs ahead on its own, which means a mistake early on can quietly propagate through everything after it before you even look. The faster and more autonomous the agent, the *bigger* the blast radius if it gets something wrong — a wrong number in a report, a bug shipped in code, an email sent you didn't mean. So the central challenge of this whole era isn't capability — the agents are capable. It's *trust and oversight*: giving people the right visibility, the right checkpoints, and the right ability to undo, so that delegating work doesn't mean losing control of it. Autonomy without good oversight isn't a feature. It's a liability.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THIS SITS (RIGHT NOW)." Google, OpenAI, Microsoft, Anthropic all racing to ship workplace agents; businesses cautiously piloting them; the definition of 'knowledge work' starting to move.]

EV: So where does this sit, right now? Google's workplace agent is live and squarely aimed at businesses, and the whole industry is racing in the same direction this very month. Companies are cautiously piloting these agents — excited by the productivity, wary of the risks. We're at the very beginning, and today's agents are impressive but still make mistakes and still need supervision. But the direction is unmistakable: more and more work will be *delegated* to AI agents, with humans shifting into setting goals and reviewing results. If you build products — or if you just *work* — the skill to start building now is *delegation*: how to clearly specify what you want, and how to verify what you got. That's rapidly becoming one of the most valuable skills there is.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Design for delegation, not operation. Win on trust and oversight."]

EV: So the principle to carry: we're moving from software you *operate* to software you *delegate* to — from giving instructions to giving goals. If you build products, stop assuming the user wants to drive every step; start asking what they'd happily *hand off* entirely, and design for that — clear goals in, visible and trustworthy work out, easy oversight and correction. The winner in the agent era won't be whoever's AI is the most *capable.* It'll be whoever makes delegation feel *safe* — the one users actually trust to act on their behalf. Capability gets you in the room. Trust wins the job.

Then land it: "The moat won't be raw capability — everyone will have capable models — it'll be *trust*: making people confident enough to hand real work to the agent. That's the product problem."
