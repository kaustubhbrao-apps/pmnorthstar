EV: Here's something wild. The core technology behind ChatGPT — the powerful AI language models — had existed, in some form, for *years* before ChatGPT. Researchers had it. Companies had it. It was sitting right there. And almost nobody outside a lab could use it. Then one team wrapped that same technology in the simplest thing imaginable — a chat box you just *talk* to — and in two months it hit a hundred million users, the fastest-growing consumer product in history. The breakthrough wasn't the brain. It was the *doorway.* Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "CHATGPT — the power of the interface."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. ChatGPT you know — you type, it answers, it writes, it explains. But the thing worth studying isn't the AI itself. It's the *packaging* — how a simple conversational interface unlocked something the world already had but couldn't touch. Here's the principle, and it's one of the most underrated truths in all of product: **the interface can matter more than the technology. The thing that wins isn't always the most powerful tech — it's the one people can actually use.** Let me show you why that's so profound.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LOCKED-AWAY POWER." Powerful AI shown as a complex control panel of code, API keys, and parameters — brilliant, but behind a wall only engineers can climb.]

EV: So picture the situation before ChatGPT. The powerful models existed, but to use them you basically had to be a developer — write code, call an API, tune settings, understand what a "token" and a "temperature" were. It was a cockpit built for pilots. For everyone else — the writer, the student, the small-business owner, the curious kid — this incredible capability may as well not have existed, because there was no door they could walk through. This is a trap product people fall into constantly: they build enormous power and assume power is enough. But power locked behind complexity reaches almost no one. The capability was there. The *access* wasn't.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE UNLOCK — JUST TALK TO IT." The cockpit dissolves into a single friendly chat box. A grandparent, a student, a chef all type plain English and get instant answers.]

EV: And here's the move that changed everything, and notice how almost *insultingly* simple it is. They took that same powerful model and put a chat interface in front of it. No code. No settings. No manual. You just… talk to it, in plain language, the way you'd text a friend. "Help me write this email." "Explain quantum physics simply." "Give me a recipe with what's in my fridge." That's it. Suddenly the capability that was locked in a developer's cockpit was available to *anyone who could type a sentence* — which is to say, everyone. They didn't make the AI more powerful. They made it *reachable.* And reach is what turns a technology into a phenomenon.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY 'JUST TYPE' IS GENIUS." A universal language — plain conversation — shown as the one interface every human already knows, no learning curve at all.]

EV: And think about *why* conversation specifically was the masterstroke. Every other powerful tool makes you learn *its* language — its buttons, its menus, its syntax. A chat box flips that: it speaks *your* language. There's zero learning curve, because you already know how to have a conversation. You've been doing it your whole life. That's the lowest-friction interface that has ever existed — the time between "I wonder if it can…" and actually doing it is basically zero. No onboarding, no tutorial, no manual. The genius wasn't inventing a new way to interact. It was choosing the one interaction every single human already mastered as a toddler.

[VISUAL: Header: "SIMPLICITY IS A GROWTH ENGINE." A single screenshot of a funny or brilliant ChatGPT reply gets shared, screenshotted, posted — spreading person to person across the internet.]

EV: Here's the second-order magic. Because *anyone* could use it, and because the results were often surprising, funny, or genuinely useful, people couldn't stop *sharing* them. Screenshots of ChatGPT writing a poem, explaining a hard idea, acing a test — flooding social media. And every share was an ad that needed no explanation, because the product was so simple there was nothing *to* explain. You just saw the result and thought "wait, I want to try that," and you could, in ten seconds, for free. The simplicity didn't just drive adoption — it drove *word of mouth*, because a product everyone can use is a product everyone can talk about. Low friction to use became low friction to spread.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." The humble chat box grows — voice, images, video with Sora, agents that take actions; hundreds of millions of weekly users; a crowded field of rivals all copying the chat interface.]

EV: And now? That simple text box became one of the fastest, biggest platforms on earth — hundreds of millions of people using it every week, a massive business, newer models, and the interface expanding: you can talk to it with your voice, show it images, generate video, even have it act as an *agent* that does multi-step tasks for you. But watch the strategic lesson echo: the entire industry — Google, Anthropic, everyone — raced to copy not just the model, but the *chat interface.* The conversational doorway became the default way humans interact with AI, full stop. OpenAI's lead now rests as much on being the household name, the default door people walk through, as on the raw model underneath — which is exactly the point. The interface was the moat all along.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Reach beats raw power. Win on access."]

EV: So the principle to carry: don't fall in love with how powerful your technology is. Fall in love with how *reachable* it is. The winning product is often not the most advanced one — it's the one that removes the most friction between a person and the value. Ask, always: "who *can't* use this yet, and what's the simplest possible door I could build for them?" Sometimes the highest-leverage work isn't making the engine stronger. It's wrapping the engine you already have in an interface so simple that the whole world can finally walk in.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "We have powerful technology but low adoption. What do you do?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a razor-sharp answer. If they say "we've got powerful tech but nobody's adopting it," don't say "add more capability." Say: "I'd assume the problem is the interface, not the power. I'd find the friction between the user and the value and crush it — ideally by meeting users in a language they already know, the way ChatGPT used plain conversation to make advanced AI usable by anyone." Then land it: "The most powerful tech loses to the most *accessible* tech. I'd compete on reach, not just raw capability." That tells an interviewer you understand that adoption, not sophistication, is what makes a product win.

The interface beat the technology. Build the door, not just the engine.
