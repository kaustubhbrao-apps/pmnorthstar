EV: For decades, making something beautifully designed — a poster, a presentation, a logo — required two things most people didn't have: expensive professional software, and years of training to use it. The tools were powerful but brutally intimidating, built for experts. Everyone else just… couldn't. One founder looked at that and thought: what if *anyone* could design something beautiful, in minutes, with no training at all? She pitched that idea to investors and got told *no* over a hundred times. Today her company is used by hundreds of millions of people. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "CANVA — democratize the hard thing."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Canva — if you've ever made a quick poster, an Instagram post, or a presentation by just dragging things around, that's it. But the thing worth studying is the *mission* underneath it. Here's the principle, and it's one of the most reliable ways to build something huge: **take a skill that's locked behind expertise and expensive tools, and make it so simple that anyone can do it. Democratizing a hard thing unlocks a market many times bigger than the experts ever were.** Let me show you how Melanie Perkins did it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LOCKED DOOR." Professional design tools shown as a members-only club: expensive, complex, guarded by years of training. A huge crowd of ordinary people stuck outside.]

EV: First, the opportunity she saw. Professional design software was incredibly powerful — but it was a members-only club. It cost a lot, and it was so complex you needed real training to use it, which is why designers are professionals. So think about who was locked *outside* that door: every small-business owner who needed a flyer, every teacher making a worksheet, every student with a presentation, every person who just wanted a nice birthday invite. Millions and millions of people who had a design *need* but no design *skill.* The experts inside the club were a small group. The people locked outside were basically *everyone.* Perkins saw that the real market wasn't the professionals — it was the enormous, ignored crowd who'd been told design wasn't for them.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE UNLOCK — TEMPLATES + DRAG AND DROP." A blank, scary canvas transforms into a grid of ready-made beautiful templates; a user just drags, drops, swaps text, done.]

EV: And here's how Canva unlocked the door. Two key moves. First: start from *templates*, not a blank page. A blank canvas is terrifying for a non-designer — where do you even begin? So Canva gives you hundreds of professionally-designed templates; you just pick one that's already beautiful and make it yours. The hard part — the actual design judgment — is baked in. Second: *drag and drop.* No cryptic tools, no manual — you just drag elements around, swap the text, change a color, and it looks good because the system guides you. Together, these mean a complete beginner produces something genuinely professional-looking in *minutes.* Canva didn't dumb design down. It did the expert thinking *for* you, and left you the fun, easy part. That's the art of democratizing a skill — hide the hard, expose the simple.

[VISUAL: Header: "FREE FOR EVERYONE — THE FREEMIUM ENGINE." Millions sign up free and make things; a slice upgrade to paid features and teams; the free users spread it everywhere.]

EV: And to actually reach everyone, Canva made it *free.* Anyone can sign up and design beautiful things for nothing — that's the freemium model. Why is that so powerful here? Because when the whole point is "design for *everyone*," a price tag at the door defeats the mission and the strategy. Free means explosive, word-of-mouth adoption — people make something, share it, others ask "how'd you make that?", and the circle widens. Then Canva makes money from the slice who want more: premium templates and images, and especially *teams and businesses* who need it for work and will happily pay. So a massive free base drives the growth, and a smaller paying base drives the revenue. The free users aren't a cost — they're the marketing engine that makes the whole thing spread.

[VISUAL: Header: "100 TIMES NO." A founder pitching; investor after investor shaking their heads — a counter ticking up: 47... 83... 100+ rejections. She keeps going.]

EV: Now the human core of this, and it's the part I most want you to hear. When Melanie Perkins started pitching this idea, investors didn't get it. She was reportedly rejected over a *hundred times.* A hundred "no"s. Imagine that — pitch after pitch, rejection after rejection, people telling you your vision won't work. Most people quit after five. She kept refining the pitch, kept believing in the mission, and kept going, for *years*, until the right people finally saw what she saw. And here's the lesson tucked inside: a bold idea that democratizes something almost always sounds "too simple" or "too obvious" to skeptics early on — the very thing that makes it huge later. Conviction through rejection isn't just inspirational fluff. For a genuinely new idea, persistence past the doubters is often the *requirement*, not a bonus.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Canva with hundreds of millions of users worldwide, a huge valuation; now packed with AI design tools (Magic Studio), pushing hard into big businesses, having acquired pro tools like Affinity.]

EV: And now? Canva is one of the most valuable startups in the world, used by *hundreds of millions* of people across nearly every country — the exact "everyone" Perkins imagined. And it's pressing its advantage: it's loaded up on AI design tools, where you can generate images, rewrite text, and create whole designs from a prompt — which makes design even *more* accessible, pushing the democratization further. It's moved upmarket into big enterprises, and even acquired professional-grade tools to serve serious designers too, so it spans from total beginner to pro. The mission never changed — empower the world to design — it just keeps widening the door. The idea a hundred investors rejected turned out to be one of the biggest in software.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Democratize a hard skill. The crowd outside is bigger than the club inside."]

EV: So the principle to carry: look for a valuable skill that's currently locked behind expertise, expensive tools, or complexity — and ask how you could make it so simple that *anyone* can do it. The people locked *outside* an expert skill are almost always a far bigger market than the experts inside it. Do the hard thinking for your user, expose only the easy, delightful part, and consider making it free to spread. And when the skeptics tell you it's too simple or won't work — remember that "too simple" is often exactly what a market-changing idea sounds like before it wins. Democratize the hard thing, and persist past the hundred no's.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you find a big, underserved market?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a strong answer. If they ask how you'd find a big underserved market, don't just say "do research." Say: "I'd look for a valuable skill that's locked behind expertise or expensive tools, and figure out how to make it simple enough for everyone — because the crowd *excluded* from an expert skill is usually a much larger market than the experts themselves. That's what Canva did with design: templates and drag-and-drop let non-designers make professional work, and freemium spread it to hundreds of millions." Then land it: "The biggest markets are often the people who've been told a thing 'isn't for them.'" That shows you think about expanding markets, not just fighting over existing ones.

[VISUAL: Recap: the locked design club, the crowd outside, templates and drag-and-drop opening the door, free spreading it, the hundred rejections, the global giant.]

EV: So — Canva. Design was powerful but locked behind expertise and expensive tools, so most people were shut out. Melanie Perkins made it effortless — templates, drag-and-drop, free for everyone — serving the enormous crowd the experts ignored, and she pushed through over a hundred rejections to do it. Take a hard skill, make it simple enough for anyone, and open the door to the people locked outside. That's how you build something for the whole world.
