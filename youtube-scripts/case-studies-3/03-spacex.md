EV: For the entire history of space travel, rockets were the most expensive disposable object ever made. You'd build a machine worth tens of millions of dollars, use it for about ten minutes, and then let it crash into the ocean and disappear. Forever. Imagine flying a jumbo jet once, across the Atlantic, and then throwing the entire plane away. That's how space worked. Everyone accepted it — it was just physics, just the cost of space. And then one company asked a stupidly simple question: "why can't we just… land it, and use it again?" Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "SPACEX — first principles."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. SpaceX you know — the rockets, the landings, Mars. But the thing worth studying isn't the engineering. It's the *way of thinking* that made the engineering happen. Here's the principle, and it's maybe the most powerful mental tool there is: **first-principles thinking — instead of accepting how things have always been done, break the problem down to its basic truths and rebuild from scratch.** Most people reason by analogy — "this is how it's done." The best reason from zero. Let me show you how that one habit rewrote the economics of space.

[VISUAL: Header: "REASONING BY ANALOGY — THE TRAP." A long line of rocket-makers, each copying the one before: "rockets are expensive and disposable because... rockets have always been expensive and disposable."]

EV: First, the trap almost everyone was in. The entire space industry reasoned by *analogy*: rockets are expensive and single-use because that's how rockets have always been. Every new rocket was basically a variation on the last one, inheriting the same assumption — you launch it, you lose it. Nobody seriously challenged it, because "that's just how it is" is an incredibly comfortable place to stop thinking. And this is the trap in *every* industry, not just rockets: we inherit the way things are done and treat it as a law of nature, when often it's just a habit nobody's questioned. The price feels fixed because everyone *agrees* it's fixed. Analogy says "match what exists." It never asks if what exists makes any sense.

[VISUAL: Header: "FIRST PRINCIPLES — BREAK IT TO ATOMS." A rocket dissolves into its raw materials — aluminum, titanium, fuel — with a price tag on each. The total is shockingly small compared to the finished rocket's price.]

EV: Here's the first-principles move, and it's beautiful. The question SpaceX reportedly asked was: forget what rockets *cost* — what are they *made of?* Break it down to the atoms. Aluminum, titanium, copper, carbon fiber, fuel. And when you add up the raw *material* cost of a rocket, it's a tiny fraction of what rockets actually sell for — a few percent. So the materials were cheap. The thing that made rockets absurdly expensive was *throwing the whole machine away every single time.* That reframes everything. The problem was never "space is expensive." The problem was "we destroy the vehicle on every trip." And *that* is a problem you can actually attack: if you could land the rocket and fly it again, the cost of space could fall by a staggering amount. First principles didn't just answer the question — it found the *real* question.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE INSANELY HARD PART — AND THE FAILURES." A montage of booster landing attempts exploding, tipping over, missing the pad — crash after crash — then, finally, one lands perfectly upright.]

EV: Now, knowing the answer and *achieving* it are very different things, and this is where most would quit. Landing a rocket is brutally hard — you're taking a slender tower the height of a building, falling from the edge of space at enormous speed, and trying to balance it upright on a tiny platform, sometimes a floating one in the middle of the ocean, using its own engines. SpaceX failed, publicly, over and over. Boosters exploded. Tipped over. Missed. Each failure filmed and mocked. But they treated every crash as *data*, not defeat — iterate, learn, try again — and eventually they did the thing the whole industry called impossible: landed a rocket, intact, ready to fly again. The willingness to fail in public, repeatedly, in pursuit of the first-principles answer is inseparable from the breakthrough.

EV: Quick one — if this is landing, subscribe to pmnorthstar so these keep coming. Now — what reusability actually unlocked.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE PAYOFF — SPACE GOT CHEAP." The same booster launches, lands, launches again, lands again — a tally of flights climbing, cost-per-launch plummeting, the sky filling with satellites.]

EV: And here's the payoff, and it's enormous. Once you can reuse the rocket, the economics of space collapse in the best way. You're not building a brand-new multi-million-dollar machine for every launch — you're refueling and reflying one you already own. That drove the cost of reaching space down dramatically, which meant SpaceX could launch far more often and far cheaper than anyone. And that cheapness unlocked entirely new things that were impossible before — like putting up a giant constellation of internet satellites, Starlink, to beam connectivity to the whole planet, which became a massive business of its own. The first-principles insight didn't just save money on rockets. It made whole new industries *possible*, because the fundamental cost barrier was gone.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Booster landings now routine, even caught by giant 'chopstick' arms at the tower; the colossal Starship testing toward full reusability; Starlink everywhere; SpaceX one of the most valuable private companies on earth.]

EV: And now? What was called impossible is *routine.* SpaceX lands boosters so reliably it's barely news anymore — they even catch the giant booster out of the sky with massive mechanical arms on the launch tower, a sight that looks like science fiction. They're testing Starship, an enormous fully-reusable system meant to make the cost drop even further and, eventually, carry people to Mars. Starlink blankets the globe. And SpaceX became one of the most valuable private companies in the world, dominating the launch market so completely that competitors are scrambling to copy the reusability they once mocked. The first-principles bet didn't just win — it reset what the entire world believes is possible in space. The question everyone was too comfortable to ask rebuilt an industry.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Don't reason by analogy. Break it to first principles."]

EV: So the principle to carry, and it applies to *any* problem, not just rockets: when everyone tells you "this is just how it's done" or "this cost is fixed," don't accept it. Break the problem down to its fundamental truths — what is this *actually* made of, what does it *actually* require — and rebuild your reasoning from there. The most stubborn constraints in any industry are often not laws of nature but unexamined habits. Reasoning from first principles is how you find the real problem hiding underneath the assumed one — and solving the real problem is how you do what others swore couldn't be done.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Everyone says this can't be done cheaper. How would you approach it?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a standout answer. If they hand you a problem wrapped in "this is just expensive / this is just how it works," don't accept the frame. Say: "I'd reason from first principles instead of analogy — break the thing down to its fundamental components and costs, and question which constraints are actually real versus just inherited habit. SpaceX did that with rockets: the materials were cheap, the waste was throwing the rocket away, so the real problem was reusability, not 'space is expensive.'" Then land it: "Often the biggest unlock is realizing the constraint everyone accepts isn't actually a law — it's just an assumption nobody's challenged." That signals you think from the ground up, not from the status quo.

[VISUAL: Recap: the rocket thrown away vs. landed, the breakdown to raw materials, the failures, the first perfect landing, space going cheap, the chopstick catch.]

EV: So — SpaceX. The whole world accepted that rockets were disposable and space was expensive, because that's how it had always been. SpaceX broke the problem to first principles — the materials were cheap, the waste was the thrown-away rocket — found the real problem, failed in public until it cracked reusability, and collapsed the cost of reaching space. Don't reason from how things are. Reason from what's true, and rebuild. That's how you land the impossible.
