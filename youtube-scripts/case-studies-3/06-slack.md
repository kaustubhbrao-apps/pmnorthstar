EV: Here's one of the great plot twists in business. A talented team spends years and a fortune building an ambitious online video game. It launches. It flops — badly. The game is dead. Most teams would pack up and go home. But as they looked at the wreckage, they realized something: the game was worthless, but the little *internal chat tool* they'd built just to communicate with each other while making it? That thing was genuinely great. So they threw away the game they'd dreamed of — and shipped the tool they'd built by accident. That tool was Slack. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "SLACK — the by-product becomes the business."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Slack you know — the app with the channels and the hashtags where whole companies chat and work. But the thing worth studying is its bizarre birth, because it hides a profound product lesson. Here's the principle: **sometimes the most valuable thing you build isn't the product you set out to make — it's the tool you built *for yourself* along the way. The by-product can be the business.** The trick is being willing to *see* it. Let me show you how Slack's team pulled that off — twice, actually.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE AMBITIOUS FAILURE." A sprawling, beautiful game world being built; huge effort, huge spend; then the player count flatlines near zero.]

EV: So the setup. This same team, led by Stewart Butterfield, was building an online game called Glitch. They believed in it deeply, poured years into it, and it simply didn't find enough players to survive. A total, expensive failure on the thing they actually set out to do. Now — pause here, because this is the fork in the road where most companies just die. The obvious, dignified move is to admit defeat and shut down. And they nearly did. But before giving up entirely, they asked a deceptively powerful question: "did we build *anything* in all this work that's actually valuable?" Not "how do we save the game" — but "what do we have in our hands right now that's good?"

[VISUAL: Header: "THE HIDDEN GEM — THE INTERNAL TOOL." Zoom into the dead game's infrastructure; one component glows: the custom chat system the distributed team used to work together every day.]

EV: And the answer was staring at them. To build the game, their team — spread across different cities — had created their own internal messaging system to communicate, share files, and coordinate. They'd used it every single day for years. And it was *fantastic* — so much better than the clunky email and tools everyone else at work suffered through, that they'd genuinely come to love it. Here's the critical realization: they weren't *customers* of a communication tool. They were *living* the problem of team communication every day, and they'd quietly solved it brilliantly for themselves. The most valuable asset from the failed game wasn't in the game at all. It was the thing they'd built on the side, almost without noticing, to make the real work possible.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY 'BUILT FOR OURSELVES' WAS A SUPERPOWER." The team is shown as both the makers AND the daily users — every annoyance fixed, every feature battle-tested by real use.]

EV: And here's *why* that internal tool was so unusually good — it's the deep lesson. Because the team had built it *for themselves* and used it relentlessly, every annoyance had already been felt and fixed. Every feature existed because *they* actually needed it, not because a focus group suggested it. It was battle-tested by years of real, daily use by demanding users who happened to also be the builders. That's an incredibly powerful position — when you build something to solve your *own* genuine pain, you have perfect taste about what matters, because you *are* the user. They had, by accident, the ideal conditions for a great product: deep, lived understanding of a real problem, and a solution refined by their own daily suffering and delight.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BRAVE PIVOT." The founders let the dream game go, turn fully toward the humble chat tool, polish it, and launch it to the world as a product.]

EV: Now came the hard, brave part — and notice it's the *same* muscle as letting go of your identity that we've seen in other great turnarounds. They had to fully let go of the game they'd dreamed of and loved, and commit entirely to a workplace chat app — far less glamorous than building a magical game world. That's an ego death. But they did it: they took their internal tool, polished it for other companies, and launched it as Slack. And because the core was already so refined from their own years of use, it was *delightful* from day one — fast, fun, with those little touches and emoji that made work communication actually enjoyable. It spread through companies like wildfire, team by team, becoming one of the fastest-growing business tools ever. The by-product they almost overlooked became worth billions.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Slack acquired by Salesforce for ~$28B; now woven into Salesforce and loaded with AI — summaries, search, and agents working inside your channels; battling Microsoft Teams.]

EV: And now? Slack was acquired by Salesforce for around twenty-eight billion dollars, and it's become a backbone of how companies work — now being reinvented again as an AI hub, where assistants summarize your unread channels, search across everything, and agents actually do tasks right inside your conversations. It fights a fierce war with Microsoft Teams, which bundles a competitor free into Office. But the origin lesson never fades: a company that *failed* at its dream found that the tool it built for itself along the way was the real treasure — and had the courage to see it and pivot. Two of history's most valuable work tools, Slack and the company behind Flickr before it, both came from this same team spotting the gem hiding inside a failure.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "The tool you build for yourself may be the business. Look for it."]

EV: So the principle to carry: pay close attention to the tools and workarounds you build *for yourself* to get your real work done — because if a problem is painful enough that you had to solve it for yourself, it's probably painful for many others too. When a project struggles, don't just ask "how do I save this?" Ask "what valuable thing did I build along the way that I'm not seeing?" Some of the biggest companies were by-products — the side tool, not the main quest. The skill is the humility to notice when the accidental thing is better than the thing you set out to make, and the courage to chase it instead.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Your main project is failing. What would you do?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a memorable answer. If they describe a failing project and ask what you'd do, don't just say "cut losses" or "pivot" vaguely. Say: "Before shutting down, I'd audit everything we built for a hidden asset — especially any internal tools we made for ourselves, because solving your own genuine pain is a sign of real product-market fit waiting to be found. Slack was literally the internal chat tool from a failed video game." Then land it: "The failure of the main goal doesn't mean everything you built is worthless — the by-product is sometimes the real business, if you're humble enough to see it." That shows resourcefulness and real product instinct.

A team failed at the game they dreamed of, but had the wisdom to see that the humble chat tool they'd built for *themselves* — refined by years of their own daily use — was the real treasure, and the courage to throw away the dream and ship the by-product. The tool you build to get your work done might be the work. Look for the gem hiding inside the failure.
