But this month, Anthropic — the company behind Claude — made two moves that have almost nothing to do with topping that chart. One: a new model, Sonnet 5.5, that's about thirty percent *faster* and thirty percent *cheaper* for the same work. Two: it committed around a hundred million dollars to *train* ten thousand engineers to build AI into real businesses. Not "we're the smartest." Instead: "we're the best *value*, and we'll own the people who *build* the future." Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "ANTHROPIC — value + ecosystem beat raw IQ."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar — special episode, because these two moves teach a lesson that applies far beyond AI. Here's the principle I want you to take: **in a race where everyone's chasing the top of the benchmark, you can win by competing on a different axis — price-performance, the best *value* for real work — and by *arming the ecosystem*: training the builders so the whole industry grows on top of you. You don't have to be the smartest. Be the most *useful* and the most *built-on.*** Let me break down both moves.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 1 — PRICE-PERFORMANCE, NOT PEAK IQ." A leaderboard where models fight over the #1 'smartest' spot by tiny margins; beside it, a different chart: 'quality per dollar, per second' — and Anthropic optimizing THAT.]

EV: Move one — competing on *value*, not peak intelligence. Everyone fixates on which model is *smartest*, but here's the real-world truth: for the vast majority of actual work — summarizing, drafting, coding, answering customers — you don't need the absolute genius model. You need one that's *smart enough*, and then *fast* and *cheap*, because you're running it millions of times. Sonnet 5.5 leans straight into that: roughly thirty percent faster, and it cuts the *cost per task* by up to about thirty percent — not by dropping the sticker price, but by getting the job done with fewer tokens and fewer steps. That's not a flashy headline, but for a business deploying AI at scale, speed and cost are *everything* — they decide whether using AI is even affordable. Anthropic is betting that the race people *think* matters — peak IQ — is less important than the race that actually pays: the best quality *per dollar, per second.* Be the best value, and you win the work that actually ships.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY VALUE OFTEN BEATS 'BEST.'" A reminder of past winners: the 'good enough, cheaper, faster' option quietly beating the technically superior one, again and again across tech history.]

EV: And this is a pattern that repeats *all over* tech history, which is why it matters. The technically "best" product frequently loses to the one that's *good enough* and dramatically cheaper or faster or easier. It's the same shape we've seen in this very series — the simpler, more accessible option winning over the more powerful one. Because most customers don't actually want "the absolute best on a benchmark." They want "more than good enough, at a price and speed I can actually live with." Optimizing for *value* — quality per cost — targets the real decision people make when they deploy something for real, not the decision reviewers make when they score a demo. Anthropic choosing that axis isn't settling. It's picking the race that actually decides who gets used.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 2 — ARM THE BUILDERS." The Claude Frontier Academy: ~$100M, ~10,000 engineers trained; they graduate and go build AI products on Claude, spreading out across the industry.]

EV: Move two is the quieter genius — the Claude Frontier Academy. Anthropic committed around a hundred million dollars to *train ten thousand engineers* to actually make AI work inside businesses. On the surface, that's a nice education program. Strategically, it's brilliant. Think about what happens: ten thousand engineers learn to build AI systems — trained *in Anthropic's world*, on *its* tools and *its* model. Then they go out into companies across the industry and build real products, naturally reaching for the platform they were trained on. It's the same move Nvidia made with its developer platform, the same move Stripe made by winning developers — *arm the people who build the future, and they'll build it on top of you.* You're not just selling a model; you're seeding a whole generation of builders whose skills, habits, and defaults point back to you. That's distribution you can't buy with ads.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TWO MOVES LOCK TOGETHER." Trained builders need a model that's affordable to run at scale → they reach for the fast, cheap one → which they were trained on. A loop.]

EV: And here's the clever part — the two moves feed each other. The engineers trained in the academy go out to build real products, and real products need a model that's *affordable to run at scale* — which is exactly what the faster, cheaper Sonnet is built to be. So the ecosystem play creates demand, and the value play satisfies it. Builders trained on Claude, deploying at scale, reaching for the best-value model — which is Claude. One move makes the builders; the other makes sure the thing they build with is the sensible economic choice. Together they aim at owning not the top of a leaderboard, but the *actual plumbing* of how AI gets deployed in the real economy. That's a much more durable place to win than a benchmark that gets beaten next month.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THIS SITS (RIGHT NOW)." The 'smartest model' headlines still dominate; meanwhile Anthropic (and rivals) quietly competing on cost, speed, safety, and developer mindshare — the fight moving from 'who's smartest' to 'who gets built on.']

EV: So where does this sit, right now? The headlines are still all about who has the *smartest* model this week — and that race is real and fierce, with OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic all pushing frontier models. But underneath the headlines, the *real* competition is shifting to things that decide actual adoption: cost, speed, reliability, safety, and — crucially — *which platform the builders know and trust.* Anthropic's two moves this month are a clear bet that the war won't be won at the top of a benchmark; it'll be won on value and on owning the ecosystem of people who build. Whether that bet pays off, we'll see — but it's a sharp read of where the fight is actually heading.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "You don't have to be the best. Be the best value, and own the builders."]

EV: So the principle to carry — and it's one of the most useful in all of strategy: when everyone is sprinting to be "the best" on some headline metric, ask whether that's even the race that decides who *wins the customer.* Often it isn't. The quieter, more durable wins come from being the best *value* for the real job — good enough, but faster, cheaper, easier — and from *arming the ecosystem*, training and equipping the people who build the future so they build it on you. Don't chase the benchmark everyone's watching. Win the economics that decide what actually gets deployed, and own the builders who decide what gets built. That's how you win a race without being in first place on the scoreboard.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "You're competing against a rival with a technically superior product. What's your strategy?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a genuinely strong answer. If they say "a competitor has a technically superior product, what do you do?", do NOT say "we'll make ours better." Say: "I probably wouldn't win a pure 'who's best' race, and I'm not sure that's the race that decides the customer. I'd compete on *value* — good enough quality, but meaningfully faster, cheaper, or easier to deploy — because that's what most customers actually choose. Anthropic just did this with a model that's thirty percent cheaper and faster rather than chasing the top benchmark." Then add the ecosystem move: "And I'd invest in *arming the builders* — training and tooling — so the next generation builds on my platform by default, the way Anthropic is training ten thousand engineers." That shows you compete on strategy and distribution, not ego.

Not "we're the smartest." Instead, "we're the most useful, and the most built-on." You don't have to lead the scoreboard to win the game. Be the best value, own the builders, and win the race that actually counts.
