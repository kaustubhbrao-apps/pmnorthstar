EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Last video we hunted the internal suspects — the things *we* did. But sometimes your product is totally fine and the cause is out in the world. These external causes are trickier, because there's no deploy log to check — you have to reason about what's happening *around* your product. And here's your clue from earlier: external causes are more likely behind *gradual* declines and broad, everywhere-at-once drops. If nothing internal fits, open this door.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 5 EXTERNAL SUSPECTS." Five suspects materialize out of the storm.]

EV: Let's meet the five external suspects — the outside forces that move your metrics without you touching a thing.

[VISUAL: Suspect 1 lights — a rival app logo rising with a "NEW!" badge.]

EV: Suspect number one — a *competitor move*. A rival just launched a killer feature, slashed their prices, or poured money into marketing — and your users drifted to them. This is especially likely behind a *gradual* decline, as users slowly migrate. The tell: your metric sags while a competitor's star is clearly rising. In an interview, you'd say "I'd check whether any competitor made a major move around when our decline started." It shows you think about the whole market, not just your own bubble.

[VISUAL: Suspect 2 lights — a calendar flipping to a holiday; a sun/snowflake cycle.]

EV: Suspect number two — *seasonality*. The single most underrated cause, and the one that embarrasses people most. Lots of products naturally rise and fall with the calendar. A food-delivery app dips when school's out and families travel. A productivity tool craters over the winter holidays when everyone's offline. A fitness app spikes in January and sags by March. Before you panic, ask: is this just the normal rhythm of the calendar? Compare to the *same period last year*, not just last month. If last December also dropped, mystery solved — it's seasonal.

[VISUAL: Suspect 3 lights — a news headline and a social-media firestorm icon.]

EV: Suspect number three — a *news or PR event*. Something happened in the world or to your brand. Maybe a data-breach story hit the press and users got spooked. Maybe a viral post trashed your product. Or flip it — maybe a *competitor* got great press and pulled attention. External reputation events can move metrics fast and hard, and they won't show up anywhere in your own systems. You'd check: any press, social buzz, or public incident around the drop?

[VISUAL: Quiet lower-third: "Subscribe to pmnorthstar."]

EV: Quick one — if the external suspects are useful, subscribe to pmnorthstar so you catch the full live case coming up. Two more to go.

[VISUAL: Suspect 4 lights — a platform logo (app store / OS) with a "POLICY CHANGED" stamp.]

EV: Suspect number four — a *platform or policy change*. This one's brutal because it's completely out of your control. You live on someone else's platform — an app store, a search engine, a social network, an operating system. When *they* change the rules, your metrics can swing overnight. The app store changes its ranking algorithm and your downloads tank. A social platform cuts organic reach and your referral traffic dies. An OS update adds a privacy prompt and your tracking breaks. Always ask: did any platform we depend on change *their* rules recently?

[VISUAL: Suspect 5 lights — a broad economic cloud; a currency/recession icon.]

EV: And suspect number five — *macro and market shifts*. The big-picture forces. An economic downturn and people cut discretionary spending, so your premium subscriptions drop. A new regulation changes what you're allowed to do. A broad behavior shift — like everyone suddenly going back to offices — changes when and how people use you. These are slow, structural, and affect whole categories, not just you. The tell: your competitors are probably hurting too. If the *whole industry* is down, it's macro, not you.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TELL — IS IT JUST YOU, OR EVERYONE?" A split: one app dropping alone vs the whole category dropping.]

EV: And here's the master clue for the entire external branch — ask "is this just happening to *us*, or to everyone in our category?" If only your product dropped, lean internal or a targeted competitor move. If your *whole industry* dropped at once, it's almost certainly external and macro — seasonality, economy, a platform change. This single question instantly tells you which branch of the tree you're really on. It's the fastest way to separate "we broke something" from "the world shifted."

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO INVESTIGATE THE INVISIBLE." A detective checks: competitor news, last-year data, press, platform changelogs, industry benchmarks.]

EV: Now, external causes are harder to *prove* because there's no internal log — so how do you investigate? You name the external evidence you'd gather. "I'd compare to the same period last year to rule out seasonality. I'd check competitor news and the app store for ranking changes. I'd look at industry benchmarks to see if the whole category is down. I'd scan for any press or platform policy announcements around the drop." Naming these shows the interviewer you don't just look inward — you understand your product lives inside a bigger, shifting world. That's mature, systems-level thinking.

[VISUAL: Recap: the five external suspects swirling, each with its one-line tell.]

EV: So — the external branch. Five suspects: a competitor move, seasonality, a PR or news event, a platform or policy change, and macro market shifts. Investigate by gathering outside evidence, and let the master clue guide you — is it just us, or the whole category? Sometimes your product is perfectly fine, and the smartest thing you can say is "this isn't us — the world changed."

[VISUAL: Header: "QUICK CATCH — THE SEASONALITY TRAP." A graph drops; then last year's graph overlays showing the exact same dip.]

EV: Let me show you the most common external catch, because it saves careers. Say a food-delivery app's orders dropped twelve percent over two weeks, and the team is panicking, hunting for a bug. You run the external checks. Internal? Nothing shipped. Is it just us? "Let me check — are competitors down too?" And then the key move: "what's the date range... mid-December? Let me compare to the same two weeks *last* year." And there it is — last December had the exact same dip. It's the holidays. People traveled, ate with family, ordered less. There was never a bug. The team was about to waste a week hunting a problem that was just the calendar. One comparison to last year — same-period, not same-month — dissolved the whole emergency. That's why seasonality is the first external check I run on any gradual drop: it's the cause that masquerades as a crisis more than any other.
