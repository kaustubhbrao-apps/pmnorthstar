[VISUAL: Header: "THE 6 MISTAKES THAT SINK CANDIDATES." Six red-flag slots fill one at a time.]

EV: Mistake one — jumping straight to a cause. The classic. Interviewer says "engagement dropped," candidate instantly says "it's probably a bug." No clarifying, no confirming, no structure — just a reflex guess. Even if you get lucky and it *is* a bug, you've shown zero process. Always walk the framework. The cause comes *after* the investigation, never before.

[VISUAL: Red flag 2 — a shotgun blasting scattered pellets labeled with random causes.]

EV: Mistake two — the shotgun blast. Firing off fifteen possible causes with no structure and no prioritization. "Could be a bug, a competitor, seasonality, a release, the weather, aliens..." Volume isn't rigor. It makes you sound scattered and anxious. A few *prioritized*, likely causes beat a random pile of twenty every time. Quality of reasoning over quantity of guesses.

[VISUAL: Red flag 3 — a candidate sprinting past a "IS IT REAL?" sign without looking.]

EV: Mistake three — never confirming the drop is real. Skipping straight past the measurement check and assuming catastrophe. We covered how often "emergencies" are just tracking bugs or data delays. Candidates who never even ask "is this real?" reveal they haven't lived through real incidents. Thirty seconds of confirming makes you look seasoned.

[VISUAL: Red flag 4 — a giant blurry "TOTAL" number the candidate keeps staring at.]

EV: Mistake four — never segmenting. Staring at the aggregate number the whole time and never slicing it. This is the biggest *missed opportunity* of all, because segmentation is what actually cracks cases. If you only take one habit from this whole series, let it be: never accept the total — always slice. The candidate who segments looks like a detective; the one who doesn't looks lost.

[VISUAL: Red flag 5 — a candidate stuck forever investigating, never concluding.]

EV: Mistake five — never landing a conclusion. Investigating beautifully and then just... trailing off, never committing to a hypothesis. The interviewer's left thinking "so... what do you actually think happened?" Always close with your leading hypothesis and how you'd confirm it. An investigation with no conclusion feels like a movie with no ending.

[VISUAL: Red flag 6 — a candidate claiming "it's DEFINITELY this" with false certainty, then getting proven wrong.]

EV: And mistake six — false certainty. Slamming down "it's definitely a bug." with total confidence and no evidence. Real analysts speak in *likelihoods*. "My leading hypothesis is X, because of Y, and I'd confirm with Z." Overconfidence without evidence is fragile — the moment the interviewer pushes, you crumble. Confident *and* humble is the sweet spot.

[VISUAL: Header: "DELIVER LIKE A PRO — THE 4-PART CLOSE." Four clean blocks stack.]

EV: Here's a four-part structure for delivering your conclusion, the way you'd brief a stressed-out executive who has thirty seconds. Part one — *the headline first*. Lead with your answer, don't bury it. "I believe the new onboarding flow is causing the drop." Executives want the conclusion up front, then the detail. Part two — *the evidence*. "Here's why: the timing lines up with the launch, it's isolated to new users, and the leak is at the onboarding step." Part three — *the confidence level*. "I'm fairly confident, but I'd confirm before acting." Honesty about certainty builds trust. And part four — *the next step*. "To confirm, I'd compare completion rates before and after, and roll back for a test group." Headline, evidence, confidence, next step. That's how a real PM briefs a room.

[VISUAL: Header: "BONUS — SEPARATE DIAGNOSIS FROM FIX." Two boxes: "WHY it happened" and "WHAT to do."]

EV: And a bonus move that impresses — know the difference between *diagnosis* and *fix*, and offer both. The question asked *why* the metric dropped — that's diagnosis. But a great candidate adds a beat: "and once confirmed, the fix would be to roll back the onboarding change and redesign it with user testing before re-launching." You're showing you don't just find problems — you *solve* them. Just don't jump to the fix before you've nailed the cause. Diagnose first, then prescribe.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MINDSET." Big kinetic text: "A CALM DETECTIVE, NOT A PANICKED GUESSER."]

EV: And zooming all the way out on this whole series — the mindset that ties it together. When a metric breaks, everyone around you panics. The person who stays calm, pulls out a clear framework, and methodically works the problem? That person becomes the one everyone trusts in a crisis. That's not just an interview skill — that's a *career* skill. Interviewers are really asking one question with these: "when something's on fire, do I want this person in the room?" Your composure and structure answer that louder than any single cause ever could.

[VISUAL: Header: "YOUR 10-SECOND PRE-FLIGHT CHECKLIST." A checklist ticks itself off rapidly.]

EV: Let me leave you with a checklist you can run in your head the instant a metric-drop question lands — your pre-flight. One: don't guess — breathe. Two: clarify — which metric, how big, when did it start? Three: confirm it's real — tracking, delay, seasonality. Four: split internal versus external. Five: segment, segment, segment — slice till one piece lights up. Six: land a hypothesis with evidence and a test. Seven: deliver headline-first. If you run that checklist on autopilot, you've basically pre-loaded a winning investigation before the interviewer finishes the question. Drill it until it's reflexive.
