[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "HANDLING CONFLICT."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Conflict questions are everywhere in behavioral rounds — "a time you disagreed with your manager," "clashed with an engineer," "pushed back on a stakeholder." And here's why interviewers love them: PMs sit at the intersection of many teams with *competing* priorities, so conflict is *constant* and *unavoidable*. They're not asking whether you *have* conflict — everyone does. They're asking *how you handle it*, because that predicts whether you'll be a great collaborator or a nightmare. Let's learn to handle it well, and tell the story brilliantly.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE #1 MISTAKE — PICKING THE WRONG STORY." A story where the candidate 'won' by steamrolling, flagged red.]

EV: Let me start with the biggest mistake, because it sinks people before they even begin — picking the *wrong story*. Some candidates pick a conflict where they "won" by *steamrolling* the other person — "they were wrong, I was right, I overruled them, the end." They think it makes them look strong. It does the *opposite* — it screams "I'm difficult, I don't listen, I bulldoze people." Equally bad is a story where you just *caved* to avoid discomfort — that reads as having no backbone. The *right* story shows you handling disagreement with *maturity*: you listened, you engaged with the substance, and you reached a *good outcome* — ideally one *better* than either original position. Choose the story that shows collaboration, not victory.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 5-STEP CONFLICT PLAYBOOK." Five steps stack.]

EV: So here's a five-step playbook for handling conflict well — both in real life and as the structure of your story. Let's walk them.

[VISUAL: Step 1 — a figure genuinely listening, understanding the other side.]

EV: Step one — *seek to understand first*. Before pushing your view, genuinely understand *theirs*. Why do they believe what they believe? What are they worried about? Often, disagreements come from different *information* or different *priorities*, not stubbornness. "I started by really listening to the engineer's concern — turns out he was worried about technical debt I hadn't considered." Leading with understanding *immediately* signals maturity, and it often dissolves half the conflict, because people soften when they feel *heard*.

[VISUAL: Step 2 — the disagreement reframed around a shared goal.]

EV: Step two — *find the shared goal*. Almost always, both sides actually want the *same* ultimate thing — a great product, happy users, a successful launch — you just disagree on *how*. So you reframe: "we both want this launch to succeed; we just see different paths." Anchoring on the shared goal turns "me versus you" into "us versus the problem." It's the single most powerful move in any conflict — you're suddenly on the *same side*, solving it together.

[VISUAL: Step 3 — data/evidence brought in to resolve neutrally.]

EV: Step three — *bring in data or evidence* to make it objective. Just like with influence, data depersonalizes the fight. Instead of two opinions clashing, you say "let's look at what the users or the numbers tell us." This takes ego out of it and gives you both a neutral tie-breaker. "We disagreed, so I suggested we run a quick test / look at the data rather than argue — and let the evidence decide." That's a beautifully mature move.

EV: Quick nudge — if this playbook's useful, subscribe to pmnorthstar so you catch the rest. Two steps left.

[VISUAL: Step 4 — two positions merging into a better third option.]

EV: Step four — *look for the better third option*. The best conflict resolutions aren't "my way" or "your way" — they're a *new* option, often *better* than either, born from combining both perspectives. "His technical concern plus my user need led us to a solution neither of us had thought of alone — we phased the rollout." When your story ends with a *third, superior* outcome, it shows you don't treat disagreement as a battle to win, but as a chance to find something *better* together. That's the gold-standard conflict resolution.

[VISUAL: Step 5 — "disagree and commit": aligning fully even after losing the argument.]

EV: And step five — *disagree and commit*, for when you *can't* reach agreement. Sometimes, after all the listening and data, you still disagree — and a decision has to be made, maybe not your way. The mature move: once the decision is made, you *commit fully* and support it, even if you argued against it — no sulking, no sabotage, no "I told you so" later. "I made my case with data, the team went the other way, and I got fully behind it and helped make it succeed." That phrase, "disagree and commit," is a famous principle at top companies, and showing you can do it is hugely impressive — it proves you can lose an argument gracefully and stay a great teammate.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE STORY STRUCTURE." A STAR conflict story hitting the playbook beats.]

EV: So the ideal conflict story, in STAR: Situation and Task — set up a real, substantive disagreement with someone (a manager or senior person is even better, as it shows courage). Action — walk the playbook: you listened first, found the shared goal, brought in data, and either found a better third option or disagreed-and-committed gracefully. Result — a good outcome *and* a preserved, even strengthened, relationship. That last part matters: always show that the *relationship survived* — ideally got *better*. A conflict story where you win the point but the relationship is left in ruins is *not* a win. Great PMs resolve conflicts *and* keep the people.

[VISUAL: Recap: the five-step playbook ending in a handshake, relationship intact.]

EV: So — handling conflict. Pick a story that shows *maturity*, not steamrolling or caving. Run the playbook: understand their side first, find the shared goal, bring in data to depersonalize, hunt for a *better third option*, and if you must, disagree *and commit* gracefully. Structure it in STAR, and always show the relationship survived and strengthened. Do this, and conflict questions become a chance to prove you're exactly the collaborator every team wants.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BRAVE PICK — DISAGREEING WITH YOUR BOSS." A candidate respectfully pushing back on a manager, handled well.]

EV: Let me give you a tip on *story selection* that can really elevate your conflict answer — when you can, pick a story where you disagreed with someone *senior*, like your manager. It feels risky, but handled well it's *far* more impressive than a disagreement with a peer, because it shows *courage* and *good judgment* at the same time. Here's the catch — it only works if you handle it with total maturity, or it flips into a red flag. The winning shape: "I respectfully disagreed with my manager's call on X. I didn't just override or grumble — I asked to share my perspective, brought data to back it up, and genuinely heard their reasoning too. We talked it through." Then one of two endings, both strong. Ending one: "They saw the data and we adjusted course — and it worked out." Shows you can move even senior people with evidence. Or ending two, the disagree-and-commit: "They still went their way, I committed fully and made it succeed — and later the data actually vindicated the concern, so we course-corrected together, no 'I told you so.'" Both show spine *plus* grace. Just never pick a boss-conflict story where you were disrespectful or where you were clearly just wrong and stubborn. Done right, "I disagreed with my manager, respectfully and with data" is one of the most powerful conflict stories you can tell — it signals you'll speak up *and* stay a great teammate, which is exactly the balance leaders want.
