[VISUAL: Header: "THE PREMISE — PAST BEHAVIOR PREDICTS FUTURE BEHAVIOR." A rear-view mirror projecting forward.]

EV: Here's the core premise behind every behavioral question: *past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior*. The interviewer can't see how you'll act once hired — so they ask how you've *already* acted in real situations, and use that to predict how you'll perform on their team. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager" is really asking "how will you handle disagreeing with *me*?" So these aren't trivia or storytime — they're the interviewer trying to *predict your future on their team* from your past. Every story you tell is evidence about the colleague you'll be.

[VISUAL: Section header slams: "THE 4 THINGS BEING TESTED." Four slots line up.]

EV: So underneath the stories, what are they actually grading? Four core things. Let's walk them.

[VISUAL: Slot 1 fills — two figures collaborating, one lifting the other. Label: "COLLABORATION & INFLUENCE."]

EV: Number one — can you work with and *influence* people? This is *the* PM superpower, because PMs famously have "all the responsibility, none of the authority." You can't *order* engineers or designers to do things — you have to *influence* them. So they probe: can you align people, build consensus, move a team *without* being the boss? Stories that show you bringing people together and driving outcomes through influence are gold. We'll dedicate a whole video to this skill.

[VISUAL: Slot 2 fills — a figure taking ownership of a problem, rolling up sleeves. Label: "OWNERSHIP & DRIVE."]

EV: Number two — do you take *ownership*? Companies want people who see a problem and *own* it — who drive things to completion rather than waiting to be told, who don't say "not my job." They're listening for whether *you* drove the outcome, or just happened to be nearby while others did. Did you take initiative, push through obstacles, and own the result — good *or* bad? Ownership is one of the most-prized traits, and it shows up in how you tell every story.

[VISUAL: Slot 3 fills — a figure navigating fog and chaos calmly. Label: "HANDLING AMBIGUITY & FAILURE."]

EV: Number three — how do you handle *ambiguity and failure*? The PM job is *full* of both — unclear problems, no right answer, things that go wrong. So they ask about times you faced the unknown or straight-up failed. And here's the key: they're *not* looking for someone who's never failed — that person doesn't exist and sounds like a liar. They want someone who handles failure with *maturity* — owns it, learns from it, grows. How you talk about failure reveals your self-awareness and resilience. We'll cover exactly how to nail these.

[VISUAL: Slot 4 fills — a figure explaining clearly, a listener nodding. Label: "COMMUNICATION & SELF-AWARENESS."]

EV: And number four — can you *communicate* clearly, and are you *self-aware*? Behavioral questions are secretly a communication test — can you tell a *structured, clear* story, or do you ramble into a swamp of irrelevant detail? And are you self-aware — can you honestly reflect on your own role, mistakes, and growth? A rambling, self-unaware answer is a red flag no matter how impressive the underlying story. Clarity and honesty about yourself matter enormously.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE #1 MISTAKE — WINGING IT." A candidate rambling vs one with prepared, structured stories.]

EV: Now the single biggest mistake people make with behavioral rounds — *winging it*. The assumption that "I lived these experiences, so I can just recall them on the spot." And then in the room, under pressure, they ramble, forget the point, bury the good part, or can't think of an example at all. Here's the truth: the best behavioral performers are *prepared*. They've thought through their key stories in advance, structured them, and know which story answers which type of question. That's not "fake" — it's *respecting* the interview. You wouldn't wing a product-sense question; don't wing these either. Preparation is the whole game, and this series is how you prepare.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE STORY BANK." A bank vault filling with labeled story-cards: leadership, conflict, failure, influence, success.]

EV: And here's the single most valuable prep tactic, which I want you to start *today* — build a *story bank*. Sit down and write out five to seven of your best real stories from work or projects — a leadership moment, a conflict you resolved, a failure you learned from, a time you influenced people, a big success, a time you handled ambiguity. For each, know the situation and the outcome cold. Why does this work so well? Because almost *every* behavioral question is a variation that one of your prepared stories can answer — often the *same* story, angled differently. Walk in with a loaded story bank, and instead of panicking to invent an example, you calmly pull the right one off the shelf. The story bank is the foundation of this entire series.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE STORY, MANY QUESTIONS." A single story-card answering five different prompts.]

EV: Let me show you the secret power of the story bank that makes prep so efficient — *one* great story can answer *many* different questions, just angled differently. Take a single rich story: say, you saved a struggling launch after the lead engineer quit. Watch how many prompts that *one* story answers. "Tell me about a time you handled a tight deadline" — that story. "A time you dealt with an unexpected setback" — same story, angle on the setback. "A time you influenced people without authority" — same story, angle on how you rallied the team and the contractor. "A time you took ownership" — same story, angle on you owning the outcome. "A time you handled ambiguity" — same story, angle on figuring it out with no playbook. *One* story, *five* questions — you just emphasize the beat that matches the prompt. So when you build your bank of five to seven stories, you're not preparing for five to seven questions — you're preparing for *dozens*, because each story flexes to cover a whole family of prompts. That's why a well-built story bank makes you feel calm and ready for almost anything they throw at you. Pick *rich* stories with lots of angles, and a handful covers the whole interview.
