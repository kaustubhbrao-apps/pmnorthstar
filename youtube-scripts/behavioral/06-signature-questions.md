EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. These two are the *signature* PM questions — so common they're almost guaranteed, so often answered with clichés that a *genuine*, *specific* answer is a breath of fresh air. Interviewers have heard "I like to work at the intersection of tech, business, and design" ten thousand times — it's become meaningless. So the goal isn't a "correct" answer; it's a *real*, *specific*, *you* answer. Let's take each one.

[VISUAL: Header: "'WHY PM?' — WHAT THEY'RE REALLY ASKING." A figure checking: do you get the job, and do you genuinely want it?]

EV: First — "why product management?" What are they really asking? Two things. One — do you actually *understand* what the job is, and not have some glamorized fantasy of it? And two — is your motivation *genuine* and *durable*, or will you bail when it gets hard? Because PM is often *not* glamorous — it's herding cats, saying no, endless alignment, taking blame when things fail and giving credit when they succeed. They want someone who wants *that* reality, not a Hollywood version. So your answer has to show you *get* the real job and genuinely want it anyway.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO ANSWER 'WHY PM' WELL." Three ingredients combine.]

EV: So here's how to answer "why PM" well — three ingredients. One — a *genuine, specific* reason rooted in *you*, ideally a real story. Not "I like tech and business" — but "I built a small project, and I realized the part I loved most wasn't the coding, it was figuring out *what* to build and *why*, talking to users, making the calls — that's PM, and I was hooked." A real moment beats any abstract list. Two — show you understand the *real* job, including the hard parts: "I know it means influence without authority and a lot of saying no, and honestly that problem-solving-through-people challenge is exactly what excites me." That proves it's not a fantasy. And three — connect it to *this* role or company specifically, so it's clearly not a copy-paste answer. Genuine story, realistic understanding, specific fit. That combination is rare and memorable.

[VISUAL: Header: "'WHY PM' RED FLAGS." Clichés and bad motivations crossed out.]

EV: And the "why PM" landmines to avoid. Red flag one — pure clichés with no substance: "intersection of tech, business, and design," said with nothing behind it. Red flag two — *bad* motivations that reveal a misunderstanding: "I want to be the CEO of the product" — no, PMs lead through influence, not command; "I love telling engineers what to build" — no, that's the *opposite* of the job; "I don't want to code anymore" — a negative, running-*away* reason, not a running-*toward* one. Red flag three — a *fantasy* view that'll shatter on day one. Show you want the *real* job, for a *real* reason that's *yours*. Steer clear of these and you're already ahead.

[VISUAL: Header: "'FAVORITE PRODUCT' — IT'S A PRODUCT-SENSE TEST IN DISGUISE." A product dissected with PM thinking.]

EV: Now — "what's your favorite product, and why?" Here's the key insight most people miss: this is *not* small talk. It's a *product-sense test in disguise*. They're checking: can you analyze a product like a *PM*, not like a *fan*? A weak answer gushes like a consumer — "I love my phone, it's so cool and sleek." A strong answer dissects it like a product manager — who it's for, what problem it solves, *why* the design choices work, what makes it successful, and even how you'd improve it. The product you pick matters *far* less than the *quality of your thinking* about it. It's product sense, wearing a casual outfit.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO ANSWER 'FAVORITE PRODUCT' WELL." A mini product teardown structure.]

EV: So treat it like a mini product teardown. Structure your answer like this. One — *pick a product you genuinely use and understand deeply* — authenticity shows, and you want to be able to go deep. Two — *who is it for, and what problem does it solve?* Show you think in users and jobs-to-be-done. Three — *why is it great, from a PM lens?* Talk about specific design decisions, the aha moment, what makes the experience work, maybe its growth loop or business model. This is where you *show off* your product sense. And four — a *thoughtful critique or improvement*: "and if I were the PM, here's one thing I'd improve, and why." That last part is *chef's kiss*, because it proves you can look at even a product you love with a critical, builder's eye — you're never just a passive fan. Who, what problem, why it works, how you'd improve it.

[VISUAL: Header: "PICK SMART." A flashy-but-shallow pick vs a genuine, analyzable one.]

EV: A quick tip on *which* product to pick. Don't pick something just because it sounds impressive or because you think it's the "right" answer — pick something you *genuinely* know well enough to analyze deeply, because the follow-up questions will expose a shallow pick instantly. Interesting, non-obvious choices can be great *if* you can go deep — they show independent thinking. But a "cool" pick you can only talk about superficially is worse than an everyday product you understand inside out. Depth of analysis beats impressiveness of choice, every time. Pick for how much *real thinking* you can show.

[VISUAL: Recap: "Why PM" ingredients beside the "favorite product" teardown structure.]

EV: So — the signature questions. "Why PM?" — answer with a *genuine, specific* reason rooted in a real story, show you understand the *real* job including the hard parts, and connect to *this* role; dodge clichés and fantasy motivations. "Favorite product?" — treat it as a *product-sense test*: pick something you know deeply, break down who it's for and what problem it solves, analyze *why* it works like a PM, and offer a thoughtful improvement. Make these two genuinely *yours*, and you'll stand out in a sea of clichés.

[VISUAL: Header: "A FAVORITE-PRODUCT TEARDOWN — LIVE." A product gets dissected PM-style in real time.]

EV: Let me run a quick favorite-product teardown live, so you hear the *PM* version versus the *fan* version. Fan answer: "My favorite product is my ride-hailing app — it's so convenient, I love it." ...and that's it. Shallow, forgettable, zero product thinking. Now the PM teardown of the *same* product. "My favorite is my ride-hailing app. Who's it for and what problem? It serves people who need to get somewhere without the hassle of owning a car or hailing a cab — solving the pain of unreliable, awkward, cash-based transport. Why is it great, from a PM lens? The *aha* moment is brilliant — you see the car and the driver approaching on a live map, which solves the real *emotional* need of anxiety and uncertainty while waiting. The cashless payment removes the awkward end-of-ride friction. And the two-sided ratings build the *trust* that makes getting into a stranger's car feel safe — that's a network effect and a moat. And one thing I'd improve? The pricing can feel unpredictable with surge, which erodes trust — so I'd experiment with more upfront price certainty or a heads-up before surge kicks in, to protect that trust I just said was their moat." Hear the difference? Same product — but the teardown shows users, jobs-to-be-done, the aha moment, emotional needs, moats, *and* a thoughtful critique that even ties back to their own strategy. That's a product-sense answer wearing a casual question's clothes — and it's exactly how you turn "favorite product" into a showcase of how you think.
