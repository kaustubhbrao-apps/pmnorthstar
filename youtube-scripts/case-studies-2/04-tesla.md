EV: Think about how every other car gets sold. Billions spent on advertising — the glossy TV spots, the billboards, the radio jingles. And dealerships everywhere, where a salesperson haggles you into a price. That's just how cars are sold. Except one company threw the entire system out. No dealerships. And for most of its life, essentially no advertising. And yet people lined up — put down deposits, *waited*, for a car they'd never seen in a showroom. How do you sell millions of cars while skipping the two things the whole industry is built on? Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "TESLA — own the demand."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Tesla you know — electric cars, the big touchscreen, the guy on the internet. But the thing worth studying isn't the car. It's the *go-to-market*: selling direct, with no middleman and no ad budget. Here's the principle I want to plant: **if you build something remarkable and own the relationship with your customer directly, you don't have to buy demand — you can generate it.** The product and the direct relationship become the marketing. Let me show you the three moves.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 1 — CUT OUT THE DEALER." A chain: carmaker → dealership → customer. The dealership fades, and the arrow goes straight: Tesla → customer, through a clean app.]

EV: Move one — no dealerships. Every traditional carmaker sells *through* dealers — independent middlemen who own the showroom and, crucially, own the customer. Tesla refused. It sells directly: you configure and order your car online or in a sleek Tesla-owned store, and that's it. Why does this matter so much? Three reasons. One, Tesla keeps the dealer's margin instead of handing it away. Two, no haggling — one fixed, transparent price for everyone, which customers actually love. And three, the big one: Tesla *owns the relationship.* It knows its buyers directly, controls the entire experience, and can update and serve them over the air. A middleman between you and your customer is a middleman who owns your customer. Tesla kept that for itself.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 2 — NO ADS, A MOVEMENT INSTEAD." An empty ad-budget box. Beside it, fans filming their own cars, a charismatic founder tweeting, press writing for free.]

EV: Move two is the wild one — almost no advertising. While rivals spent billions on ads, Tesla spent close to *nothing* and let three things do the work. First, the product itself was so novel and polarizing that it generated constant free press — every journalist wanted to write about it. Second, a loud, charismatic founder with a massive online following who could announce a product to millions for free. And third, and most powerfully: *owners became evangelists.* Tesla built a car people felt was an identity, a statement — so owners showed it off, filmed it, converted their friends. Tesla didn't buy attention. It built something worth talking about, and let the talking be the marketing. That only works if the product is genuinely remarkable — "remarkable" literally meaning worth making a remark about.

[VISUAL: Header: "MOVE 3 — OWN THE WHOLE EXPERIENCE." Beyond the car: Supercharger stations, over-the-air software updates improving the car overnight, the app as a key.]

EV: Move three — Tesla owned not just the sale but the whole *ownership experience.* Worried about where to charge? Tesla built its own Supercharger network, so the experience didn't depend on anyone else. The car getting new features overnight, while parked, from a software update — like a phone. The app as your key, your remote, your service channel. Because Tesla controlled the entire stack end to end — no dealer, no third parties in the way — it could make the whole thing feel seamless and premium in a way a traditional carmaker, fragmented across dealers and gas stations, simply couldn't. Owning the demand meant owning everything *after* the sale, too. And that experience fed right back into the word-of-mouth.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CATCH." A single point of failure glows: when the brand IS the founder, the brand rises and falls with the founder.]

EV: Here's the catch nobody mentions when they praise this playbook. When your marketing *is* your product and your founder, you've concentrated enormous risk in both. If the product stumbles, there's no ad machine to prop up demand. And if the founder — who *is* the brand — becomes divisive, the brand inherits that division directly. There's no buffer. The same direct, unfiltered connection that generated free demand for years can just as easily transmit damage straight to the business. Owning the relationship cuts both ways: you get the upside with no middleman, and you get the downside with no shield.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Sales wobble as rivals like BYD surge; the founder's politics divide the brand; and — the twist — Tesla quietly starts... running ads. Plus a driverless Cybercab and a big robotaxi bet.]

EV: And that catch is exactly the story *now.* After years of effortless demand, Tesla hit turbulence. Competition exploded — BYD and a wave of other EV makers caught up on price and quality. And the founder's very public politics became genuinely divisive, denting the brand for a chunk of buyers in a way no ad could fix — the direct-connection risk, made real. The tell? Tesla, the company that proudly *never advertised*, actually started experimenting with paid ads to shore up demand. Meanwhile the whole thesis is pivoting toward autonomy — the Cybercab, a bet that the future isn't selling you a car but running a fleet of self-driving robotaxis. The direct-to-customer model is still intact and still an advantage. But 2025-26 is the proof that "the product and the founder are the marketing" is a high-wire act with no safety net. When it's good it's magic. When it's not, there's nothing to catch you.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Earn demand by being remarkable — but know the risk of no buffer."]

EV: So the principle, with the full picture: the dream is to be so good you don't have to buy demand — own the customer directly, build something remarkable, and let the product and the relationship do your marketing. That's real, and it's powerful, and it saves a fortune. But respect the tradeoff: when you have no ad machine and no middleman, you also have no buffer. Your demand is only as stable as your product's excellence and your brand's reputation. Own the relationship — but don't fool yourself that it's all upside. Direct means direct, good news and bad.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you launch a product with a tiny marketing budget?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a great answer when they throw you the classic constraint — "you have almost no marketing budget, how do you grow?" Don't say "we'll be scrappy with ads." Say: "I'd make the product itself the marketing — build something remarkable enough to earn word of mouth and press, and own the customer relationship directly so there's no middleman diluting it or taking the margin. Tesla sold millions on basically no ad spend that way." Then show range by naming the risk: "The tradeoff is you have no buffer — if the product or the brand stumbles, there's no ad machine to catch demand, which is exactly what's testing Tesla now." Naming both the power *and* the fragility is what makes you sound senior.

[VISUAL: Recap: the dealership fading, the empty billboard, owners evangelizing, the Supercharger and OTA update — then the recent wobble and the first-ever ad appearing.]

EV: So — Tesla. It skipped the two pillars of car-selling — dealers and ads — by owning the customer directly and building something people couldn't stop talking about. The product was the marketing; the owners were the sales force; the direct relationship was the moat. And the very same directness that generated free demand for a decade is now the thing testing it. Earn your demand by being remarkable — and respect that owning the relationship means owning all of it.
