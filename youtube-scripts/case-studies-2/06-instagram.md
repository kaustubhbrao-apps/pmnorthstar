EV: Imagine you've built an app. You spent months on it. It has check-ins, loyalty points, event planning, a whole pile of features — and almost nobody uses it. Most founders would add *more* to fix it. These founders did the opposite. They stared at the data, found the one tiny feature people actually loved, and then deleted *everything else.* Threw away ninety percent of their own work. What was left they renamed Instagram — and it hit a million users in weeks. The courage wasn't in what they built. It was in what they were willing to destroy. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "INSTAGRAM — the pivot."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Instagram you know — photos, Stories, Reels, the gradient. But the thing worth studying is its *birth*: a failing app that became a giant by subtracting. Here's the principle I want to plant: **when something isn't working, the answer is usually not more features — it's finding the one thing people love and having the courage to cut everything else to serve it.** A great pivot is an act of deletion. Let me show you how it happened.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE FAILING APP." Burbn's crowded screen; a tiny usage chart where every feature is flat — except one small bar that's climbing.]

EV: So, the original app was called Burbn. It was a check-in app — you'd log where you were, make plans, earn points, the works. It was complicated, and it was losing. But the founders did the single most important thing a product person can do: they looked honestly at *how people actually used it*, not how they hoped they would. And buried in the data was one surprise. Of all those features, the one people kept coming back to was a small one — sharing *photos.* People didn't care about the check-ins or the points. They loved posting pictures. That little climbing bar, in a sea of flat ones, was the whole business trying to tell them something.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BRAVE CUT." A hand drags feature after feature into a trash can — check-ins, points, plans — leaving one glowing tile: a photo with a filter.]

EV: Here's where most teams fail. It's one thing to *notice* that photos are the hit. It's another to act on it ruthlessly. The founders made a brutal, brave decision: strip the app down to *just* photos. Delete the check-ins. Delete the points. Delete the plans — all those months of work, gone. Rebuild around the single thing people loved: take a photo, make it beautiful, share it instantly. That's terrifying, because you're throwing away real work and betting everything on one narrow idea. But a muddled app that does five things poorly will always lose to a sharp one that does *one* thing people adore. They chose the knife over the pile.

[VISUAL: Header: "MAKE THE ONE THING MAGIC — FILTERS + SPEED." A dull phone photo instantly transformed by a filter into something gorgeous; then it uploads in a blink, even on a slow connection.]

EV: And then they made that one thing *magical.* Two moves. First, filters. Phone cameras back then took mediocre photos, and most people aren't photographers. Filters fixed that in one tap — suddenly your lunch looked stunning, your selfie looked pro. Instant pride, instant reason to share. Second, speed. They engineered the upload to feel instant even on slow connections by cleverly uploading the photo *while* you were still writing the caption. So posting felt effortless and immediate. They didn't just pick the right feature — they made it so delightful and so frictionless that sharing became a reflex. Pick the one thing, then obsess over making it genuinely great.

[VISUAL: Header: "IT NEVER STOPPED PIVOTING." A timeline: photos → Stories (copying Snapchat) → Reels (answering TikTok). Each a bold self-reinvention.]

EV: And here's the deeper lesson: Instagram never stopped doing this. The willingness to cut and reinvent wasn't a one-time birth story — it became the company's *instinct.* When Snapchat's disappearing Stories threatened them, Instagram didn't defend its old format out of pride — it built Stories right into Instagram and arguably did it better. When TikTok's short video exploded, Instagram pivoted *hard* again into Reels, reshaping the entire app around video even though it was famous for photos. Each time, it was willing to disrupt its *own* successful product to chase where attention was going. The same muscle that deleted Burbn kept Instagram alive through threat after threat. Pivoting isn't a sign of failure. For the best products, it's a permanent capability.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Reels and video dominate the feed; Instagram spins off Threads; teen-safety "Teen Accounts" roll in; AI tools appear — and some longtime users grumble it's "not a photo app anymore."]

EV: And now? Instagram is one of the most valuable pieces of the entire Meta empire — reportedly a huge chunk of its advertising revenue — and it's *still* reinventing. It leaned so far into video and Reels to fight TikTok that longtime users grumble it's barely a photo app anymore — a real tension between chasing the format of the moment and keeping the soul that made it special. It spun out Threads as a separate text app. It rolled out Teen Accounts with stricter safety defaults under regulatory pressure. And it's weaving AI creation tools into posting. Watch the balance it's walking: the pivot instinct is a superpower, but pivot too far and you can alienate the very people who loved the original thing. The company that was born by deleting features now has to be careful it doesn't delete its own identity.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Follow what users love. Cut the rest, even when it hurts."]

EV: So the principle to carry: your users are telling you what matters — in the data, in how they actually behave, not in what they say or what you planned. Find the one thing they genuinely love, and then have the courage to cut everything that distracts from it, even work you're proud of, even features that feel safe. And treat that willingness to pivot as a permanent skill, not a one-time emergency. The product that wins is rarely the one with the most features. It's the one brave enough to be about the *right* one.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "Your product has low engagement. What do you do?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a fantastic answer to the classic "engagement is low, what now?" The weak answer is "add features to boost it." Don't. Say: "First I'd dig into the usage data to find what people *do* love — there's almost always one feature or behavior that's quietly working. Then I'd double down hard on that and cut the noise around it, even if that means removing things, so the core gets sharp and magical. That's exactly how Burbn became Instagram — they deleted everything except photos." Then land it: "The instinct to add is usually wrong. The signal is already in how users behave; the job is to listen and have the nerve to cut." That shows you're data-led and decisive, not a feature-piler.

[VISUAL: Recap: cluttered Burbn → the brave deletion → filters and instant upload → Stories → Reels → the modern app, still shifting.]

EV: So — Instagram. It was a failing, cluttered app that found its one beloved feature, photos, deleted everything else, and made that one thing magical with filters and speed. Then it turned that courage into a habit, reinventing through Stories and Reels, threat after threat. A pivot is an act of deletion and of listening. Follow what people love, cut the rest, and be brave enough to do it again and again.
