EV: Here's a comeback story, and it's the perfect note to end this series on. By the early 2000s, one of the most beloved toy companies in the world — a brand parents trusted and kids adored for generations — was *days to weeks from bankruptcy.* Losing enormous amounts of money, drowning in debt. And the reason it was dying is the twist: it wasn't failing because it made a bad product. It was failing because it got bored of its *great* product and tried to do everything *else* — clothes, theme parks, video games, jewelry. It nearly died of distraction. Then it saved itself by remembering the one simple thing it was for. How LEGO rebuilt itself, brick by brick. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "LEGO — survive by focusing."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. LEGO you know — the little plastic bricks you've definitely stepped on barefoot. But the thing worth studying isn't the toy. It's the near-death and the *recovery* — one of the greatest turnarounds in business history. Here's the principle, and it's the perfect bookend for everything we've covered: **when you're drowning, the answer is almost never to do more — it's to cut back to your core and do that one thing better than anyone.** Focus doesn't just make you great. Sometimes it's the only thing that keeps you alive. Let me show you.

[VISUAL: Header: "DEATH BY DIVERSIFICATION." The core brick business, small and ignored, surrounded by a chaotic ring of unrelated ventures draining money.]

EV: So how does the world's favorite toy nearly go bankrupt? Diversification — too much of it. LEGO looked at its simple brick business and worried it was old-fashioned, that kids in a digital age wouldn't want plastic bricks. So, panicking about the future, it sprawled in every direction: LEGO clothing lines, LEGO watches, LEGO video games, LEGO theme parks, LEGO TV shows, even LEGO jewelry for girls. The logic was "spread out, chase every opportunity, don't depend on just bricks." The result was catastrophe. Each new venture was something LEGO wasn't actually good at, each one cost money and management attention, and *none* of it made money the way bricks did. They spread themselves so thin chasing everything that they nearly lost the one thing that was genuinely theirs. Diversification felt like growth. It was actually bleeding.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BRUTAL REFOCUS — BACK TO THE BRICK." A hand sweeps away the clutter of side-ventures; what remains, glowing, is a single brick. Everything rebuilds from it.]

EV: The turnaround started with a painful, clarifying decision: go *back to the brick.* New leadership came in and essentially asked, "what are we actually for?" The answer was simple — the LEGO brick and the magic of building. So they cut the distractions. They sold off the theme parks. They killed the ventures that were draining cash and attention and had nothing to do with building. They shrank the sprawling product range dramatically, cutting the number of different pieces they made roughly in half, because the catalog had exploded into chaos and complexity nobody could manage. It was brutal — you're admitting years of expansion was a mistake and deliberately making the company *smaller.* But you cannot fix a focus problem by adding more. You fix it by cutting. They cut their way back to clarity.

[VISUAL: Header: "THEN — INNOVATE INSIDE THE CORE, NOT AWAY FROM IT." The brick stays central; around it grow smart extensions — themed sets, movies, fan ideas — all feeding back INTO building.]

EV: But here's the sophisticated part — refocusing didn't mean becoming boring or standing still. It meant innovating *around* the core instead of *away* from it. Once the brick was the center again, LEGO got brilliant at extending it in ways that *strengthened* building rather than replacing it. They leaned hard into licensed themed sets — Star Wars, Harry Potter, superheroes — which are still fundamentally about building, just with stories kids already loved. They made The LEGO Movie, which wasn't a distraction from bricks; it was a two-hour celebration *of* them that sent kids running to build. They started listening to adult fans and even let fans submit set ideas. Every one of these innovations pointed *back* to the brick and made building more desirable. That's the difference: old LEGO innovated by fleeing the core; new LEGO innovated by deepening it.

[VISUAL: Header: "A SURPRISE AUDIENCE — THE ADULTS." Grown-ups building enormous, intricate sets; a whole product line aimed at adult fans.]

EV: And refocusing revealed something LEGO had been ignoring: a huge, passionate audience it hadn't taken seriously — *adults.* Grown-up fans, who had loved LEGO as kids, wanted intricate, challenging, display-worthy sets — and they had real money to spend. By focusing on the brick and listening to who actually loved it, LEGO discovered this whole premium market: elaborate architecture sets, giant detailed models, sophisticated builds aimed squarely at adults. It turned out the answer to "will anyone still want bricks?" wasn't to flee to clothes and jewelry — it was to serve the people who loved bricks *more deeply*, including ones nobody expected. The core wasn't old-fashioned at all. They'd just stopped paying attention to it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." LEGO as one of the most valuable brands in the world; adult fans huge; collaborations like Fortnite; plant-based bricks and a big sustainability push.]

EV: And now? The company that was nearly bankrupt is one of the biggest and most valuable toy and entertainment brands *on earth*, posting record revenues — and still running the exact playbook that saved it: focus on the brick, then innovate around it. The adult-fan segment it rediscovered is now enormous. It keeps extending through smart collaborations — teaming up with Fortnite to bring building into gaming, partnering across the biggest franchises in the world — always pulling play *back* toward building rather than abandoning it. And it's investing heavily in the future of the brick itself, chasing sustainable, plant-based and recycled materials so the core product survives a changing world. Notice the discipline: even now, flush with success, it innovates *inside* the core, not away from it. The lesson it nearly died learning became the lesson it never forgot.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "When in trouble, subtract to your core. Then innovate within it."]

EV: So the principle, and it's the one that ties this whole series together: when things go wrong, the instinct is to do *more* — new markets, new products, chase every opportunity. Resist it. The answer is almost always to subtract — cut back to the core thing you're genuinely great at, the thing that's truly yours, and do *that* better than anyone. And then, crucially, innovate *within* that core rather than fleeing it: extend it, deepen it, find the audiences who love it most. Focus isn't the opposite of growth. Done right, focus is what makes growth *durable.*

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "A company is struggling and spread across too many bets. What would you do?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a powerful closing-caliber answer. If they describe a company that's struggling and spread across too many bets, don't suggest a bold new initiative. Say: "I'd do the hard, unglamorous thing — subtract. Identify the true core, the thing the company is genuinely best at and that customers love, and cut the distracting ventures draining cash and focus, even if that means getting smaller first. Then I'd innovate *within* that core — extend it, serve its most passionate users — rather than fleeing it. That's exactly how LEGO came back from near-bankruptcy." Then land it: "You don't fix a focus problem by adding more. You fix it by cutting back to what's real." That's an answer that sounds like someone who's actually run a turnaround.

[VISUAL: Recap: the chaotic sprawl of side-ventures, the brutal cut back to the brick, innovating around the core, the surprise adult audience, the record-breaking comeback.]

EV: So — LEGO. The world's favorite toy nearly died, not from a bad product, but from losing faith in its great one and sprawling into everything else. It survived by doing the hardest thing — cutting back to the brick, admitting the expansion was a mistake, and then innovating *around* its core instead of running from it. From weeks-to-bankruptcy to one of the most valuable brands alive. When you're drowning, don't add. Subtract to what's real, and build from there.
