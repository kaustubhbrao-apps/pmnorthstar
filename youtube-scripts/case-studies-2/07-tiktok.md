EV: For over a decade, social media ran on one idea: you follow people, and you see what they post. Your feed was your friends. Then an app showed up that basically ignored your friends entirely — it didn't care who you knew. Instead, it watched *what you watched*, and from that alone, it built a feed so addictive that people lost hours without noticing. It beat the biggest social networks on earth not with a better friend graph, but by deciding your friends were beside the point. How TikTok rewired social media around an algorithm. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "TIKTOK — the interest graph."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. TikTok you know — endless short videos, the swipe, the sounds. But the thing worth studying isn't the dancing. It's the *mechanism*: how it figured out what you like better and faster than any app before it. Here's the principle: **you don't need to know a user's friends to serve them perfectly — you just need to watch what they actually do, and let that signal, not their social network, drive everything.** The behavior is the data. Let me show you why that changed the game.

[VISUAL: Header: "SOCIAL GRAPH vs INTEREST GRAPH." On the left, a web of friend connections. On the right, no friends at all — just a profile built from what you watch, like, rewatch, and skip.]

EV: Here's the core idea. The old social networks ran on the *social graph* — the map of who you're connected to. To get a good feed, you had to find and follow the right people, and the app was only as good as your friend list. TikTok threw that out and ran on the *interest graph* instead. It doesn't need your friends. It figures out what you're interested in purely from your *behavior* — which videos you watch to the end, which you rewatch, which you swipe away from in half a second, what you like, what you share. Every tiny action is a vote. And from those votes, it builds a scarily accurate picture of what will keep *you* watching — no social network required.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE GENIUS OF THE DEFAULT FEED." A brand-new user, zero follows, opens the app — and immediately gets a full-screen, perfectly-chosen video. No setup, no empty state.]

EV: And this solved a problem that cripples most social apps: the empty beginning. On a traditional network, a brand-new user sees… nothing. An empty feed, because they haven't followed anyone yet. Boring — so they leave. TikTok has no empty state. The *first* thing you see is a full-screen video it's already guessing you'll like, and within a few swipes, by watching how you react, it's dialing in. You get value in literally seconds, before you've followed a single person or filled out a single preference. That instant payoff is a huge reason people got hooked so fast. The interest graph means the app is useful from the very first second — no work required from the user.

[VISUAL: Header: "ONE VIDEO AT A TIME — THE FEEDBACK LOOP." Full-screen, one video, no distractions; each swipe feeds the algorithm, which sharpens the next pick, tighter and tighter.]

EV: Then there's the format, and it's not an accident — it's a data machine. One video at a time, full screen, no clutter, autoplay, infinite swipe. Why is that so powerful? Because it forces a clean, constant signal. There's nothing else on screen to distract from the one question: do you watch this, or swipe? Every single swipe is a crisp data point feeding the algorithm, which immediately uses it to pick a sharper next video. Watch, swipe, learn, serve — a feedback loop running every few seconds, getting more accurate the longer you stay. The design and the algorithm are the same weapon: the format exists to feed the brain, and the brain exists to keep you swiping.

[VISUAL: Header: "ANYONE CAN GO VIRAL." A brand-new account with zero followers posts a great video; the algorithm pushes it to millions purely because people watch it.]

EV: And here's the second-order effect that supercharged everything. Because the feed is driven by *what's engaging*, not by *who you follow*, a total unknown with zero followers can post one great video and reach millions — if people watch it. On a follower-based network, you're invisible until you've built an audience. On TikTok, the content wins on merit, every time. That did two magical things: it motivated creators to post constantly, because anyone could blow up overnight — which flooded the app with fresh content. And it meant the algorithm always had an endless supply of new videos to test and learn from. Great content surfaced regardless of fame, which made the feed better, which pulled in more creators. The interest graph didn't just serve viewers — it unlocked a creator gold rush.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Rivals cloning the format (Reels, Shorts); governments wrestling over ownership; a U.S. deal reshaping who controls the app and its prized algorithm.]

EV: And now? Two huge things. First, the interest-graph model *won* so decisively that everyone copied it — Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, even LinkedIn and Netflix borrowed the full-screen swipe-and-learn feed. The algorithmic "For You" feed is now simply how social media works; TikTok changed the whole industry's default. Second, its very success made it a geopolitical flashpoint. Governments, especially in the U.S., fought over who should own an app this influential, given its foreign ownership — leading through 2025 and into 2026 to deal-making that reshaped control of TikTok's U.S. operations and, crucially, oversight of that famous algorithm. Sit with the irony: the algorithm became so powerful, so central to culture, that *who controls it* turned into a matter of national policy. That's how much one recommendation engine came to matter.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Let behavior, not declarations, drive the experience."]

EV: So the principle to carry: people's *actions* tell you far more than their stated preferences or their social connections. If you can capture clean behavioral signal and act on it fast, you can personalize an experience so well it feels like magic — and you can deliver value from the very first second, with no setup. Design your product to generate crisp signal and to close the loop quickly: observe, learn, serve, repeat. Don't ask users to tell you what they want. Watch what they do, and give them more of it.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you improve engagement for a content app?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a sharp, modern answer. If they ask how you'd boost engagement on a content product, don't say "add more content" or "improve search." Say: "I'd move from a follow-based model to an interest-based one — personalize from *behavioral* signal like watch time and skips, not just declared follows, so even a brand-new user gets a great experience instantly with no empty state. And I'd design the format to produce clean signal and a tight feedback loop, the way TikTok's one-video-at-a-time feed does." Then land it: "The key is reducing the time-to-value to near zero and letting behavior, not setup, drive personalization." That shows you understand modern recommendation-driven products deeply.
