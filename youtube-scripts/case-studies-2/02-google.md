EV: Here's a question. Making a map isn't actually that hard — the roads are public, satellites are for rent, anyone can draw the streets. So why, after twenty years and billions of dollars, can *nobody* build a maps app that beats Google's? Apple tried. It was a disaster for years. The answer isn't that Google drew better roads. It's that every single person using Google Maps is, without knowing it, quietly making the map better for the next person. The map builds itself. Let's get into it.

[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "GOOGLE MAPS — the data flywheel."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Google Maps you know — the app that tells you where to go and how long it'll take. But the thing worth studying isn't the directions. It's *why it's unbeatable.* Here's the principle, and it's one of the most powerful moats in all of tech: **a product that gets better the more people use it becomes impossible to compete with.** Not because you out-spend rivals, but because your users build an advantage that money alone can't buy. Let me show you the loop.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LOOP." A circle: more users → more data → better map → more users. Each arrow lights up as EV names it.]

EV: Follow the loop with me. Google Maps has the most users. All those users, moving around with their phones, send back a constant stream of signal — anonymously, in aggregate — how fast traffic is actually moving, which roads are jammed, whether that shortcut really is faster. That data makes the map *better*: more accurate traffic, smarter routes, live conditions. A better map attracts *more* users, because why would you use the second-best one? And more users send back even more data. More users, better map, more users again. Round and round. Each turn makes the next turn easier. That's a flywheel — and it's spinning for Google and nobody else.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY A RIVAL CAN'T CATCH UP." A challenger map starts empty; even with money, it has no live data because it has no users, and it has no users because the map is empty.]

EV: And here's the cruel part for any competitor. Imagine you launch a rival tomorrow with a billion dollars. You can buy the satellite imagery. You can draw every road. But you *cannot* buy the thing that matters: millions of people using it right now, feeding it live data. Your map doesn't know that this street is jammed, that this cafe closed, that this route is faster at 6pm — because you have no users to tell you. And you can't get users, because without that live data your map is worse. It's a chicken-and-egg trap with no door. That's the beauty of a data moat: the advantage isn't in Google's bank account, where you could match it. It's locked inside Google's *users*, where you can't touch it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE DATA COMPOUNDS." Layers stack onto the map one by one — traffic, reviews, photos, business hours, Street View, 3D.]

EV: And it's not one kind of data — it's layers, stacking for years. People leave reviews and ratings, so Maps knows the good restaurants. They upload photos, so you see the place before you go. Businesses add their hours and menus. Google's cars drove the whole world for Street View. Every layer makes the map more useful, and more useful means more users, which means more of every layer. Twenty years of this compounding is why opening Google Maps feels like opening a live, breathing model of the planet — and why a newcomer opening a competitor feels like staring at an empty grid. Time plus users is a moat you simply cannot shortcut.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE MAP AS A PLATFORM." Other apps — ride-hailing, food delivery, real estate — all plug into the same Google Maps engine.]

EV: Here's the masterstroke. Once Google had the best map on earth, it didn't just keep it in one app. It let *other* companies build on top of it. The ride-hailing app showing your driver's car moving? That's Google's map underneath. The food-delivery tracker, the store locator, half the apps on your phone — many are quietly powered by Google Maps, paying Google for the privilege. So the flywheel doesn't just defend Maps; it turned the map into *infrastructure* the rest of the internet rents. The users made the data, the data made the moat, and the moat became a platform other businesses depend on. That's how a free app becomes one of the most strategically valuable assets a company owns.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Maps grows an AI layer — you ask it a question in plain language ("find me a cozy dinner spot with parking") and it answers using all those layers; Gemini and Immersive View fold in.]

EV: And right now, that twenty-year data advantage is becoming the fuel for the next leap: AI. Google's been weaving its Gemini AI directly into Maps — so instead of just searching, you can *ask*: "find me a lively dinner spot nearby that's good for a group," and it reasons over the reviews, photos, and live data it already owns to answer. Immersive View lets you fly through a 3D version of a place before you arrive. And notice *why* Google can do this better than anyone — the AI is only as good as the data under it, and Google has the deepest data of all, built by its users over two decades. Even as Google navigates big antitrust fights over its dominance, the Maps flywheel just keeps spinning, now with an AI turbocharger bolted on. Same loop. New fuel.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Build a loop where users create your moat."]

EV: So the principle to carry: the most durable advantage isn't a clever feature — features get copied in a quarter. It's a *loop* where your users, just by using the product, create an asset competitors can't replicate. Ask of whatever you're building: "does this get better as more people use it? Is my advantage sitting in my users, where it's safe, or in my features, where it can be copied?" Data that compounds, networks that thicken, content that accumulates — those are the moats that hold. Design the flywheel, then let your users turn it.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you build a long-term competitive advantage?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a sophisticated answer. If they ask how you'd build a lasting advantage, don't say "we'll build better features" — that's weak, features are temporary. Say: "I'd build a data or network flywheel — a loop where every user makes the product better for the next user, so my moat lives inside my user base, not inside a feature list a rival can copy. Like Google Maps: more users produce more live data, which makes the map better, which attracts more users. A competitor can match my budget but not my users' data." Then land it: "The goal is an advantage that compounds with scale, not one that resets every time someone copies me." That framing tells an interviewer you think in moats, not features.

[VISUAL: Recap: the loop spinning — users, data, better map, more users — layers stacking, other apps plugging in, an AI glow settling over the whole thing.]

EV: So — Google Maps. Not the best-drawn map; the best-*fed* one. Every user pours in data, the data makes the map better, the better map pulls in more users, and twenty years of that loop built a moat no amount of money can cross — then became a platform the whole internet runs on, and now the base layer for AI. Build the loop, and your users build your moat.
