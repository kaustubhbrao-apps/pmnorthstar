EV: Rewind to the early 2010s. Microsoft was a punchline. Yes, it was huge and profitable — but it was seen as the slow, arrogant dinosaur of tech. It missed the entire mobile revolution; its phone was a flop. Its search engine was a joke next to Google. Its stock had basically gone *nowhere* for a decade while Apple and Google ate the future. Smart people genuinely believed Microsoft's best days were behind it. And then, over the next several years, it quietly became one of the most valuable companies *on the planet* — worth trillions. No new iPhone moment. No single magic product. So what changed? Let's get into it.

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. Microsoft you know — Windows, Office, the stuff on half the computers at work. But the thing worth studying is the *comeback* — how a company everyone wrote off reinvented itself without inventing a flashy new gadget. Here's the principle I want to plant, and it's one of the deepest in all of business: **sometimes survival means killing the very thing that made you successful, and resetting the culture that's holding you back.** You have to let go of your old identity to earn a new one. Let me show you how.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TRAP — PROTECTING WINDOWS." Everything inside old Microsoft bends to protect Windows; new ideas get strangled if they might threaten it.]

EV: First, the trap they were in. For decades, Microsoft's crown jewel was Windows — the operating system was the center of the universe, and everything had to *protect* it. That sounds fine, until you realize it became a prison. Every decision was filtered through "does this help Windows?" So they crippled their own products to avoid threatening it — their software wouldn't play nicely on Apple or Android devices, because those were the enemy. They were so busy defending the old empire that they couldn't embrace where the world was actually going. This is the classic trap for successful companies: the thing that made you great becomes the thing you protect so fiercely that you can't adapt. Your biggest asset turns into your biggest blind spot.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE CULTURE RESET — 'KNOW-IT-ALL' TO 'LEARN-IT-ALL.'" A boardroom of people competing and hoarding; it dissolves into a room of people collaborating, curious, admitting what they don't know.]

EV: So the new CEO, Satya Nadella, started not with a product, but with the *culture* — because he understood the rot was internal. Old Microsoft was famously cutthroat: teams competed against each other, people hoarded information, everyone had to look like the smartest person in the room. He called that a "know-it-all" culture and set out to replace it with a "learn-it-all" one — curious, collaborative, humble, willing to admit mistakes and learn. Now, that can sound like soft HR talk. It isn't. A company where teams sabotage each other and nobody admits they're wrong *cannot* adapt, because adapting requires admitting the old way was wrong. You literally cannot change course if your culture punishes the admission that you need to. Fixing the culture was what made every other change *possible.*

[VISUAL: Header: "THE BET — EVERYTHING ON THE CLOUD." The Windows-centric universe is deliberately dismantled; a new center forms: Azure, the cloud, glowing blue.]

EV: Then came the strategic bet, and it was radical: stop organizing the company around Windows, and bet everything on the *cloud* — Azure, renting computing power and services to businesses over the internet. This was the future Microsoft had been too distracted to chase. Going all-in meant accepting that Windows would no longer be the center — a wrenching admission for a company built on it. But here's the genius move that went with it: Microsoft started making its products work *beautifully everywhere* — Office on your iPhone, on Android, even its software running on the open-source Linux it once called a cancer. The old Microsoft would rather you *not* use Office than use it on an Apple device. The new Microsoft said: meet customers wherever they are. Serve the user, not the old empire. That shift unlocked enormous growth.

[VISUAL: Header: "FROM OWNING TO SERVING." Old: "use our thing or nothing." New: "we'll help you, on whatever you already use."]

EV: Underneath it all was one mindset flip. Old Microsoft wanted to *own* you — lock you into Windows, trap you in its ecosystem, win by control. New Microsoft decided to *serve* you — be genuinely useful on whatever platform you already use, and win by being the best tool, not the only option. That's a profound change in posture, from domination to partnership. And paradoxically, by *stopping* trying to trap everyone, Microsoft became far more valuable than when it tried to control everything. When you serve customers instead of imprisoning them, they actually choose you — and they stay because they want to, not because they're stuck. Letting go of control was how they regained relevance.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHERE THEY ARE NOW (2025–26)." Azure surging; a giant bet on OpenAI; Copilot woven into Windows, Office, and code everywhere — Microsoft positioned at the front of the AI wave.]

EV: And now? The turnaround set Microsoft up to catch the biggest wave in tech — AI — from the front, not the back. Having rebuilt itself around the cloud and a learn-it-all culture, it made an enormous, early bet on OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, and wired AI "Copilots" into everything — Windows, Office, its developer tools, Azure. The company that *missed* mobile a decade earlier was, this time, positioned right at the center of the defining shift. Its market value climbed into the trillions, trading places at the very top with Apple and Nvidia. It's not all smooth — the OpenAI relationship has its tensions, and the AI race is brutal and expensive. But sit with the contrast: a company left for dead in 2014 is a frontrunner of the AI era in 2026. That's not luck. That's what a culture reset and a willingness to abandon your old identity *buys* you — the ability to catch the next wave instead of clinging to the last one.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE LESSON." Card: "Fix the culture first. Then be willing to kill your sacred cow."]

EV: So the principle to carry: when you're stuck, the problem is usually two-fold, and both are hard. First, the *culture* — if your people can't collaborate or admit they're wrong, no strategy will save you, so fix that before anything else. Second, the *sacred cow* — the very thing that made you successful is often the thing you're over-protecting, and real adaptation may mean dethroning it and serving customers where they actually are, not where you wish they'd be. Letting go of your old identity is terrifying. It's also, sometimes, the only way forward.

[VISUAL: Header: "IN AN INTERVIEW." Mock prompt: "How would you turn around a struggling, stagnant company or product?"]

EV: And in an interview, this is a mature, standout answer. If they ask how you'd turn around a stagnating product or company, don't jump straight to "ship a new feature." Say: "I'd start with two things most people skip. One, the culture and incentives — if teams are territorial or can't admit what's not working, no strategy sticks, so I'd fix how people collaborate first. Two, I'd question the sacred cow — the legacy product everything bends to protect is often the real anchor, and I'd be willing to deprioritize it to meet customers where they now are. That's exactly how Microsoft went from written-off to trillions." Then land it: "Turnarounds are usually about letting go of the old identity, not bolting on a new feature." That signals real strategic maturity.

EV: So — Microsoft. Everyone wrote it off as a slow, arrogant dinosaur. It came back not with a flashy gadget but by resetting a toxic culture into a curious one, betting everything on the cloud, and dethroning its own sacred cow, Windows, to serve customers everywhere instead of trapping them. That reinvention put it at the very front of the AI era. Fix the culture, kill the sacred cow, and you can rise from the dead.
