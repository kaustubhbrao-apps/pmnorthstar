Quick caveat up front: frameworks are *lenses to organize thinking*, never a script to recite. Name them, use them lightly, don't drown in them. Let's go.

[VISUAL: Header: "PORTER'S 5 FORCES." A central market with five arrows pressing in from around it.]

EV: First — Porter's Five Forces, from Michael Porter. The big idea: how *profitable and winnable* a market is depends on five competitive forces pressing on it. The more intense these forces, the harder it is to make money there. It's your tool for answering "is this market even *worth* entering?" Let's hit all five fast.

[VISUAL: Each force lights up as EV names it, with a quick icon.]

EV: Force one — *competitive rivalry*: how many competitors, and how fiercely do they fight? A market with two giants in a price war is brutal. Force two — *threat of new entrants*: how easy is it for *new* players to jump in? If anyone can start a competitor in a weekend, your profits get competed away fast — high barriers to entry protect you. Force three — *supplier power*: do your suppliers have you over a barrel? If one supplier controls a critical input, they squeeze your margins. Force four — *buyer power*: can your *customers* force prices down? If buyers are few and powerful, or switching is free, they'll squeeze you. And force five — *threat of substitutes*: can customers meet the same need a totally different way? Even with no direct competitor, a substitute can eat your market. Five forces, one verdict: the weaker these forces, the more attractive the market.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO USE 5 FORCES — DON'T RECITE IT." A candidate lightly scanning the forces vs robotically listing all five.]

EV: Now — crucial — how to *use* this without sounding like a robot. Do NOT march through all five forces mechanically in an interview; that's boring and screams "I memorized a framework." Instead, use it as a mental *checklist* and surface only the forces that actually *matter* for this market. "This market's tough because rivalry is intense and barriers to entry are low — anyone can copy us. But buyer power is weak, which helps." You hit the two or three forces that drive the answer and move on. The framework works *behind* your thinking, not *as* your answer. That's how pros use it.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE STRATEGY STACK." A vertical stack: MISSION → STRATEGY → ROADMAP → FEATURES, each layer flowing into the next.]

EV: Second framework — the strategy stack. Where Five Forces looks *outward* at the market, the stack looks *inward* at whether your strategy is actually *coherent* top to bottom. It's a vertical stack of levels, and each one should flow logically from the one above. At the top — the *Mission*: why you exist, the big north-star purpose. Below it — the *Strategy*: your where-to-play and how-to-win choices that serve the mission. Below that — the *Roadmap*: the themes and big bets that execute the strategy. And at the bottom — the *Features*: the specific things you build, which should ladder all the way back up to the mission.

[VISUAL: The stack glows; a test line connects a bottom feature all the way up to the mission.]

EV: And here's the power of the stack — it's a *coherence test*. You should be able to pick any feature at the bottom and trace it straight up: this feature serves this roadmap theme, which executes this strategy, which advances this mission. If you *can't* draw that line — if a feature doesn't connect to the strategy, or the strategy doesn't serve the mission — something's broken. You're either building random stuff, or your strategy is just disconnected goals. The stack catches the two most common failures: strategy that's really just vague aspiration, and execution that's disconnected from strategy. In an interview, saying "let me make sure this feature actually ladders up to the strategy" is a gorgeous coherence check.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE TWO FRAMEWORKS TOGETHER." 5 Forces (outward) + Strategy Stack (inward) as a complete picture.]

EV: So the two work beautifully together. Five Forces points *outward* — "is this market attractive and winnable?" The strategy stack points *inward* — "is my plan coherent from mission down to features?" Outward to judge the battlefield, inward to make sure your army is organized. A strategy answer that uses both — lightly, naturally — looks remarkably complete: you've assessed the market's attractiveness *and* shown your plan hangs together. That combination is exactly what makes an answer feel genuinely strategic rather than just a pile of opinions.

[VISUAL: Header: "A WORD ON OTHER FRAMEWORKS." SWOT and others flash by with a "use sparingly" tag.]

EV: One quick word — you'll hear about other frameworks too, like SWOT: strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats. SWOT can be useful for a fast situational scan, but be careful — it's so generic it often becomes a lazy list that doesn't lead to a *decision*. My advice: lean on Five Forces for market attractiveness and the strategy stack for coherence, and use lighter tools like SWOT only to quickly organize a point, never as your whole answer. Remember the rule from the start — frameworks serve your thinking; the *decision* and the *reasoning* are what interviewers actually want.

[VISUAL: Recap: the five arrows of Porter beside the four-layer strategy stack.]

EV: So — two core frameworks. Porter's Five Forces: rivalry, new entrants, supplier power, buyer power, substitutes — your tool for judging if a market is attractive and winnable, used as a checklist, not a chant. And the strategy stack: mission, strategy, roadmap, features — your coherence test that everything ladders up. Outward and inward. Use them lightly, surface only what matters, and let them structure your judgment rather than replace it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WATCH 5 FORCES ON A REAL MARKET." The ride-hailing market gets scanned, only key forces surfaced.]

EV: Let me run Five Forces on a real market so you see the *light-touch* way, not the robotic way — the ride-hailing market. I'm not going to chant all five; I'm going to surface what matters. Rivalry? *Brutal* — a couple of giants locked in a price war, burning cash to win riders. That alone tells me margins are thin. New entrants? Barriers are *moderate* — the tech isn't impossible to copy, but you need huge capital to subsidize rides and build liquidity, so not just anyone jumps in. Buyer power? *High* — riders have zero loyalty and switch apps for a dollar discount, which keeps prices crushed. Substitutes? Real — public transit, owning a car, biking. Supplier power? Drivers can multi-home across apps, giving them some leverage. Verdict, in one breath: this is a *tough, low-margin* market because rivalry and buyer power are intense. Notice I didn't lecture all five mechanically — I surfaced the three that *drive the answer*, hit the others in a phrase, and landed a conclusion. That's Five Forces used like a scalpel, not a sledgehammer. That's how you want to sound.
