EV: But first — what *is* strategy, really, and what are interviewers testing? Because most people get the definition wrong, and that's why they flail.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHAT STRATEGY IS *NOT*." A list of goals crossed out: "be the market leader!", "grow 50%!", "be the best!"]

EV: Let's start with what strategy is *not*, because this trips up almost everyone. Strategy is NOT a goal. "We want to be the market leader" — that's a goal, a wish, not a strategy. "Grow fifty percent this year" — goal. "Be the best product out there" — goal, and a vague one. These are *destinations*. Strategy is the *route* — the actual set of choices about how you'll get there, given limited resources and real competitors. A candidate who answers a strategy question with a pile of ambitious goals has completely missed the point. Interviewers are allergic to it. Strategy is about *choices*, not *aspirations*.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHAT STRATEGY *IS*." Two glowing questions: "WHERE TO PLAY?" and "HOW TO WIN?"]

EV: So what *is* strategy? Here's the cleanest definition, and I want you to burn it into your memory — strategy boils down to two questions. *Where to play*, and *how to win*. Where to play: which market, which customers, which segment are we actually competing for? And how to win: what will we do *differently or better* that makes us succeed there against everyone else? That's it. Every strategy question you'll ever get is secretly asking you to answer those two. Where to play, how to win. If your answer clearly addresses both, you're doing strategy. If it doesn't, you're just listing wishes.

[VISUAL: Section header slams: "THE 4 THINGS BEING TESTED." Four slots line up.]

EV: So underneath every strategy question, interviewers grade four things. Let's walk them.

[VISUAL: Slot 1 fills — a wide landscape view with the whole board visible. Label: "BIG-PICTURE THINKING."]

EV: Number one — can you see the big picture? Strategy demands you zoom *out* — to the whole market, the competitors, the trends, the company's position in the ecosystem. Candidates who immediately dive into feature details fail strategy questions, because they're thinking tactically when they need to think systemically. The strongest answers start wide: "let me first understand the market and the competitive landscape before I talk about what to build." Altitude is the skill.

[VISUAL: Slot 2 fills — a fork in the road with a decisive choice made. Label: "MAKING CHOICES / TRADE-OFFS."]

EV: Number two — can you actually make *choices*? Strategy is fundamentally about saying "we'll do *this*, and *not* that." A strategy that tries to serve everyone, everywhere, with everything, is no strategy at all. Interviewers want to see you *commit* — pick a target, pick an angle, and consciously give up the rest. The courage to choose, and to name the trade-off, is central. Same theme as prioritization — strategy is what you say *no* to, at the company level.

[VISUAL: Slot 3 fills — a shield/moat around a castle. Label: "DEFENSIBILITY — 'HOW TO WIN.'"]

EV: Number three — do you think about *defensibility*? It's not enough to enter a market — can you *win and keep winning*? This is the "how to win" half: what's your edge, and why can't competitors just copy it? Candidates who propose entering a market with no real advantage — "we'll just build a better version" — get challenged hard, because "better" isn't a strategy if anyone can out-build you next month. Thinking about moats and durable advantage is a huge signal of strategic maturity. We'll devote a whole video to it.

[VISUAL: Slot 4 fills — a chess player anticipating the opponent's counter-move. Label: "ANTICIPATING REACTIONS."]

EV: And number four — do you anticipate how others will *react*? This is the chess part, and it separates good from great. If you enter a competitor's market, they won't sit still — they'll respond. Strong candidates think a move ahead: "if we do this, the incumbent will probably retaliate by cutting prices, so our plan has to account for that." Business isn't a solo game against a static world — it's played against *reactive* competitors. Showing you see their next move is deeply impressive.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE SHAPES OF STRATEGY QUESTIONS." Three cards flip.]

EV: Now, strategy questions come in a few common shapes, and recognizing them helps you attack. Shape one — the *market entry*: "Should Company X enter market Y?" or "How would you launch into Z?" Shape two — the *competitive response*: "A competitor just did X — how should we react?" Shape three — the *growth strategy*: "How should this company grow over the next few years?" They look different, but all three come back to our two questions — where to play, how to win — plus anticipating reactions. One mental model, many disguises. This series arms you for every shape.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE GOLDEN STRUCTURE — ZOOM OUT, THEN IN." A funnel: market → competition → our position → choice → how to win.]

EV: And let me give you the golden structure for *any* strategy answer right now, so you have a backbone from day one. Zoom out, then zoom in. Start wide — understand the *market*: how big, how's it growing, what are the trends? Then the *competition*: who's there, who's winning, why? Then *our position*: what are our strengths and weaknesses relative to them? *Then*, and only then, make your *choice* — where to play. And finally, your *how to win* — the durable edge. Market, competition, our position, where to play, how to win. Walk that path out loud and you'll never ramble through a strategy question again. It's the five-beat spine under everything in this series.

[VISUAL: Background softens. Big kinetic text: "GOOD STRATEGY MEANS SAYING WHERE YOU WON'T COMPETE."]

EV: And the mindset for the whole series — good strategy is defined by focus. The companies that win don't try to beat everyone everywhere. They pick a specific battlefield where they have an edge, pour everything into winning *there*, and deliberately *cede* the rest. A strategy that sounds exciting because it does *everything* is actually a strategy that will lose, because focus beats spread every time. Where you choose *not* to compete is as much a part of strategy as where you do.
