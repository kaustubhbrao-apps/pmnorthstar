[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "SHOULD WE LAUNCH IT?"]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. The "should Company X launch Product Y" question feels intimidating because it's so open — but it's actually one of the most *structured* strategy questions, once you have the right framework. The secret: don't just rattle off pros and cons randomly. Walk a clear set of lenses, each answering a specific question, and let them build to a confident go or no-go. Let me give you the four lenses, plus the clarify step, plus how to actually *decide*.

[VISUAL: Header: "STEP 0 · CLARIFY THE GOAL." A question bubble: "why is the company even considering this?"]

EV: Step zero, always — clarify. Before you evaluate, ask *why* the company is even considering this. What's the strategic goal? Growth? Defending against a competitor? Entering a new market? Increasing engagement? Because the *same* product launch might be a brilliant idea for one goal and a terrible idea for another. "Should the company launch this — to drive new revenue, or to defend its core?" Pin the goal down first, because it's the yardstick you'll measure everything against. This takes thirty seconds and immediately makes you sound structured.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE 4 LENSES." Four windows open in sequence.]

EV: Now the four lenses. Think of them as four windows you look through, each answering a make-or-break question. Let's walk them.

[VISUAL: Lens 1 — a market with size and growth arrows. Label: "1 · IS THE MARKET ATTRACTIVE?"]

EV: Lens one — is the *market* attractive? Here's where you pull in everything from earlier in this series. How big is it — size it with TAM, SAM, SOM. Is it growing or shrinking? And how brutal is it — Porter's Five Forces: lots of fierce competitors, or room to win? If the market's tiny, shrinking, or a bloodbath, that's a strike against launching, no matter how cool the product. The market has to be worth entering in the first place.

[VISUAL: Lens 2 — a company's strengths matching (or not) the new product. Label: "2 · DO WE HAVE THE RIGHT TO WIN?"]

EV: Lens two — does *this company* have the right to win? This is the most important and most *forgotten* lens. Just because a market is attractive doesn't mean *this* company should enter it. Ask: do they have *strengths* that give them an unfair advantage here? An existing user base they can funnel in? Technology, data, a brand, distribution that transfers? Or are they a random outsider with no edge? The best launches *leverage existing strengths* — the company already has something that makes them likely to win *this specific* fight. A launch that ignores the company's actual capabilities is usually a no, even in a great market.

[VISUAL: Lens 3 — a strategy stack checking the new product ladders up to the mission. Label: "3 · DOES IT FIT THE STRATEGY?"]

EV: Lens three — does it *fit the strategy*? Remember the strategy stack? Does this new product actually ladder up to the company's mission and overall strategy, or is it a random shiny distraction? Does it strengthen the core business, or pull focus and resources *away* from it? Some launches look attractive in isolation but don't *fit* — they scatter the company's focus. A product that reinforces the core strategy — maybe deepens the moat, or increases engagement with the main product — is far more compelling than an unrelated side-quest, however tempting.

[VISUAL: Lens 4 — a cost/risk scale weighing against the upside. Label: "4 · WHAT ARE THE COSTS & RISKS?"]

EV: Lens four — what are the *costs and risks*? The honest downside check. What would it cost to launch — money, people, focus? What's the opportunity cost — what *else* could the company do with those resources instead? And what are the risks — could it *cannibalize* the core business, damage the brand, or provoke a brutal competitive response? This is where you show maturity by considering the *downside*, not just the exciting upside. The strongest answers weigh what could go *wrong*, including the sneaky ones like cannibalization — launching a product that steals customers from your *own* more profitable product.

[VISUAL: Header: "THEN — MAKE THE CALL." The four lenses feed into a decisive GO or NO-GO.]

EV: And here's what separates a great answer from a wishy-washy one — after the four lenses, you *make a call*. Don't just list considerations and trail off. Synthesize: "So — attractive, growing market: yes. The company has a real right to win because of their existing user base: yes. It fits the strategy by deepening engagement: yes. Costs and risks are manageable, with cannibalization the main watch-out. So my answer is *yes, launch it* — but I'd start with a wedge in this segment and watch for cannibalization." Confident, reasoned, with a clear verdict and nuance. Even "no" is a great answer if it's well-reasoned — "I'd pass, because despite the attractive market, the company has no right to win here." The *decision* is the point. Commit.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE POWER MOVE — 'IT DEPENDS, ON X.'" A conditional answer with the key hinge named.]

EV: And a pro move for nuance — sometimes the honest answer is "it depends," but you must say *what it depends on*. Weak: "well, it depends." Strong: "This hinges on one thing — whether the company can leverage its existing user base. If they can funnel even a small percentage of their hundreds of millions of users in, it's a clear yes. If those users won't cross over, it's a no." Naming the *single hinge* the decision turns on is incredibly sharp — it shows you've found the crux of the whole question. That's a senior-level move.

[VISUAL: Recap: clarify-the-goal, then four lenses — market, right to win, strategic fit, costs/risks — feeding a go/no-go.]

EV: So — "should Company X launch Product Y?" Clarify the goal first. Then four lenses: Is the market attractive? Does the company have a right to win — the most-forgotten one? Does it fit the strategy? And what are the costs and risks, including cannibalization? Then *make the call* — a confident, reasoned go or no-go — and if it genuinely depends, name the exact hinge. That structure turns a scary open question into a clean, impressive answer.

[VISUAL: Header: "WATCH IT ON A REAL PROMPT." "Should a big social app launch a payments feature?" runs through the four lenses fast.]

EV: Let me run the framework fast on a real prompt so the lenses click — "Should a big social messaging app launch a payments feature, letting people send money to friends?" Clarify the goal: likely *engagement and lock-in* — keep users in the app and make it stickier. Lens one, market: payments is *enormous* and growing, but *brutally* competitive with banks and fintechs — attractive but tough. Lens two, right to win — and here it gets interesting: does a social app have an edge in payments? *Yes* — they have a massive existing user base and the *social graph*; sending money to friends is inherently social, and they already own the friend connections. That's a real, specific advantage. Lens three, strategic fit: does it serve the mission? Strongly — payments deepens engagement and switching costs, reinforcing the core. Lens four, costs and risks: huge — financial regulation, fraud, security, and the trust bar for handling money is *way* higher than for messaging. One scandal could damage the core brand. So — synthesize: attractive market, genuine right to win via the social graph, strong strategic fit, but serious regulatory and trust risks. My call: *yes, launch — but* start with a simple wedge like friend-to-friend transfers, nail security and trust first, and expand carefully. See how the four lenses turned a big fuzzy question into a crisp, reasoned yes-with-conditions? That's the framework earning its keep.
