[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "MARKET ENTRY."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. We've sized the market and thought about moats. Now the practical question: *how* do you enter? Because the same market can be entered in very different ways, and choosing the right one is itself a strategic decision. First, the three big approaches — build, buy, partner. Then the wedge, which is less about the door and more about *where* you attack. Let's go.

[VISUAL: Header: "GATE 1 · BUILD." A company constructing its own product brick by brick.]

EV: Gate one — Build. You develop the product yourself, from scratch, in-house. The upside: you get *full control*, you keep all the value, and you build your own capabilities and moat along the way. The downside: it's *slow* and *expensive*, and you might be too late to a fast-moving market. Build makes sense when the product is core to your strategy, when you have the skills, and when time isn't critically against you. It's the default, but it's not always right — sometimes the market won't *wait* for you to build.

[VISUAL: Header: "GATE 2 · BUY." One company acquiring another with a handshake and a price tag.]

EV: Gate two — Buy, meaning acquire. You purchase a company that's *already* in the market — their product, their team, their customers, instantly yours. The upside: *speed*. You skip years of building and leapfrog straight into the market with a proven product and existing users. The downside: it's *expensive* up front, and integrating an acquired company is genuinely hard — cultures clash, systems don't mesh, people leave. Buy makes sense when *speed* matters more than cost, when there's a great target available, and when building yourself would simply take too long. The classic move: a giant buys the scrappy startup that cracked a market the giant couldn't.

[VISUAL: Header: "GATE 3 · PARTNER." Two companies linking hands, sharing capabilities.]

EV: Gate three — Partner. Instead of building or buying, you team up with someone who has what you lack — a technology, a distribution channel, a customer base. The upside: *low cost and low risk*, and fast access to a capability you don't have. The downside: you *share* the value and give up some control, and you're now dependent on a partner whose interests might drift from yours. Partnering makes sense when you need a specific capability quickly, when you don't want to own that piece long-term, or when the risk of going alone is too high. It's the lightest-weight door.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO CHOOSE — SPEED vs CONTROL vs COST." A triangle trade-off.]

EV: So how do you choose among the three? It's a trade-off triangle: *speed*, *control*, and *cost/risk*. Build maximizes control but costs you speed. Buy maximizes speed but costs you money and integration pain. Partner minimizes cost and risk but costs you control and value. So the choice flows from what the *situation* demands: "if we're racing a competitor and speed is everything, I'd lean buy or partner over build." Naming *which* factor dominates — and why — is exactly the strategic judgment interviewers want. There's no universally right door; there's the right door *for this situation*.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE WEDGE — DON'T CHARGE THE FRONT GATE." A tiny focused entry point cracking open a huge market.]

EV: Now the strategic masterstroke — the wedge. However you build, buy, or partner, there's still the question of *where* in the market you attack first. And the biggest beginner mistake is charging the *front gate* — trying to take on the whole market, head-to-head with the incumbent, from day one. That's how you get crushed. The wedge strategy says: *don't*. Instead, find one narrow, specific entry point — an underserved niche, a segment the incumbent ignores, a single use case you can own completely — and *dominate that* first. Get a beachhead where you're unbeatable, *then* expand outward from your position of strength.

[VISUAL: A small wedge driving into a log, splitting it wide open.]

EV: Think of it literally like a wedge splitting a log — you don't hit the whole log at once, you drive a thin, sharp point into one spot, and *that* cracks the whole thing open. The pattern shows up everywhere: the company that started by serving *just* one tiny customer segment that the giants found too small to bother with, got unbeatable there, and then expanded up-market until they were a threat to everyone. Why does the wedge work? Because in your narrow niche, you can be *better than anyone* with limited resources, you build real moats before you're big enough to be noticed, and by the time the incumbent takes you seriously, you've got a fortified base. Attack where you can *win*, not where the prize looks biggest.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SHOWS UP IN INTERVIEWS." An entry question answered with build/buy/partner + a wedge.]

EV: So in an interview, a strong market-entry answer has two layers. Layer one — the *mode*: "Given we're racing the clock here, I'd lean toward acquiring rather than building, because speed matters most." Layer two — the *wedge*: "And rather than attack the whole market head-on against the incumbent, I'd start with this specific underserved segment, dominate it, build a moat, and expand from there." That combination — the right door *plus* a smart wedge — is a genuinely sophisticated entry strategy. It shows you understand both *how* to get in and *where* to strike, which is exactly the strategic depth they're probing for.

[VISUAL: Recap: three gates (build/buy/partner) beside the wedge side-door cracking the market.]

EV: So — market entry. Three doors: Build for control, Buy for speed, Partner for low cost and risk — choose based on whether speed, control, or cost dominates the situation. And the wedge: never charge the front gate against the whole market; drive a sharp point into one underserved niche, dominate it, build a moat, then expand. Right door plus smart wedge equals an entry strategy that actually wins.

[VISUAL: Header: "A WEDGE STORY YOU KNOW." A tiny niche beachhead expanding into a huge market over time.]

EV: Let me make the wedge unforgettable with the pattern you've seen play out over and over in tech. A company wants to take on a giant, established market — but head-on, they'd be crushed. So instead they find one tiny, unglamorous corner the giants ignore because it's "too small to matter." Maybe it's a single type of customer, or one narrow use case. They go *all-in* on that corner and become *genuinely the best in the world* at it — something the giant, spread across everything, can't match in that one spot. They win the niche completely, build real loyalty and a moat there, and earn a profitable, defensible base. *Then* — and only then — they expand outward: an adjacent segment, then another, each time using their fortified position to push into the next. By the time the giant finally notices and takes them seriously, it's too late — the wedge has become a wall. That's the whole playbook: start absurdly narrow, dominate, expand. The counterintuitive lesson is that the path to a *huge* market often starts by deliberately choosing a *tiny* one. In an interview, proposing a specific, well-chosen wedge instead of "we'll take on the whole market" instantly signals you understand how underdogs actually win.
