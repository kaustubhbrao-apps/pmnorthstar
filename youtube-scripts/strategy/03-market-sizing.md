[VISUAL: pmnorthstar logo pops. Title card: "MARKET SIZING · TAM / SAM / SOM."]

EV: I'm EV, from pmnorthstar. In strategy, before you commit to entering a market, you have to size it — because a brilliant strategy for a tiny market is a waste, and you need to know the realistic prize, not the fantasy one. The classic tool is three nested circles: TAM, SAM, and SOM. They go from the biggest, dreamiest number down to the realistic one you could actually capture. And good news — if you watched our guesstimates series, you already have the muscles for this. Let's define the circles.

[VISUAL: Outer ring lights, labeled "TAM — TOTAL ADDRESSABLE MARKET."]

EV: The biggest circle — TAM, Total Addressable Market. This is the *total* demand if you captured one hundred percent of everyone who could *possibly* ever use a product like yours, worldwide. It's the dream number, the whole pie. For a food-delivery app, TAM might be "everyone on Earth who eats food and could order delivery" — a massive, almost theoretical figure. TAM answers "how big could this *ever* be?" It's useful for showing the ambition and the ceiling — but nobody ever actually gets it all, so we narrow down.

[VISUAL: Middle ring lights, labeled "SAM — SERVICEABLE ADDRESSABLE MARKET."]

EV: The middle circle — SAM, Serviceable Addressable Market. This narrows TAM to the portion you could *realistically serve* given your actual business — your geography, your product, your model. Our delivery app doesn't serve the whole planet; maybe it operates in one country, in cities with enough restaurant density. So SAM is "people in the regions and segments we can actually reach with our offering." It's the slice your business model can address. SAM answers "how big is the market we can *actually* go after?"

[VISUAL: Inner core lights bright, labeled "SOM — SERVICEABLE OBTAINABLE MARKET."]

EV: And the bright inner core — SOM, Serviceable Obtainable Market. This is the realistic chunk you could actually *win*, especially in the near term, given competition and your resources. You won't get all of your SAM — competitors exist, you're not everywhere at once. SOM is "of the market we can serve, how much can we *realistically capture*?" — maybe a market-share percentage of SAM. This is the honest, grounded number — what you could actually book as revenue. SOM answers "what's the real prize *for us*, realistically?" TAM inspires, SAM scopes, SOM grounds.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW TO CALCULATE — TWO APPROACHES." Two paths: top-down funnel vs bottom-up build.]

EV: So how do you actually calculate these? Two approaches — and they're exactly our guesstimates methods. Top-down: start with a huge number, like total population or total industry revenue, and funnel it down with filters to your market — fast, but can be hand-wavy. Bottom-up: start from the unit — like "number of target customers times how much each spends per year" — and build up — more credible, more defensible. The pro move, straight from guesstimates: do it *both* ways and sanity-check that they roughly agree. If your top-down TAM and bottom-up build land in the same ballpark, you've got a trustworthy number. Market sizing *is* a guesstimate with a strategic purpose.

[VISUAL: Header: "WHY SOM IS THE HONEST NUMBER." A huge TAM shrinking dramatically to a modest, realistic SOM.]

EV: Here's a trap that sinks people, especially founders — falling in love with TAM. "The market is a *trillion* dollars." Sounds amazing... but TAM is the *fantasy* ceiling nobody ever reaches. The number that actually matters for a *decision* is SOM — what you can realistically *obtain*. A trillion-dollar TAM with a SOM of two million dollars, because the market's locked up by entrenched giants, is a *bad* opportunity dressed up in a big number. So when you size a market in an interview, always walk all the way down to SOM, and be *honest* about it — factor in competition and your real resources. Showing you don't get seduced by the big TAM number, and that you ground your decision in the *obtainable* slice, is a huge sign of strategic maturity.

[VISUAL: Header: "SIZING INFORMS THE DECISION." A size number flowing into a go / no-go gate.]

EV: And the whole *point* of sizing is that it feeds a *decision* — never size for sizing's sake. The number tells you things like: is this market big enough to be worth our effort and investment? Is the obtainable slice large enough to hit our growth goals? Is it *growing*, or shrinking? A huge but *shrinking* market might be worse than a smaller, *fast-growing* one — so always consider growth *rate*, not just current size. In a strategy answer, connect the size straight to the call: "the SOM here is around X, which is big enough to justify entry" — or — "honestly, the obtainable market is too small given the competition, so I'd reconsider." Sizing is a *means* to the go/no-go, not a destination.

[VISUAL: Header: "HOW IT SHOWS UP IN INTERVIEWS." A strategy question with a crisp sizing step.]

EV: In interviews, market sizing shows up two ways. Sometimes it's the *whole* question — "estimate the market size for electric scooters in this country" — which is basically a guesstimate, so you'd funnel down through TAM, SAM, SOM out loud. And sometimes it's a *step inside* a bigger strategy question — "should we enter this market?" — where you quickly size the prize to judge if it's worth it. Either way, show the three circles, do it both top-down and bottom-up if you can, land honestly on SOM, and tie it to the decision. That sequence makes you look rigorous and grounded.

[VISUAL: Recap: the three nested circles — TAM (dream), SAM (reachable), SOM (realistic) — with a decision gate beside them.]

EV: So — market sizing. Three nested circles: TAM, the total dream; SAM, the slice your business can serve; SOM, the realistic chunk you can actually win. Calculate top-down and bottom-up and cross-check — just like guesstimates. Don't fall for a seductive TAM; the honest decision number is SOM. And always tie the size, and its *growth rate*, back to the actual go/no-go call. Size the prize before you fight for it.

[VISUAL: Header: "WATCH A QUICK SIZING — BOTH WAYS." TAM/SAM/SOM circles fill with numbers for a music-app market.]

EV: Let me size a market live, both ways, so you see the cross-check in action — a paid music-streaming service in a given country. Top-down first. TAM: the country has, say, a hundred million smartphone users — the theoretical ceiling if every single one paid. SAM: narrow to those who'd realistically pay for *music* and can afford a subscription — maybe thirty percent, so thirty million. SOM: of those, what can *we* capture against existing competitors in a few years — maybe we win ten percent of the serviceable market, so three million paying users. Now the bottom-up cross-check: "three million users times, say, five dollars a month times twelve is about a hundred eighty million dollars a year." Does that feel right against what we know about the industry? If it's wildly off from a top-down revenue estimate, I've made an error somewhere and I go hunting. But if top-down and bottom-up land in the same ballpark — like they roughly do here — I trust the number. That dual-method cross-check is *exactly* the guesstimates move, and doing it out loud makes your sizing bulletproof.
