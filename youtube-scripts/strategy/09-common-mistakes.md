[VISUAL: Header: "THE 6 STRATEGY MISTAKES." Six landmine slots fill one at a time.]

EV: Mistake one — confusing goals with strategy. The classic, the one we opened the whole series on. "Our strategy is to be the market leader." That's a *goal*, a destination, not a strategy. Strategy is the *route* — the choices about where to play and how to win. If your answer is a pile of ambitious outcomes with no actual *choices* in it, you've made this mistake. Always force yourself to name the *choices*, not just the dreams.

[VISUAL: Landmine 2 — a product trying to be everything to everyone, stretched thin and failing.]

EV: Mistake two — trying to do everything, refusing to choose. Strategy *is* choice — it's as much about what you *won't* do as what you will. The candidate who says "we'll target every segment, enter every market, build every feature" has no strategy at all, because they've made no trade-offs. Focus beats spread. A real strategy says "we'll win *here*, specifically, and deliberately give up *there*." Name what you're giving up — that's the courage interviewers want to see.

[VISUAL: Landmine 3 — a market entry with no moat; a competitor instantly copies and wins.]

EV: Mistake three — ignoring defensibility. Proposing to enter a market with no answer for "how do we *keep* winning?" "We'll just build a better product" — and then what, when a competitor copies it next quarter? If you don't address the *moat* — network effects, switching costs, scale, brand — your strategy is a sandcastle. Always ask "and why can't competitors just copy this?" If you don't have an answer, your strategy has a hole.

[VISUAL: Landmine 4 — a strategist planning in a vacuum while competitors move unseen behind them.]

EV: Mistake four — ignoring the competition's reaction. Planning as if the world will sit still while you execute. It won't — competitors are *reactive*. If you enter an incumbent's market, they'll respond: cut prices, copy features, lean on their moat. The candidates who plan in a vacuum get blindsided. Strong ones think a move ahead: "if we do this, the incumbent will likely retaliate with X, so our plan accounts for that." It's chess, not solitaire — anticipate the counter-move.

[VISUAL: Landmine 5 — a candidate robotically reciting a framework while the actual question goes unanswered.]

EV: Mistake five — hiding behind frameworks. Reciting Porter's Five Forces mechanically, or marching through SWOT like a checklist, without ever reaching an *insight* or a *decision*. Frameworks are *tools to organize thinking*, not the answer itself. The candidate who robotically lists all five forces but never says "and *therefore* this market is attractive or not" has mistaken the map for the journey. Use frameworks *lightly*, surface only what matters, and always drive to a *conclusion*. The insight and the decision are what count, not the framework name-drop.

[VISUAL: Landmine 6 — a candidate endlessly analyzing, never committing to an answer.]

EV: And mistake six — never making a call. Analyzing beautifully and then... trailing off, leaving it as "well, there are many factors." Strategy questions *demand a decision* — should they enter or not? What's the play? A great answer commits: "my recommendation is yes, enter, via this wedge, and here's why" — or a well-reasoned no. Even "it depends" works *only* if you name exactly what it depends on. Endless analysis with no verdict reads as indecision, and indecision is the opposite of strategy. Always, always land the plane.

[VISUAL: All six landmines map out, then a "SAFE PATH ✓" banner traces cleanly through them.]

EV: Map those six — goals-as-strategy, no choices, no moat, ignoring competitors, framework-reciting, and no decision — and you'll walk the strategy minefield clean. Now let me flip it, because avoiding mistakes keeps you safe, but a few *positive* moves make you *shine*.

[VISUAL: Header: "THE STANDOUT MOVES." A few gold stars light up.]

EV: The standout moves, fast. One — *always zoom out before you zoom in*. Start with the market and competition before features; altitude signals strategic maturity instantly. Two — *think in moats*, always asking "how do we keep winning?" — it's the rarest and most valued instinct. Three — *anticipate the competitor's reaction* out loud; the chess move that most people miss. Four — *name the hinge* when it depends: "this whole decision turns on whether X" shows you found the crux. And five — *commit to a clear, reasoned call*. Combine those, and you don't just avoid the mines — you look like someone who could actually run strategy at a real company. That's the goal.

[VISUAL: Header: "YOUR STRATEGY-ANSWER CHECKLIST." A checklist ticks off rapidly.]

EV: Let me leave you with a checklist you can run in your head the moment any strategy question lands, so you never freeze. One — clarify the *goal*: why are they even considering this? Two — *zoom out* to the market and competition before anything else. Three — size the prize honestly, down to SOM. Four — ask "do we have a *right to win* here?" Five — find the *moat*: how do we keep winning? Six — anticipate the *competitor's reaction*. Seven — make a clear, reasoned *call*, and name the hinge if it depends. Clarify, zoom out, size, right-to-win, moat, reaction, decide. If you run that seven-beat checklist, you've basically pre-structured a senior-level answer before the interviewer finishes the question.
