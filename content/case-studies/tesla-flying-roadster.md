In October 2026, Tesla did something that looked less like a car launch and more like a rocket test: it showed a Roadster that briefly leaves the ground, using SpaceX-style cold-gas thrusters, performing a short mid-air maneuver before settling back down. The internet did what the internet does, and the clip went everywhere. It is tempting to argue about whether a car should fly, or whether this is a serious product at all. That argument misses the point. Judged as engineering, it is a spectacle. Judged as *strategy*, it is a near-perfect example of one of the most useful ideas in product: the **halo product**.

## What a halo product actually does

A halo product is a flagship whose primary job is not to sell in volume. Its job is to make the *entire brand* look more innovative, more desirable, more like the leader, which lifts the sales and the pricing power of the ordinary, affordable products underneath it. The dazzling thing at the top pulls up everything beneath it.

This pattern is everywhere once you see it. A carmaker builds a halo supercar almost no one buys, because it makes the whole badge feel fast and special. A phone maker ships an ultra-premium flagship that most customers skip, because it re-rates the desirability of the mid-range phones they actually purchase. The halo does not need to be a big business on its own. It needs to change how people feel about the brand.

The flying Roadster is that idea taken to its logical, cinematic extreme. Almost no one will own a car that lifts off the ground. That is not the point. The point is that a flying Tesla makes *Tesla* feel, once again, like the company building the future, which quietly benefits every ordinary Model that sits in a showroom.

## Why the timing is the real signal

The most revealing thing about a halo product is usually *when* it appears. A spectacular flagship is a cheap, high-leverage way to shift a narrative, to pull attention away from hard questions (competition, margins, slowing demand, delays) and back toward vision and leadership.

When a company under pressure unveils something jaw-dropping, it is making a bet: that a single viral moment of "look what we can do" is worth more to the brand than a quarter of careful messaging about fundamentals. For a company whose entire valuation has always rested on being perceived as a step ahead, protecting that perception is not vanity. It is core strategy. The halo product is how you defend the story.

## Selling the vision, not the units

The deeper mechanic here is that Tesla has always sold *the future* as much as it sells cars. Reservations and deposits on a barely-real flagship are, in effect, customers paying to buy into the vision, to place a bet that the company will deliver something extraordinary. That is a different transaction from buying a car. It is buying membership in a story about where the world is going.

This is why obsessing over whether the flying Roadster is practical misreads it. Its value is not utility. Its value is *symbolism*: proof that the company can still do the impossible, which keeps the brand attached to words like "future," "innovation," and "leader." For a brand built on those words, that symbolism is a real, if intangible, asset.

## The catch: vision versus over-promising

Here is the honest tension, and it is the thing that decides whether a halo product helps or hurts. The line between *visionary* and *over-promising* is thin, and easy to cross.

A halo works when it is a credible extension of what a company already does well, and when the spectacle is eventually backed by something real. It stops working, and starts actively damaging trust, when a company repeatedly sells dazzling futures that arrive very late or never arrive at all. Do that often enough and the halo inverts: each new spectacle is met not with awe but with skepticism, and the brand's credibility, its most valuable asset, erodes. Spectacle is a loan against trust. It only pays off if you can repay it with delivery.

## Risks and open questions

- **Spectacle can read as distraction.** If the core business has real problems, a flying car can look like a company changing the subject rather than solving them.
- **Credibility is finite.** Each visionary promise draws down trust. Without eventual delivery somewhere in the lineup, the halo stops lifting and starts dragging.
- **The halo must sit on a solid core.** Brand lift only converts to value if there are strong, affordable products underneath for the halo to lift. A halo over a weak core lifts nothing.
- **Details are fluid.** Treat the specifics of this demonstration as directional and verify against the latest reporting before relying on them.

## Takeaways for product managers

1. **A product's job isn't always to sell in volume.** A halo product can be a rational investment even if it never ships at scale, because its real output is *brand perception*, and perception lifts the whole lineup. Measure it by the halo, not the unit sales.
2. **One flagship can re-rate an entire brand.** The dazzling thing at the top changes how people feel about everything beneath it. When you need to shift perception, a single spectacular product can do more than a hundred incremental improvements.
3. **Watch the timing of vision.** Halo moments often appear exactly when a company needs to change the narrative. That is not cynical; it is strategic, but it only works if the underlying business can benefit from the lift.
4. **Build the halo on a solid core, not instead of one.** Spectacle borrows against trust. It pays off only when the company keeps delivering real products that justify the vision, so keep the promise close enough to reality that it re-rates the brand instead of overdrawing its credibility.
5. **Symbolism is a real asset for a story-driven brand.** For companies whose valuation rests on being seen as the leader, protecting and refreshing that perception is core work, not theater, as long as it stays tethered to genuine capability.
