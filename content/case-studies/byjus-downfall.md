**BYJU'S did not fail because the market disappeared or a competitor out-built it. It failed because the business model only worked while it was growing, and the moment growth paused, every mechanism that had powered the ascent — loan-financed contracts, debt-funded acquisitions, aggressive sales — reversed into a mechanism of collapse.** By 2022 the company was worth $22 billion, employed more than 50,000 people, and owned a dozen edtech businesses. By 2024 the founder had been removed from the board, lenders had frozen the Irish subsidiary, and regulators in two countries were investigating. The arc from most valuable Indian startup to NCLT insolvency took barely two years, and it is one of the largest destructions of equity value in startup history.

The instructive part is not that it ended badly. It is that the ending was encoded in the beginning. Every decision that looked like acceleration was also adding fragility, and no one priced the fragility until it was too late to unwind.

## A good product sitting on a dangerous financial engine

The problem BYJU'S set out to solve was real. India's middle-class parents wanted consistent, high-quality instruction for competitive exams that gate access to good colleges and good jobs, and traditional coaching was expensive, local, and wildly uneven. BYJU'S premium app, narrated by Byju Raveendran himself, promised that quality at scale, and the early product — the math instruction especially — was genuinely good. Parents responded.

But the product and the business model were two different things, and only one of them was sound. Revenue depended on selling expensive multi-year contracts to parents, frequently financed through loans the company helped arrange. Acquiring each of those customers required heavy spending on a large, incentivized sales force and relentless brand marketing. That produces a specific and dangerous shape: customer acquisition cost is paid up front and in cash, while the revenue that justifies it arrives slowly, over years, and only if the customer stays satisfied and keeps paying. The model is solvent only while new sales grow fast enough to fund the cost of the last cohort. It is, in effect, a business that must keep sprinting to stay standing — and growth always eventually slows.

## The acquisition spree bought complexity, not a moat

Between 2020 and 2022 BYJU'S spent over $2.5 billion acquiring more than a dozen companies: WhiteHat Jr ($300M, kids' coding), Aakash Educational Services ($950M, physical test-prep centers), Great Learning ($600M), Toppr ($150M), Tynker, Epic, HashLearn and others. Three separate logics justified the spree. The strategic logic was to become a one-stop edtech conglomerate spanning every age, subject and format. The competitive logic was that buying fast would build a moat against Unacademy, Vedantu and PhysicsWallah. The financial logic was that the COVID surge in online learning would validate almost any price.

All three were wrong in the same way: each treated acquisition as a substitute for integration. The businesses were bought but never stitched together — each carried its own unit economics, its own culture, its own operating model, and none folded into a coherent whole. The COVID surge turned out to be a one-time bubble that deflated the moment schools reopened. And the competitors the deals were meant to repel proved more capital-disciplined than BYJU'S, which is the detail that should trouble any acquirer: the spree did not out-position rivals, it simply loaded the balance sheet with goodwill that only growth could support.

## The subsidy stops, and the engine runs backward

The execution failures surfaced everywhere at once, because they shared a root. WhiteHat Jr — the most expensive purchase relative to revenue — became a public scandal over aggressive sales tactics and a hostile culture. Aakash, a physical chain, was structurally a different business that never fit the app-first playbook. Sales practices at the core product drew mounting scrutiny, with ex-employees describing pressure to sell unaffordable contracts to anxious parents. Then the financial scaffolding gave way: auditors raised revenue-recognition concerns, Deloitte resigned as auditor in 2023, three independent board members resigned the same day, and lenders froze the Ireland-based subsidiary that held a $1.2 billion term loan over alleged missed disclosures.

From there the same machinery that drove the ascent drove the descent. Revenue stagnated and declined; the company missed debt-service payments; Prosus marked its stake down by about 90 percent. Raveendran was removed by an extraordinary general meeting, India's Enforcement Directorate filed a money-laundering case, and the SEC opened an investigation into US subsidiaries. By 2024-25 the company was in NCLT insolvency, with creditor groups petitioning for liquidation. Aakash was separated, WhiteHat Jr wound down, Great Learning sold. What remained by 2026 was a shell of acquisitions being unwound, litigation against the founder, and more than $20 billion of destroyed equity value — a figure that may still grow as residual claims settle.

## The damage did not stay inside BYJU'S

A collapse this large resets a whole category. Investor confidence in Indian edtech evaporated; funding to the sector fell by roughly 80 percent or more between 2022 and 2024. Companies once considered second-tier behind BYJU'S, including Unacademy and Vedantu, were forced into severe layoffs and pivots. Foreign investors grew more cautious about Indian unicorn valuations generally, and employees and customers across the acquired companies were left in limbo as licensing disputes stranded content buyers had already paid for. The externalities of a growth-at-all-costs failure are rarely contained to the company that chose the strategy.

## What this means for operators

- **A model that only works while growing is a liability disguised as momentum.** If paying for the last cohort depends on selling the next one faster, you are not scaling a business, you are outrunning a reckoning. Stress-test every plan against the question BYJU'S never answered: what happens the first quarter growth pauses?
- **Acquisition is not integration, and goodwill is not a moat.** Twelve companies in two years produced an unmanageable conglomerate, not a defensible one. If you cannot articulate the operating synergy before the deal — and staff the integration after it — you are buying complexity and calling it strategy.
- **Fragile unit economics punish premium pricing hardest.** Expensive, loan-financed contracts amplify every dissatisfied customer into reputational risk. When outcomes failed to justify the price, negative word-of-mouth compounded into a market-wide credibility crisis the marketing budget could not outspend.
- **Founder control is an asset in the climb and a hazard in the fall.** Raveendran's outsized authority accelerated decisions when they were right and removed the checks that would have caught them when they were wrong. Governance is not bureaucracy; it is the mechanism that lets a company survive its founder's worst call.
