**Substack is the definitive case that restraint can be a strategy — that deliberately not building the algorithm everyone expected was its strongest differentiator. In 2017 the business of writing online worked for platforms and almost no one else: writers built audiences on [Twitter](/compare/twitter-vs-substack) and Medium that they didn't own and could lose to a feed-ranking change overnight. Chris Best and his co-founders bet the opposite premise — that enough readers would pay directly for a writer they trusted — and built an intentionally minimal product around the one asset a creator can truly own: the email list. The lesson is that owning the customer relationship is the most valuable thing you can give a creator, and that aligning your revenue with their success (taking a cut only when they get paid) buys trust no marketing can. (See the head-to-head in [Twitter vs Substack](/compare/twitter-vs-substack).)**

Every other platform inserted itself between the writer and the reader. Substack's whole bet was to get out of the way and charge only when the writer won.

## A broken economy for everyone but the platform

In 2017, digital publishers chased ad dollars with high-volume, low-margin content tuned for social feeds, and individual writers discovered they didn't own the audiences they'd built — a single algorithm change could erase their reach. Medium kept pivoting its monetization, leaving contributors uncertain they'd ever be paid. The prevailing belief was that readers would pay only for bundled publications, never for individuals. Best, Hamish McKenzie, and Jairaj Sethi founded Substack on the contrary bet that readers would pay directly for writing they valued, if the friction of collecting that money disappeared.

## The inbox as owned real estate

The problem was structural, not technological. The tools to send email and charge a card already existed, but stitching them together turned a writer into a part-time engineer and accountant — and every platform inserted itself between writer and reader, owning distribution or skimming monetization. The writer was a tenant subject to eviction by algorithm. Substack's insight was that the email inbox is the last piece of digital real estate a creator can actually own, because a subscriber list is portable and a direct relationship can't be throttled by a feed.

## Deliberately building less

The decisive move was to build less, not more. Substack launched intentionally minimal — a clean editor, a subscriber list, [Stripe](/case-study/stripe-developer-first) payments — with no recommendation algorithm, no engagement-maximizing feed, no gamification. That was a strategic stance disguised as a feature set: the founders argued algorithmic distribution corrupts writing by rewarding outrage and volume, and refused to build it despite pressure. Its pricing reinforced the stance — nothing for free newsletters, 10% only of paid revenue — aligning the company's success entirely with writers earning money rather than with traffic or ad impressions.

## Proof, then the pull of growth

Substack recruited credible writers to prove the model, sometimes offering advances through Substack Pro to lure marquee names. When Casey Newton left The Verge for Platformer, or Heather Cox Richardson turned a history newsletter into a six-figure business, each became a recruitment ad for the next. It crossed one million paid subscriptions in 2021 and passed four million, overturning the belief that micro-publications couldn't be monetized — especially for niche expertise where a small, devoted audience sustains a full-time income. Success then drew competitors (Ghost, Beehiiv) and pulled Substack toward the very features it had resisted — a Notes feed, an app, recommendations — testing which of its founding principles were truly load-bearing.

## What this means for operators

- **Restraint can be the product.** Choosing not to build the expected algorithm was Substack's differentiator — the absence of engagement-bait was the value. Ask what everyone expects you to build that you'd be stronger for refusing.
- **Align your model with your users' success.** Taking a cut only when writers get paid builds trust no campaign can buy. Make your revenue contingent on your users winning.
- **Give creators the relationship, not just the tools.** The owned, portable subscriber list was the real gift. Platforms that disintermediate their own users eventually lose them.
- **Expect the gravitational pull of growth.** Even a company founded on minimalism faced relentless pressure to add what it rejected. Decide in advance which principles are load-bearing and which are negotiable.
