**Facebook Home failed because it confused a product people loved to visit with a product people wanted to live inside. Even Facebook's most engaged users — people who spent hours a day in the feed — did not want Facebook to become their phone's operating system, because a phone's home screen is personal territory and Home annexed it. The distinction is the whole lesson: visiting a product and surrendering your device to it are different acts of consent, and no amount of love for the former buys the latter. User agency over their own device is non-negotiable, and a product that removes it will be rejected regardless of how popular its content is.**

The instructive part is that Home was built on a real, correct insight about attention — and still failed, because it tried to convert attention into control the user had never agreed to give.

## The ambition: become the layer, not the app

In April 2013 Facebook launched Home, an Android launcher that replaced the phone's entire home screen with a constantly updating feed called Cover Feed. Instead of an app you opened, Facebook would become the operating layer of your mobile experience — the first thing you saw every time you picked up the phone. Zuckerberg framed it as a paradigm shift: "We're not building a phone, and we're not building an operating system. We're building something that's a whole lot deeper than an app." HTC built a dedicated device, the HTC First, with Home as the default.

## A correct insight pointed at the wrong prize

The insight behind Home was real. In an app-based world, Facebook was one icon among dozens, and its data showed users spent hours on their phones but only a fraction inside the Facebook app. Home was an attempt to capture the time before a user chose which app to open — to own the idle, pre-decision surface. From Facebook's seat, owning the home screen was the ultimate distribution advantage. The error was not the observation that the home screen is valuable attention; it was concluding that Facebook was entitled to take it. Attention you earn inside your app is not a mandate to seize the surface outside it.

## Why the home screen was the wrong thing to annex

A phone's home screen is deeply personal — curated over time into app icons, widgets, and shortcuts that reflect each person's priorities and habits. Home replaced all of it with a stream of posts the user had not asked for and could not customize. Opening your phone now showed friends' updates instead of your apps, your widgets, your organization, and users felt their phones had been hijacked. The execution deepened the offense: Cover Feed took the whole screen, so basic functions (the time, the camera, finding an app) required dismissing Facebook content first; Chat Heads deprioritized other apps' notifications; and the thing drained battery and data. The phone's most basic functionality had been subordinated to one company's content, and it felt like living inside an advertisement rather than using a tool.

## The market rejected it instantly

The response was immediate and brutal. The Play Store rating fell to 1.5 stars within days, with reviews like "This turns your phone into a Facebook phone" and "I want my home screen back." AT&T cut the HTC First from $99 to $0.99 within a month, and the phone was discontinued shortly after. Facebook quietly pulled Home from the Play Store with no announcement; total downloads were around 500,000 — a rounding error for a company with over a billion users. The speed of the rejection is the signal: this was not a feature that needed tuning, it was a boundary the market refused outright.

## The lesson Facebook itself absorbed

Home did not dent Facebook's trajectory — mobile ad revenue kept growing through the standard apps — but it changed how the company moved. Messenger, Instagram, and WhatsApp were kept as separate apps with their own identities rather than merged into one monolithic experience, a direct inheritance from Home's overreach. The company learned that owning more of the user's surface is not the same as serving them, and that respecting the boundary was the more durable strategy.

## What this means for operators

- **Distinguish a product people visit from one they'll let run their device.** Love for your app does not transfer into consent to become the user's operating layer. The two are different grants of permission, and assuming the first buys the second is how popular products fail.
- **User agency over their own device is non-negotiable.** Any product that seizes control a user did not deliberately give will be rejected, no matter how good the content inside it. Enhance the user's control; never replace it.
- **A correct insight can still point at the wrong action.** Facebook was right that pre-app attention is valuable and wrong that it could take it. Validate not just that the prize exists but that the user will willingly hand it over.
- **There is a line between utility and intrusion, and the best products maximize value on the right side of it.** Market rejection of a boundary violation is fast and total — read a 1.5-star collapse as a verdict on the premise, not a bug list.
