**Miro is the definitive case that some of the most valuable products are built for a future that arrives on its own schedule — and that investing in genuine technical depth before demand peaks means you're ready when the wave hits instead of scrambling under fire. For most of a decade, Miro built a multiplayer infinite canvas almost nobody urgently needed, solving the brutally hard engineering of real-time collaboration while the world still whiteboarded in person. When COVID forced knowledge work remote in March 2020, the latent need became urgent overnight, and Miro was the only mature product ready to absorb it. The lesson is that infrastructure can be a moat and timing is often exogenous: you can't manufacture the shift, but you can position so that when the need becomes acute, you're the obvious answer.**

Miro spent nine years building the hard part no one was asking for. That patience is exactly why it didn't have to build it under pressure when the world changed.

## A product for a future that hadn't arrived

When the founders started RealtimeBoard in 2011, a shared digital whiteboard was a niche curiosity. Collaboration happened in person around physical whiteboards, or in documents one person edited at a time. The team believed visual, spatial collaboration deserved a digital home that worked across distance — but for most of a decade it was a hard sell. Teams were colocated, video was clunky, and the pain of not being able to brainstorm visually with a remote teammate simply wasn't acute enough to pay for a dedicated tool. Miro grew steadily but unspectacularly, building for a future that had not yet come.

## A real but latent problem

The need was real and dormant. Knowledge work increasingly involved distributed contributors, yet the most generative collaboration — workshops, brainstorms, journey mapping, retrospectives — stayed stubbornly physical. A remote participant was relegated to a grainy video square while the real thinking happened on a whiteboard they couldn't touch. Documents captured conclusions but not the messy, spatial process of reaching them. The gap was a true multiplayer surface where dozens could think together visually at once — and demand for solving it was almost entirely theoretical until something forced the world apart.

## Patient investment in the hard part

The decisive choice was to invest deeply in the hardest parts long before the market rewarded it. An infinite, zoomable canvas with many simultaneous editors demands real-time synchronization, conflict resolution, performance as boards grow to thousands of objects, and reliable cross-device rendering. Many competitors offered lightweight whiteboards that buckled under real collaborative load; Miro treated the multiplayer infrastructure as the core product and poured years into making it robust and genuinely real-time. It paired that with a large template library — agile boards, retrospectives, journey maps — that defused blank-canvas anxiety and showed teams concrete ways to use the surface. An unglamorous, compounding bet on depth over flash.

## The moment the wave hit

Execution met its moment in March 2020, when the pandemic sent knowledge workers home overnight. The latent pain turned urgent and universal: in-person workshops, design sprints, and planning sessions all had to move online, and Miro was the most mature product ready to absorb the demand because it had already built the hard infrastructure and the templates that made visual collaboration immediately usable. Adoption exploded, the freemium model spread it organically within organizations, and the company raised a round in early 2022 valuing it near $17.5 billion. The years of unrewarded investment paid off precisely because, when demand arrived, Miro didn't have to build the hard part under pressure — it already had it.

## What this means for operators

- **Build depth before demand peaks.** The most valuable products are sometimes made for a future that arrives on its own schedule. Invest in genuine technical depth early so you're ready when the wave hits instead of scrambling.
- **Infrastructure can be the moat.** Real-time multiplayer at scale is hard enough that years of patient engineering created an advantage competitors couldn't quickly match. The unglamorous core can be the defensibility.
- **Lower the activation barrier as much as the capability.** Miro's templates turned an intimidating blank canvas into immediate value. The best underlying engine still needs a frictionless first use.
- **Timing is often exogenous — position for it.** You can't manufacture a pandemic-scale shift, but you can be the obvious answer when a latent need suddenly becomes urgent.
