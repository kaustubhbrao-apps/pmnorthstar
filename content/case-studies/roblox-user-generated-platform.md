**Roblox is the purest proof that the highest-leverage product is often not the thing users consume but the tools that let them create it. Founded in 2006, Roblox built no games of its own; it built the engine, the social layer, and — crucially — a real economy, then let millions of young creators make the games and keep the money. The result dwarfs any single studio: over 70 million daily users and more than $700 million paid to creators in a single year, for content the company never financed. The lesson is that when you tool and monetize a creative community properly, it generates value at a scale no internal team could ever match.**

The conventional path was to make a great game and hope for a hit. Roblox's path was to make no games and own the platform every game runs on.

## The wall that kept kids from creating

David Baszucki and Erik Cassel founded Roblox in 2006 on a lesson from Baszucki's earlier company, Knowledge Revolution, whose physics-simulation software students used to invent experiments far beyond what the designers intended. Game creation at the time was locked behind expertise: even a simple multiplayer game needed programming, art, servers, and distribution — an insurmountable wall for a twelve-year-old with an idea. Yet the demand to build, not just play, was enormous and unmet. The gap was a missing platform that handled all the hard infrastructure so that creativity was the only required input — the YouTube of games.

## An economy is a feature

The decisive move was to make Roblox a platform with a real economy rather than a free hobbyist tool. Roblox Studio gave creators a genuinely powerful engine for free, but the masterstroke was Robux — a virtual currency players spend inside games that creators can convert back into real dollars through the Developer Exchange. That turned a children's building tool into an economic engine: a kid could build a game, earn real money, and reinvest in better assets and collaborators. By handing creators financial upside, Roblox aligned its own growth with the ambition of its most talented users, turning hobbyists into entrepreneurs who ran their games as businesses.

## Nurturing both sides of the flywheel

Execution meant feeding creator and player sides at once. For creators: heavy investment in Studio, documentation, revenue share, and later funds and conferences to professionalize top studios. For players: social features — avatars, friends, chat — that made Roblox a place to hang out rather than a game to finish. Hit experiences like Adopt Me! and Brookhaven drew tens of millions of players and life-changing income for the small teams behind them. Roblox also went where the kids were, pushing across PC, mobile, and console so children could play together regardless of device, removing the fragmentation that fractures most multiplayer games.

## Scale, and the responsibility it brings

The thesis was vindicated spectacularly — 70M+ daily users, most of them young, and a creator economy paying out over $700M in a year — and Roblox went public in 2021 in the tens of billions, valued almost entirely on community-made content. But the same scale brought hard questions: how much of the economy Roblox retains versus what creators receive, and the safety obligations of a platform whose users and contributors are largely minors. Child-safety scrutiny and moderation became permanent features of operating something this large and this young.

## What this means for operators

- **Tools can out-scale content.** The product that lets users create often generates more value than anything you could build in-house. Ask whether your highest-leverage move is the thing people consume or the thing that lets them make it.
- **An economy turns hobbyists into partners.** Letting creators earn real money converts casual builders into committed entrepreneurs and aligns their incentives with yours.
- **The boring infrastructure is the moat.** Payments, networking, and moderation are unglamorous and exactly what competitors can't easily replicate. Invest in the pipes.
- **Scale is responsibility, not just growth.** When your users and contributors are children, safety and fair compensation are core product obligations, not edge cases to patch later.
