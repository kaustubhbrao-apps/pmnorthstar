## The Crushed Dream of the SMS Search Engine

In 2007, Naveen Tewari, an IIT Kanpur and Harvard Business School graduate, was sitting in a cramped apartment in Mumbai alongside co-founders Amit Gupta, Mohit Saxena, and Abhay Singhal.

Their startup was called **mKhoj**, an SMS-based local search engine. The thesis seemed clever: most Indians possessed simple feature phones without internet access. If a user wanted to find an Italian restaurant in Mumbai, they sent an SMS to mKhoj, and human researchers looked up the answer and texted back.

Within six months, the business was in financial ruin:
- Telecom carriers charged mKhoj hefty commercial fees for every outbound SMS.
- Users treated it as a free novelty, asking trivial trivia questions.
- Revenue was practically zero, and the company was weeks away from burning through its angel savings.

The founders sat on the floor of their apartment with maxed-out personal credit cards. They realized that SMS search had no future. But during their trials, they noticed a critical technical shift: mobile phone carriers were beginning to roll out **GPRS mobile internet data** across developing nations.

Even on cheap Nokia feature phones running Symbian, users were starting to browse primitive mobile websites.

## The Global Emerging Market Arbitrage

In 2008, the founders rebranded mKhoj to **InMobi** and executed a daring pivot: **they set out to build a global mobile advertising network.**

At the time, desktop advertising was completely monopolized by Google and Yahoo. But mobile advertising was a chaotic frontier. In Silicon Valley, startups like AdMob were building mobile ad networks for the emerging iPhone market.

InMobi made a brilliant strategic decision: **they refused to compete in the United States.**

Instead, InMobi focused exclusively on high-growth, under-served emerging markets across Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America:
- While Google and AdMob ignored Indonesia, Kenya, and Nigeria, InMobi partnered with thousands of regional mobile publishers and game developers across those countries.
- InMobi optimized its lightweight ad SDKs to run flawlessly on slow 2G mobile networks and resource-constrained Nokia and BlackBerry devices.
- Because InMobi had the only functional ad network across developing markets, global brands like Nokia, Unilever, and Samsung turned to InMobi to reach millions of mobile consumers across Asia and Africa.

By the time Google acquired AdMob for $750 million in 2009, InMobi had quietly built the second-largest mobile ad network on Earth, processing billions of ad impressions every single month.

## Conquering the United States and Europe

Having conquered emerging markets, Tewari made an audacious move: **he attacked the Western incumbents on their home turf.**

InMobi opened major engineering and sales hubs in San Francisco, London, and Tokyo:
- **Rich Media & Interactive HTML5 Ads:** InMobi pioneered interactive, gamified mobile ads. Instead of static banner ads that users accidentally clicked, InMobi designed rich-media ads where users could swipe 3D cars, play mini-games, or scratch digital coupons directly inside mobile apps.
- **Performance App-Install Engine:** When mobile apps on iOS and Android exploded, InMobi built algorithms that predicted user lifetime value, helping gaming companies like Supercell, Zynga, and King acquire high-spending mobile players.

By 2011, InMobi was delivering over **30 billion ad impressions per month** across 165 countries, directly challenging Google AdMob and Apple’s iAd network.

## The First Indian Unicorn

In September 2011, Masayoshi Son, the legendary founder of SoftBank, summoned Naveen Tewari to Tokyo. Son was astounded that an Indian engineering team had built a globally dominant deep-tech advertising platform operating across dozens of countries.

SoftBank invested **$200 million** into InMobi at a $1 billion valuation. 

It was the largest venture funding round in the history of the Indian technology industry, and officially crowned InMobi as **India’s first technology unicorn**.

InMobi proved to an entire generation of Indian engineers and founders that Indian companies didn't have to be low-cost back-office IT services contractors for Western corporations; an Indian deep-tech startup could build global, cutting-edge software and compete head-to-head with Google and Apple on the world stage.

## Deep-Dive Takeaways for Builders

1. **Avoid the Well-Funded War; Dominate the Overlooked Perimeter:** InMobi avoided competing with Google and AdMob in the US. By capturing emerging markets in Asia and Africa first, it built global scale and cash flow before attacking Western markets from a position of strength.
2. **Optimize for Low-Bandwidth Realities:** Building an ad SDK that performed reliably on sluggish 2G networks with low-memory feature phones gave InMobi an unbeatable performance advantage across billions of emerging market consumers.
3. **Pivots Require Burning the Ships:** When mKhoj's SMS search model failed, the founders didn't tinker around the edges; they shut it down completely, maxed out their credit cards, and committed 100% of their energy to mobile advertising. Decisive pivots save dying startups.
