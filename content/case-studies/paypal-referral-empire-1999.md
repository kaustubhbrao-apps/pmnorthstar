**PayPal's $20-per-signup referral program is the origin story of growth hacking, and its real lesson is counterintuitive: in a network-effects business, paying to acquire users at a loss can be the most rational move on the board. Confinity gave every new user $20, and another $20 for each friend they brought, deliberately burning millions in venture capital on the bet that in digital payments the only durable moat is density. Growth hit 7 to 10 percent per day, PayPal crossed a million users within months, and the network it bought became so entrenched on eBay that eBay was eventually forced to acquire it for $1.5 billion. The lesson is that a high acquisition cost is only reckless if you misjudge the lifetime value of the network you are buying.**

Critics called it suicidal because they were pricing a single user. Thiel and Levchin were pricing a node in a network.

## The cold-start trap

Confinity, co-founded by Peter Thiel and Max Levchin, started by beaming money between Palm Pilots over infrared before pivoting to email-based payments on the web. That pivot ran straight into the hardest problem in payments: a network is worthless until enough people are already on it. PayPal needed critical mass fast, before well-funded banking incumbents woke up — and traditional banner ads and PR were too expensive and converted too poorly to get there in time.

## Paying people to join

So PayPal did something audacious: it bribed people. Starting in late 1999, signing up deposited $20 of real, usable digital cash into your wallet, and referring a friend earned both of you another $20. In an era before digital wallets, free spendable money was an irresistible pitch, and it went viral across early internet forums and email chains. Growth exploded to 7–10% per day — and the company hemorrhaged venture capital doing it, which is exactly what the critics fixated on.

## Why the "reckless" CAC was rational

The skeptics argued PayPal was buying disloyal users who would grab the $20 and vanish. They were pricing a user; Thiel and Levchin were pricing a node. In a two-sided payment network, each new member raises the value of the network for everyone else, so a $20 acquisition cost bought a position in a compounding, inescapable web. The effect crystallized on eBay: sellers began adding "PayPal Accepted Here" to listings to attract buyers, which pulled more buyers onto PayPal, which pulled in more sellers. PayPal had effectively weaponized eBay's own traffic to build its monopoly — and as organic adoption took over, it tapered the incentive from $20 to $10 to $5 to zero, watching acquisition cost fall off a cliff as lifetime value soared.

## The outcome that proved it

PayPal hit 1 million users by March 2000 and roughly 5 million by that summer. It went public in early 2002 and was acquired by eBay later that year for $1.5 billion — a deal forced less by negotiation than by the sheer inevitability of PayPal's dominance on eBay's platform. The "bribe" had bought a network eBay could not dislodge, so it bought the network instead.

## What this means for operators

- **In a network-effects market, buy density, not users.** A high acquisition cost is cheap if each user raises the value of the whole network. Judge CAC against the lifetime value of the network, not the individual.
- **Incentivize both sides, then taper.** Rewarding referrer and referee compounds the loop; declining the incentive as organic growth takes over keeps the spend finite.
- **Parasitize a network where your users already gather.** PayPal grew fastest by riding eBay's existing liquidity. Find the platform that already aggregates your demand and attach to it.
- **Make sure the incentive produces active nodes, not vanity signups.** PayPal's bought users actually transacted, which is what made the network real. An incentive that buys dormant accounts buys nothing.
