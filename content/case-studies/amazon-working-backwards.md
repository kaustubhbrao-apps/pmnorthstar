**Amazon's Working Backwards process exists to prevent the single most common and expensive failure in product development: building something impressive that nobody wants. It does this by inverting the usual sequence — the team writes the launch-day press release before writing a line of code, forcing the customer value proposition to be articulated and tested before any resources are committed. The genius is not the document; it is the discipline it imposes. If you cannot explain, in two plain paragraphs, why a customer would care, no amount of engineering will save the product — and Working Backwards makes you confront that at the cheapest possible moment, not after months of build.**

The process has produced AWS, Kindle, Alexa, and Prime, but its real contribution is a repeatable way to kill bad ideas early and clarify good ones before they cost anything.

## The ritual: a press release before any code

At Amazon, a new product begins not with a spec, a prototype, or a competitive analysis, but with the announcement that would go out on launch day. Formalized around 2004, the press release must state the customer problem, the proposed solution, the benefits in the customer's own language, and a fictional customer quote expressing genuine delight. If it reads as boring, confusing, or unconvincing, the product does not advance — regardless of how technically feasible or strategically interesting it is. The document is a filter that runs before the expensive work starts.

## The failure it's engineered to prevent

Working Backwards targets a specific, costly failure mode. Traditional development starts from technical capabilities or competitive features and hopes to find customers later, which Amazon saw producing an unacceptable rate of expensive misses — engineers spending months building against assumptions about customer needs, only to learn at launch the assumptions were wrong. The press release flips the sequence: it forces the value proposition to be confronted before any investment, so a weak idea dies on a page instead of after a quarter of engineering. The entire value is in moving the moment of truth upstream, where being wrong is cheap.

## Why the format itself does the work

Bezos's real decision was not inventing the press release but making it non-negotiable and pairing it with a FAQ that answers two kinds of questions — external (pricing, availability, compatibility) and internal (architecture, cost, timeline). Together they form a six-page narrative memo that replaced the PowerPoint decks Bezos banned, on the belief that slides reward presentation skill and superficial thinking over analytical rigor. Prose forces complete arguments where bullets permit gaps. The format is not bureaucracy; it is a constraint that makes sloppy reasoning visible.

## The meeting ritual that removes politics

Execution is deliberate. Every product review opens with 20–30 minutes of silent reading, so everyone shares the same understanding before discussion — eliminating the asymmetry where the presenter prepared for hours and attendees wing it. The conversation then stays on the document: Is the customer problem real? Is the solution compelling? Are the benefits clearly stated? By anchoring to a written customer narrative, the discussion is about the customer rather than internal politics or the loudest voice, which is how the process also democratizes product thinking — anyone can write a press release, so good ideas can come from anywhere.

## The proof: the products it produced

The method generated Amazon's most transformative products, each born as a press release before a product. AWS imagined any developer renting computing on demand without buying servers. Kindle imagined downloading any book in 60 seconds. Alexa imagined a voice assistant controlling your home. Prime imagined unlimited two-day shipping for a flat annual fee. In each, the press release clarified the vision in a way specs never could, because it forced the benefit into plain, jargon-free customer language — and the clarity of that sentence is what told the team whether the thing was worth building. The framework has since spread industry-wide, taught alongside jobs-to-be-done and design sprints and documented in the book *Working Backwards* by former Amazon executives Colin Bryar and Bill Carr.

## What this means for operators

- **Write the launch announcement before you build.** If the press release is boring, confusing, or unconvincing, the product is too — and you have learned it for the cost of a page instead of a quarter. Move the test of value proposition upstream of engineering.
- **If you can't explain the benefit in two plain paragraphs, more features won't help.** A weak value proposition is not fixed by adding scope; it is fixed by finding a real customer problem. Articulating customer value in simple language is the core PM skill, and any process that forces it improves outcomes.
- **Prose exposes what slides hide.** A narrative memo demands complete arguments where bullet points let gaps pass. Make people write and read in full sentences when the decision matters.
- **Anchor decisions to a shared written artifact, read in the room.** Silent reading removes preparation asymmetry and keeps the discussion on the customer rather than politics. It also lets good ideas surface from anywhere, not just from senior people with the loudest voices.
