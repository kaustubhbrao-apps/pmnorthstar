Here is a strange thing to do: build a payments app in a market where payments make no money, against two incumbents who already own roughly 85% of the market, after your parent company already lost the biggest payments app in the country. That is the starting position super.money chose. Flipkart once owned PhonePe — India's largest UPI app — but PhonePe was spun out and fully separated in 2022, taking Flipkart's fintech crown jewel with it. In July 2024, Flipkart walked back into the same brutal market with a new app, super.money, led by Prakash Sikaria, a former senior Flipkart executive who had earlier founded Shopsy. Within about seven months it had climbed to become India's **fifth-largest UPI app**, overtaking Kunal Shah's CRED. The interesting question is not how it grew — aggressive cashback will always buy growth — but *why Flipkart would re-enter a zero-margin business at all*, and what the answer reveals about a smarter way to think about payments.

## The core problem: UPI is a business that doesn't make money

To understand super.money, you have to understand the trap of Indian payments. UPI — the Unified Payments Interface — is the rail that runs almost all digital payments in India, and for more than six years it carried a **zero merchant discount rate**. No MDR means that when you pay a merchant over UPI, nobody along the chain earns a fee on the transaction. The payment is free for the consumer and, crucially, free of revenue for the app that processed it.

This produces one of the strangest dynamics in global fintech. The two giants, PhonePe and Google Pay, process billions of transactions and command around 85% of the market combined — and the core payment activity itself generates essentially no direct revenue. Apps spend heavily to acquire users and process volume, then have to find *something else* to monetize. Payments, in India, are not a business. They are a distribution channel in search of one. Any new entrant who treats UPI market share as the goal is running expensively toward a prize that pays nothing.

super.money's entire strategy is a refusal to make that mistake.

## The founding insight: payments are the wedge, credit is the business

super.money is best described not as a UPI app but as a **credit-first app that happens to lead with UPI**. The payment is the top of the funnel — the cheap, high-frequency, habit-forming reason to open the app every day. The actual business sits underneath it: lending and financial products sold to the users that payments brings in.

The revenue mix makes this unmistakable. Of super.money's roughly $3 million in monthly revenue (about a $36M annualized run-rate), an estimated **80% comes from personal loans**, around **10% from credit cards**, and only about **10% from payment-linked products**. In other words, the thing the app is famous for — UPI payments — is its *smallest* revenue line. The payment is not the product being monetized; it is the acquisition engine for the products that are. This is the move Flipkart learned the hard way by losing PhonePe: owning the payment habit is worthless unless you also own the monetizable financial relationship on top of it.

Framed this way, the economics flip from hopeless to elegant. super.money is effectively willing to run payments at a loss, treating cashback and processing cost as customer-acquisition spend, because it has a second act — credit — where the money actually is. The UPI transaction is the cheapest, stickiest way to get a young Indian consumer into a daily relationship. Once they are in, super.money can underwrite them, lend to them, and sell them a card.

## Guaranteed cashback: turning rewards from a lottery into a product

The first visible expression of this strategy is how super.money does rewards. Most UPI apps reward you with scratch cards and luck-based prizes — pay a bill, get a chance at a random payout. super.money instead offers **guaranteed cashback on every payment**: you pay, you earn, every time, no gamble.

This looks like a small UX choice and is actually a sharp strategic one. A lottery reward is emotionally exciting but behaviorally noisy — users can't predict it, so it doesn't reliably change habits, and the company's reward spend is lumpy. Guaranteed cashback converts that spend into a *predictable, controllable customer-acquisition line*. super.money knows almost exactly what each active user costs in rewards, and it can treat that number as CAC against the lifetime value of the loans and cards it expects to sell them. It also builds a cleaner habit: because every payment pays, the rational move for a user is to route *all* their spending through super.money, which maximizes both frequency and the behavioral data super.money needs to underwrite them later. Guaranteed cashback is not generosity. It is a disciplined acquisition instrument disguised as a consumer perk.

## The contrarian thesis: 20-30 million high-value users, not 400 million

The decision that most separates super.money from the field is who it refuses to chase. PhonePe and Google Pay compete for *everyone* — hundreds of millions of users, the entire Indian smartphone base. Sikaria has been explicit that super.money is not playing that game: the target is **20-30 million high-value customers, not 400 million users**.

This is a deliberate, almost heretical choice in a market that worships monthly-active-user counts. But it follows directly from the credit-first model. If payments don't pay and lending does, then a user is only valuable if they are *monetizable* — creditworthy, willing to borrow, able to carry a card. A huge fraction of India's 400 million UPI users will never be profitable to a lender. So super.money optimizes for depth of monetization per user rather than breadth of reach. It would rather have 25 million users it can lend to than 250 million it can only process free payments for. Nearly 70% of its users are under 28 — young, early in their financial lives, exactly the cohort a credit-first platform wants to acquire early and grow with. The strategy treats scale as a means, not the goal; the goal is monetizable relationships.

Notably, it does all this with a remarkably lean team of roughly 130-150 people — a sign that the model is built on leverage and focus rather than brute-force headcount.

## The masterstroke: FD-backed secured credit cards

The sharpest product in super.money's arsenal solves the central contradiction of its own thesis. super.money wants to lend to young, under-28 users — but those users mostly have **thin or no credit history**, which makes them nearly impossible to underwrite and dangerous to lend to unsecured. How do you build a credit business on customers that no normal issuer would approve?

The answer is the **secured credit card**. The customer places a fixed deposit — as little as ₹1,000 — and that FD acts as collateral for the card. This elegantly dissolves the risk: super.money can issue credit *without income verification*, because the deposit backs the exposure, so default risk is minimal. The customer, in turn, gets a real credit card and — critically — starts building the credit score that unlocks their future financial life. It is a rare product where the risk-free version for the lender is also the on-ramp the customer actually needs.

The results validated it: super.money became **one of India's largest issuers of secured cards, with roughly a 10% market share**. It found an underserved, un-underwritable population and built exactly the instrument that makes them lendable. This is product design as risk engineering — the constraint (no credit history) didn't kill the business; it *defined* the product that became the business.

## Where they are now (2025-26): scale, a bank partner, and the end of free UPI

By late 2025, the trajectory was undeniable. super.money had scaled from around 2.5 million to **more than 250 million monthly transactions**, processing roughly ₹9,852 crore (about $1.03 billion) a month, with 15 million-plus users and around 85% retention. It sat fifth on the UPI leaderboard. Flipkart had put in about $50 million across two rounds ($20M in July 2024, $30M in September 2025), and super.money was reported to be raising a further $60-100 million at a valuation near $1 billion — Flipkart's second potential fintech unicorn, this time one it fully owns.

Two 2025-26 developments sharpen the story.

**The Kotak partnership.** In October 2025, super.money teamed with Kotak811 (Kotak Mahindra Bank's digital arm) to launch a "3-in-1 Super Account" bundling a savings account, UPI, and an FD-backed secured card — targeting roughly 2 million secured cards in 12 months and 5 million in two years. The partnership is expected to contribute around 10% of revenue the following year. It is the credit-first thesis industrialized: a bank partner supplies the balance-sheet and regulatory rails, super.money supplies the distribution, the UX, and the young user base.

**The end of free UPI.** This is the macro shift that may vindicate the entire model. From **15 October 2026**, India introduced a **0.4% MDR on person-to-merchant UPI transactions above ₹2,000** (capped at ₹300 per transaction; P2P payments, sub-₹2,000 payments, and small merchants remain free). After six-plus years, the rails can finally earn. For most UPI apps that monetized *nothing* on payments, this is a structural change in the business. For super.money, which already built its credit and card layer and wired up card-based rails through Kotak, it is pure tailwind: a company engineered to make money *around* zero-MDR payments now gets to make some money *on* the payments too. The business was designed for the hard version of the market; the market just got easier. Around the same window, super.money also rolled out products like SplitStore and quietly expanded its reach through a partnership with Juspay — widening the surface for both acquisition and monetization.

## The strategic tension, stated plainly

super.money is a bet that **the right few users beat the most users** — that in a zero-margin payments market, the winner is not whoever processes the most transactions but whoever converts the most transactions into lending relationships. It is, in essence, an anti-PhonePe strategy: let the giants own the mass-market, free-payment land-grab, and instead quietly assemble a smaller base of young, creditworthy users and monetize them deeply through loans and cards.

The risk is the mirror image of the strength. A business that is ~80% personal loans is, underneath the friendly cashback UX, a **lending company** — exposed to credit cycles, defaults, and the RBI's tightening scrutiny of digital lending. Growth bought with guaranteed cashback is only rational if the lifetime value of those users genuinely materializes; if monetization lags acquisition, the cashback becomes a subsidy with no second act. And "high-value users" is a narrower, more contested pool than it sounds — banks, CRED, and the giants all want the same affluent young borrower. super.money's edge is distribution and focus; its exposure is that it is lending-heavy in a market where lending is exactly what regulators watch most closely.

## Risks and open questions

- **It's a lending business wearing a payments UX.** With ~80% of revenue from personal loans, super.money carries real credit risk and sits squarely inside India's tightening digital-lending regulation. A credit cycle or a rule change hits the core, not the edge.
- **Cashback is a subsidy until the LTV shows up.** Guaranteed cashback is only disciplined CAC if the lending revenue per user actually arrives. If monetization trails acquisition, the model is just paying people to use free payments.
- **The high-value pool is crowded.** Chasing 20-30M monetizable users sounds focused, but every bank, CRED, and both UPI giants want that same creditworthy young borrower. Differentiation has to hold.
- **Dependence on bank partners.** The secured-card and account products rely on partners like Kotak for the balance sheet and compliance. That is leverage, but also a dependency super.money doesn't fully control.
- **Can it stay lean as it scales?** A 130-150 person team is a feature today. Scaling lending, compliance, and collections across tens of millions of borrowers may test how long that leanness survives.

## Takeaways for product managers

1. **A free, zero-margin product can be your best acquisition channel — if you have a second act.** super.money runs UPI at a loss on purpose, because payments are the cheapest way to build a daily habit and lending is where the money is. Don't ask "how do I monetize this feature?" Ask "what does this feature give me access to that I *can* monetize?"
2. **Choose your users, don't just count them.** The brave call was targeting 20-30M monetizable users instead of 400M vanity ones. When your revenue comes from a subset of users (here, borrowers), optimize every decision for *that* subset's value, not for the top-line MAU chart.
3. **Design the product around the user's real constraint.** Young users had no credit history, so unsecured lending was impossible — and the secured, FD-backed card was born from that exact constraint. The limitation didn't block the business; it specified the winning product.
4. **Make rewards a product, not a gamble.** Guaranteed cashback beats lottery scratch cards because it is predictable for the user (so it changes behavior) and predictable for the company (so it's a controllable CAC line). Turn your incentive spend into a measurable instrument.
5. **Position for the hard version of the market, and let tailwinds be upside.** super.money built a model that works even when payments earn zero. So when the rules changed and UPI payments started to earn (the 0.4% MDR), that wasn't a rescue — it was a bonus. Build for the brutal case; benefit when it eases.
