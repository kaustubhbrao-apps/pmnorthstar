**Miro and Figma look like rivals and are really neighbors: they own adjacent moments in the same product-development cycle. Miro owns the thinking layer — the whiteboard, the brainstorm, the workshop, the retrospective. Figma owns the building layer — the UI design, the prototype, the handoff. Both are multiplayer tools that spread by making collaboration feel real, and both are now racing to annex the other's adjacent moment and own the whole cycle. The interesting part is not the overlap but how differently each one spread through organizations, because that explains which company is better positioned to win the land in between.**

A team starts a feature in Miro, where the thinking is still loose, and moves to Figma once it has crystallized into something to build. The seam between those two moments is the prize both are fighting over.

## Two layers of the same process

Miro captured the feeling of a sprint's opening hours — sticky notes in clashing colors, lines connecting ideas that make sense in the room and look chaotic a week later, the artifact of a team thinking together before it knows what it is building. That became one of the fastest-growing productivity tools of the 2020s: 60 million users across 250,000 organizations and a $17.5 billion valuation by 2022. Figma captured a different feeling — a pixel-perfect design in the browser, edited simultaneously, the designer in San Francisco and the developer in Warsaw watching each other's cursors. Launched in 2016 on the bet that browsers were finally fast enough to run professional design software, it became the default for product design, all but replaced Sketch, and threatened Adobe enough to draw a $20 billion acquisition offer. Thinking and building are distinct jobs, and each company owns one of them.

## The race to close the seam

Because the two moments are adjacent and the handoff between them is awkward, both companies are trying to own the transition. Figma launched FigJam in 2021, a whiteboard aimed squarely at Miro's territory; Miro added template-driven design tools edging toward Figma's. It is the same play from both sides: capture the adjacent moment before the other does, and turn a point solution into ownership of the full cycle. Whoever closes the seam first makes the other's handoff friction into their own feature.

## Distribution shape decides who annexes whom

The decisive difference is how each spread. Figma moved bottom-up and deep within a function: one designer adopts it, the developer notices the inspection panel, the PM starts commenting directly on designs, and soon the whole product team is inside. Miro moved laterally and wide across functions: a consultant runs one workshop, invites fifteen people from different departments, and fifteen accounts are created. Figma's roots go deep in one discipline; Miro's spread thin across many. That shape matters for the land grab — Figma can push outward from a dense base that already owns the building moment, while Miro can push inward from broad reach that touches every function's thinking moment. Each is strong exactly where it started, which is why neither has taken the other's ground yet.

## What this means for operators

- **Map whether you own a moment or a cycle.** Point tools own a moment; platforms own the sequence. The durable position is owning the adjacent moments too, so the handoff happens inside your product rather than out of it.
- **The seam between tools is where expansion happens.** Awkward handoffs are not annoyances to tolerate; they are the territory. Close your customer's most painful transition before a neighbor closes it for you.
- **Your distribution shape is a strategic asset.** Deep-in-one-function and wide-across-functions enable different expansions. Know which you have, because it determines which adjacent moment you can realistically annex.
