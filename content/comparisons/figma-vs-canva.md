**[Figma](/case-study/figma-browser-design) and [Canva](/case-study/canva-freemium-design) launched within a year of each other, both became worth tens of billions, and they almost never compete for the same user. That is not luck — it is the entire strategy. Figma went deep on a small, high-value audience of professionals; Canva went wide on an enormous, low-friction audience of everyone else. The instructive part is their shared discipline: each refused to chase the other's customer, and that refusal is what made both category-defining. Depth and breadth are different markets, and one product serving both usually serves neither.**

The temptation in any tool category is to believe a great product should win every user. Figma and Canva are the counterexample — proof that choosing *which* users to be great for is the decision that compounds.

## Depth: power for specialists

Figma's wedge was collaboration. While Adobe and Sketch shipped single-player desktop tools, Figma put professional-grade design in the browser and made it multiplayer — anyone with a link could open, comment, and edit in real time. That rewired how product teams worked, and it spread bottom-up through exactly the people who valued it most: designers and the engineers and PMs around them. Crucially, Figma embraced a steep learning curve rather than fighting it, because its users were specialists who wanted power, not simplicity. The payoff is a monetization model of per-editor seats and enterprise contracts that extract high value from a relatively small base — and a position strong enough that Adobe tried to buy it for $20 billion before regulators blocked the deal.

## Breadth: simplicity for everyone else

Canva's wedge was the mirror image: radical simplicity. Its insight was that most people who need to make something look good are not designers at all — teachers, marketers, small-business owners, students — and for them the existing tools were intimidating to the point of uselessness. Canva replaced the blank canvas with templates and drag-and-drop, dropping the learning curve to near zero and unlocking a market orders of magnitude larger than professional design. It scaled past 200 million monthly active users through a viral freemium funnel and reached profitability by extracting a little from an enormous crowd rather than a lot from a few.

## Why not chasing the other customer was the win

The deeper point is that the learning curve was a strategic choice on both sides, not an accident of execution. Figma's complexity is a feature for specialists and a wall for everyone else; Canva's simplicity is liberating for amateurs and limiting for professionals. Had either tried to serve both, it would have had to blunt the exact trait that won its core market — Figma dumbing down its power, Canva adding complexity that scares off its base. By accepting that their strengths were also their boundaries, each built a product that was unambiguously the best for its chosen user, which is a far stronger position than being second-best for everyone.

## What this means for operators

- **Decide depth or breadth; they are different markets.** High value from a few power users and low value from a massive crowd are both great businesses, but they demand opposite products. Trying to span them usually forfeits the trait that would have won either.
- **A learning curve is a positioning decision, not just UX.** Complexity selects for specialists who want power; simplicity selects for the mainstream who want results. Choose the curve that matches the customer you actually want.
- **Refusing a customer is a strategy.** Figma and Canva each left the other's market on the table on purpose. The discipline to not pursue a tempting adjacent user is often what keeps the core product the best in its category.
