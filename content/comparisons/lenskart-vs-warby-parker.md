**Founded the same year to fix the same category, [Lenskart](/case-study/lenskart-omnichannel-eyewear) and [Warby Parker](/case-study/warby-parker-home-try-on) built opposite companies — because the market's dominant friction, not the founders' taste, dictated the playbook. The pairing isolates a variable most case studies cannot: hold the problem constant, change the geography, and watch the correct strategy invert.**

Both launched in 2010 against the same target: eyewear, dominated by a few incumbents selling overpriced frames through sleepy optical shops. Same diagnosis, opposite execution. The reason is the most transferable lesson in the comparison.

## The friction defines the wedge

Warby Parker, built in the United States, made two moves that defined D2C retail for a decade. The home try-on box — five frames mailed free — turned the frightening act of buying glasses sight-unseen into something risk-free and even fun. And a brand that felt like a tech startup, design-led and mission-driven, not an optician. This worked because the US market permitted it: reliable logistics, near-universal credit cards, and a returns culture made glasses-by-post viable from day one. Warby Parker could start pure D2C and add stores as a second act.

Lenskart, built in India, faced a market where that playbook fails. Trust in buying prescription eyewear unseen was low, customers wanted a physical eye test and to try frames on their own face, and the logistics and returns infrastructure that made the box work were far thinner. So Lenskart built the inverse: omnichannel from early on, a strong app paired with an aggressive physical rollout, and — critically — a vertically integrated supply chain including manufacturing and lens labs. Warby Parker won with a mechanic and a brand; Lenskart won by owning production and showing up in person.

## Why vertical integration was the moat, not an operational detail

Lenskart's supply-chain control is the deliberate core of its strategy. By making frames rather than only designing them, it offered genuinely affordable eyewear without surrendering margin or quality, and served a price-sensitive mass market and an aspirational premium tier from one chain. Where the central barrier is trust and affordability, owning the means of production is the moat. Warby Parker, operating where trust and logistics were lower barriers, could stay asset-lighter and spend its energy on brand and experience instead. Neither chose wrong; each matched its investment to its market's binding constraint.

## What this means for operators

- **Do not import a playbook — diagnose the binding friction.** The same category rewarded mail-order-and-brand in one market and stores-and-vertical-integration in another. The winning wedge is whatever removes the specific barrier stopping customers from buying, and that barrier is local.
- **Infrastructure context decides asset-heavy versus asset-light.** Warby Parker could be asset-light because US logistics and credit did the heavy lifting. Lenskart had to own the stack because its market's infrastructure would not. Capital intensity is a response to environment, not a preference.
- **Convergence does not retroactively equalize origins.** Both are omnichannel now — Warby Parker runs stores and eye exams; Lenskart keeps deepening vertical control and expanding abroad. But the origin strategy is the lesson: the right first move is the one calibrated to the friction you actually face, not the one that worked somewhere else.
