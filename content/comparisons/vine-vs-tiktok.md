**[Vine](/case-study/vine-instagram-video-2013) invented short-form mobile video and died with 200 million users still active; [TikTok](/case-study/tiktok-algorithm) took the identical format four years later and built the defining platform of a decade. The gap between them was never the format — TikTok did not invent six-second looping video any more than it invented vertical phones. The gap was the algorithm, and underneath it, the willingness to keep and pay the creators who made the content. Vine's legacy is the format. TikTok's legacy is the product that sat on top of it. The format was table stakes; the algorithm and the creator economy were the business.**

Vine is a double lesson — in acquisition neglect and in confusing a format for a moat — and both halves matter.

## A format without a business model

Vine, built in 2012 by Dom Hofmann, Rus Yusupov, and Colin Kroll, was "Twitter for moving images": six seconds, looping, shot on a phone, shareable by link. Twitter paid $30 million to acquire it before it even publicly launched, which made strategic sense — a real-time network buying the fastest-growing mobile format. After the 2013 launch Vine produced the first wave of internet-native video stars, and its influence outlived it: people still say "Vine energy" to mean a specific absurdist humor. But Twitter never built the one thing the platform needed to survive — creator monetization. No revenue share, no brand-deal marketplace, nothing that let the people making the content earn from it. The format was beloved and the business beneath it was empty.

## Neglect turned the asset into an exodus

The failure to monetize was not a missing feature; it was a slow-motion loss of the supply side. Vine's biggest creators were earning nothing from the platform, surviving on brand deals their managers arranged, and in 2016 a group of the most prominent allegedly offered Twitter a deal: pay each of them $1.2 million a year and they would stay. Twitter declined. The creators left for YouTube and Instagram, the audience followed them, and Twitter shut Vine down in January 2017 with 200 million people still using it. A content platform's creators are its real inventory, and Vine let a competitor recruit them by simply offering a way to get paid.

## The algorithm, not the format, was the product

TikTok launched internationally the year Vine died, merged with Musical.ly in 2018, and made the same format dominant by adding the thing Vine never had: the For You Page. Vine's discovery was social-graph-based — you found a creator because someone you followed shared them. TikTok's was purely algorithmic — a creator with zero followers could reach 10 million people overnight because the machine decided you would like them. That democratized reach gave every creator a shot and gave TikTok a self-replenishing supply of content, the exact asset Vine had failed to retain. Vine gave creators a stage; TikTok gave everyone a stage, and paid attention to keeping them on it.

## What this means for operators

- **A format is not a moat.** Vine's six-second video was copied and perfected by a latecomer. If your differentiator is a format or a feature, assume it is table stakes and ask what defensible system — algorithm, economics, network — you are building on top of it.
- **Your creators (or suppliers) are inventory; monetize them or lose them.** Vine's stars generated the value and captured none of it, so a competitor simply offered payment and took them. On a two-sided platform, the supply side leaves the moment someone else lets them earn.
- **An acquisition you neglect is a competitor you fund.** Twitter owned the category and let it wither while TikTok built the real product. Owning the asset is worthless without the will to invest in what it needs to survive.
