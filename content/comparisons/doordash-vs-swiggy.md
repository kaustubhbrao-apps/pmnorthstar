**[DoorDash](/case-study/doordash-suburban-logistics) and [Swiggy](/case-study/swiggy-instamart-bet) run the same business — a three-sided marketplace stitching together restaurants, couriers, and hungry consumers — and the comparison is valuable precisely because the model is held constant. What changes is the income level of the market underneath it, and that single variable forces two companies with identical playbooks into fundamentally different financial engineering. The lesson is about the limits of model portability: a model travels, its economics do not.**

Both companies were founded on the same wedge within a year of each other, and both won by finding the customers the obvious players ignored. The interesting question is not who copied whom. It is why the same marketplace produced a company with a $35 order and a company with a $4 one, and what each had to do to survive its own math.

## The same wedge, found in the gaps others ignored

DoorDash started in 2013 when its founders went door-to-door at Palo Alto restaurants with a handmade flyer and a Google Voice number. The defining choice was not technology but geography: while Uber Eats and Grubhub fought over dense urban cores, DoorDash went to the suburbs — customers with cars but little time and few local options, and almost no competing couriers. It built dense driver networks where rivals saw no market, and when the pandemic spiked delivery demand everywhere, DoorDash was already positioned across the full US map. Operational obsession, not platform sophistication, is the culture that won it roughly two-thirds of the US market.

Swiggy, founded in Bengaluru in 2014, ran the same move against a different gap: urban India's professional class, which had the income to pay for delivery but not the time to cook or queue. Same three-sided marketplace, same underserved-convenience insight, same early reliance on logistics density as the real product. The strategies rhyme because the model is genuinely portable. The divergence comes later.

## The order value decides the business, not the strategy

The gulf between the two companies today reflects the income gap between their markets more than any difference in skill. DoorDash's average order is about $35; it charges $3–7 in delivery fees, offers DashPass at $10/month to make delivery feel free, and reached GAAP profitability in 2024. Swiggy's average order is about ₹350 (~$4), with delivery fees of ₹0–40. The delivery *cost* is not 8x cheaper in India — a courier trip runs roughly ₹40–60 versus $5–8 — so the fee eats a similar share of each order in both markets. But the absolute margin per order in India is a fraction of the American one. Identical operational efficiency produces a profitable company in one market and a structurally thin one in the other.

## Why the models diverge at scale

That is where the single business splits into two. DoorDash can subsidize restaurants and consumers to buy share because its per-order economics eventually carry the weight. Swiggy could not reach sustainable economics on food delivery alone at Indian price points, so it had to manufacture new margin: quick commerce through Instamart (grocery baskets of ₹600+ lift the average order toward numbers that actually work), premium memberships, and B2B catering. The destination — a profitable marketplace — is the same. The route is dictated by the order value, which is dictated by the market, which neither company chose.

## What this means for operators

- **A model is portable; its economics are not.** The three-sided marketplace worked in both countries. Importing it without re-deriving the unit economics for the local income level is how you ship a strategy that cannot pay for itself.
- **Order value is destiny.** When the absolute margin per transaction is small, operational excellence is necessary but not sufficient — you must either raise the order value (quick commerce) or run volumes most Western playbooks never contemplate.
- **Find the gap before you optimize the core.** Both winners started where incumbents weren't — suburbs, professional-class urban India. Density in an ignored segment compounds into a moat that a better app in a crowded segment rarely does.
