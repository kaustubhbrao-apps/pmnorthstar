**[Kodak](/case-study/kodak-digital-camera-failure) invented the digital camera. BlackBerry defined the smartphone. Each was then destroyed by the exact shift it was best positioned to lead — and that is the point. The popular story of disruption is one of incumbents failing to see the future. The truer and more useful story is that both of these companies saw it perfectly and could not bring themselves to live in it, because living in it meant killing the profitable business that made them great. Incumbents rarely die of blindness. They die defending the cash cow.**

What makes the pair so instructive is that they fail identically across different industries and decades, which means the cause is structural, not circumstantial. It is the innovator's dilemma in its purest form.

## Kodak: the company that invented its own killer

The detail is almost too perfect. A Kodak engineer built the first working digital camera in 1975 — the company literally created the technology that would end it. But Kodak's margins came from film, photo paper, and developing chemicals, and digital threatened every one of them. So the prototype was shelved as a curiosity, and Kodak kept optimizing the business it already had. By the time it committed to digital, the margins had evaporated and faster competitors owned the category. Bankruptcy came in 2012. The failure was not ignorance of digital photography; Kodak understood it better than anyone. The failure was an unwillingness to trade a high-margin present for a lower-margin future, even though the future was coming regardless.

## BlackBerry: strengths that became a prison

BlackBerry's version ran through the 2000s. It defined the enterprise smartphone — secure email, the beloved physical keyboard, all-day battery — and owned the professional's pocket. Then the iPhone reframed the category around the touchscreen and the app store. BlackBerry's distinctive strengths became the exact things that trapped it: leadership underestimated apps, dismissed the consumer shift, and doubled down on the keyboard and security that had won the last war. By the time BB10 arrived, developers had standardized on iOS and Android, and BlackBerry exited handsets around 2016. The assets that made it dominant were precisely what it could not let go of.

## Why the rational choice is the fatal one

The deadly part is that defending the core is the correct short-term decision every single quarter. Cannibalizing a profitable business to chase a smaller, riskier one looks irresponsible to boards, shareholders, and the people whose bonuses depend on this year's margins. The logic that protects the cash cow is sound right up until the moment it is catastrophic, and there is no quarter where the trade-off looks obviously worth making. That is why the hardest thing a successful company can do is destroy its own best business before a competitor does it first — the incentives all point the other way until it is too late.

## What this means for operators

- **Seeing the future is the easy part; cannibalizing for it is the hard part.** Both companies had the insight and the technology. What they lacked was the willingness to trade present margin for future relevance. Assume your competitors can see the shift too — the edge is in acting on it.
- **Your greatest strength is your most likely blind spot.** Kodak's film margins and BlackBerry's keyboard were assets that became anchors. Audit what you are least willing to abandon; that is usually where disruption will enter.
- **If the shift is coming regardless, do it to yourself first.** The choice is not whether the cash cow dies but who kills it. Doing it yourself, early, is painful and survivable. Letting a competitor do it is neither.
