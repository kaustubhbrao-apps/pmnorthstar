**Salesforce and HubSpot sell overlapping software and almost never fight, because they enter the customer from opposite ends of its life. Salesforce sells top-down to the CIO, with deep configurability and a switching cost so high that leaving is catastrophic. HubSpot sells bottom-up to the marketing manager who signs up free without telling IT, and lets the company grow into paying before a salesperson ever calls. Salesforce sells to the CIO; HubSpot sells to the intern who will become the CIO. The same category sustains two giants because they own different moments in the customer's maturity, and a company often uses both in sequence.**

The instructive part is that the go-to-market motion, chosen at founding, determines nearly everything downstream — contract size, cost of sale, growth rate, even which customer each can serve at all.

## Two founding motions

Marc Benioff launched Salesforce in 1999 on "No Software" — enterprise CRM in the cloud, rented like a utility — a genuinely shocking pitch to Fortune 500 CIOs used to data-center racks and multi-year implementations. It took a decade for them to believe it and another to become dependent. Brian Halligan and Dharmesh Shah launched HubSpot in 2006 on inbound marketing: rather than interrupt customers with cold calls and banner ads, attract them with content, get found when they search, and win the brand before they are even comparing products. By the time a HubSpot prospect evaluates CRMs, they have been reading HubSpot's blog for six months. One motion persuades the executive who signs; the other earns the practitioner who adopts.

## The motion builds the business

Those two motions produce radically different companies. Salesforce runs top-down: account executives sell to CIOs and IT, average contracts run north of $150,000, deals take 6–18 months, and the revenue — $35 billion in fiscal 2024 — requires large sales teams, Dreamforce, and a partner ecosystem that charges hundreds of millions to configure what was sold. HubSpot runs bottom-up product-led growth: a free CRM lands via a marketing manager, expands as more of the team uses it, and converts to paid tiers before a salesperson engages. Its contracts are a fraction of Salesforce's — but so is the cost of winning them. Neither economics is better; each is the logical consequence of who the product talks to first.

## Why they coexist instead of colliding

The size gap — $35 billion versus $2.6 billion — mostly reflects that enterprises spend far more than SMBs, and Salesforce has owned enterprise CRM for twenty years. But HubSpot has grown faster for most of the last decade, with net revenue retention above 100%, meaning it expands revenue even signing no new customers. The reason they do not destroy each other is that they occupy adjacent, non-overlapping stages: HubSpot is the right tool for the company that cannot afford or absorb Salesforce, and Salesforce is where that company often graduates once it reaches enterprise scale. They are less competitors than successive stops on the same journey.

## What this means for operators

- **Your go-to-market motion is a product decision, not a sales tactic.** Top-down demands configurability, a sales force, and a long cycle; bottom-up demands self-serve simplicity and a free entry. Choose early, because the motion shapes contract size, cost of sale, and which customers you can even reach.
- **Winning the practitioner can beat winning the buyer.** HubSpot captured the person who adopts before they became the person who approves. Bottom-up adoption compounds inside an account and lowers acquisition cost in a way top-down selling never matches.
- **Adjacent stages let rivals coexist.** Serving different points in a customer's maturity is more durable than fighting over the same one. If a larger player owns the enterprise, owning the stage before it — and being the natural on-ramp — is its own strong position.
