**[Dunzo](/case-study/dunzo-hyperlocal-collapse) and [Zepto](/case-study/zepto-ten-minute-grocery) set out to deliver things fast in urban India, and their opposite fates come down to a single variable: focus. Dunzo tried to deliver everything from anywhere; Zepto delivered one category, groceries, from its own stores. The breadth that was Dunzo's value proposition was also what made its unit economics impossible, while the narrowness that looked like a limitation was exactly what let Zepto's economics work. Same country, same premise, three years apart — and the one that picked one thing and built the whole stack around it is the one that survived.**

The comparison is unusually clean because it isolates focus from almost everything else. The lesson is that in operations-heavy businesses, scope is not a marketing choice; it determines whether the math can ever close.

## Breadth was Dunzo's pitch and its undoing

Dunzo launched in 2015 as India's first hyperlocal delivery service: anything, from anywhere in the city, in 45–60 minutes — groceries, medicines, documents, pet food, dry cleaning. The vision was a city-scale concierge, and it worked well enough that millions depended on it and Google invested $12 million in 2020. But breadth made the operations ungovernable. If a courier must navigate to a different store for every order, you cannot predict delivery times, optimize routes, or build the order density that makes delivery economics function — and at India's typical order values, that density is the whole game. The variety customers loved was the exact thing that stopped the business from ever paying for itself; by 2023–24 operations had wound down and Google wrote the stake to near zero.

## Focus was Zepto's constraint and its engine

Zepto, launched in 2021 by founders barely out of their teens, made an audacious promise — groceries in 10 minutes — and made it real through a narrow operational architecture: dense networks of "dark stores," micro-fulfillment centers stocked with the 2,000–3,000 SKUs that cover 80% of household demand. Ten minutes is achievable only if the store sits within ~1.5 km of your home, and that is achievable only because Zepto controls the store, the inventory, and the radius. Picking one category made every hard problem tractable: delivery time was predictable because fulfillment was always in range, order value was optimizable because Zepto chose the assortment, and the model replicated across cities because the dark-store blueprint was standardized. Zepto raised $1.35 billion by 2024 at a $3.6 billion valuation.

## Why the narrow model is the one that scales

The counterintuitive point is that the constrained model is the scalable one. Dunzo's breadth created infinite operational variance, and variance is the enemy of both reliability and margin. Zepto's focus created a repeatable unit — one store format, one assortment logic, one delivery radius — that could be stamped out city by city. Blinkit and Instamart reached the same conclusion simultaneously: all three picked grocery, all three built dark stores, and the category crystallized around the one scope that made economic sense. The everything-stores were left behind not for lack of ambition but because ambition without focus produced economics that could not close.

## What this means for operators

- **In operations-heavy businesses, scope decides the unit economics.** Breadth multiplies operational variance — routes, times, inventory, quality — and variance destroys margin. Narrowing scope is often how you make the math close, not a retreat from the opportunity.
- **A standardized unit is what lets you scale.** Zepto's repeatable dark store stamped across cities; Dunzo's bespoke every-errand model could not. If each new unit is different, you are not scaling, you are starting over repeatedly.
- **Control the stack around the one thing you chose.** Zepto owned the store, the assortment, and the radius, which is why it could keep its promise. Focus plus control over the operational stack is what turns an audacious claim into a defensible business.
