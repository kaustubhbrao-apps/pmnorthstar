**Netflix and Spotify are both subscription-entertainment giants, and they answered the defining question of that model — what do you own that customers cannot get elsewhere — in opposite ways. Netflix chose to own the content, spending its way into becoming a studio. Spotify chose never to own the content, and instead to own the algorithm that routes listeners to it. Both work, which proves the real lesson: subscription entertainment succeeds only when you own something irreplaceable, and the catalog alone is never it. You must find a second thing — a library you made, or a recommendation engine no one else has — that keeps the subscriber from leaving.**

The comparison is a clean experiment in defensibility, because the two companies sit in nearly identical business models and diverge almost entirely on this one axis.

## Own the content, or own the routing

In 2013 Reed Hastings turned a streaming service into a Hollywood studio. The bet costs billions — $17 billion on content in 2024 alone — but House of Cards, Stranger Things, and Squid Game built a library no competitor can offer, and that is why Netflix's gross margin climbed past 40% while rivals bled trying to replicate the catalog. Daniel Ek made the opposite call: Spotify would never own music. Rather than fight the labels for what they controlled, it became the best possible interface between listeners and the catalog, investing in recommendation, creator tools, and podcasts. It pays roughly 70 cents of every dollar to rights holders, which keeps margins thin — but it can add every new release the instant it drops and never has to surrender control of its core business to a studio. One owns the supply; the other owns the demand-side intelligence.

## Why the catalog alone is never the moat

The reason both had to find a second thing is that the catalog is rented, not owned, for anyone who licenses it — and even a company that makes its own content competes against every other catalog a subscriber could choose instead. Netflix's defensibility comes from exclusivity: you cannot watch Stranger Things anywhere else, so the content is also the switching cost. Spotify's comes from personalization: your library, your playlists, and a Discover Weekly that knows your taste better than your friends are things a rival catalog cannot hand you on day one. Each company turned a different asset into the thing the subscriber cannot replace by switching.

## The trade-off shows up in the margins

The two strategies price differently. Netflix in 2024 posted nearly $39 billion in revenue and over $7 billion in operating income — a multiple of five years prior, lifted by password-sharing enforcement and an ad tier layered on top of the subscriber base. Spotify crossed 600 million users and 250 million paid subscribers to become the largest music service on earth, yet its operating margins stay thin, because owning the algorithm but not the content leaves the label oligopoly with the pricing power. Owning content is capital-intensive but margin-rich and lock-in-heavy; owning the interface is asset-light but margin-poor. Neither is free; each pays for its defensibility in a different currency.

## What this means for operators

- **Decide what irreplaceable thing you own beyond the catalog.** A library everyone can license is not a moat. Netflix made content that is also a switching cost; Spotify made personalization that is. Find your second thing before you scale.
- **Owning supply and owning the interface are different bets with different costs.** One is capital-heavy, high-margin, and locked-in; the other is capital-light, thin-margin, and exposed to whoever controls supply. Choose with eyes open about which cost you can bear.
- **Subscription compounding is real but conditional.** Retained subscribers are cheaper than new ones and let you raise prices where value is proven — but only if churn stays low, and churn stays low only because of the irreplaceable thing. The moat funds the compounding, not the other way around.
