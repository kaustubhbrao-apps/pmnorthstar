**boAt did not beat [Sony](/case-study/sony-betamax-format-war-1975) at making headphones — it will never beat Sony at making headphones. It beat Sony at a different game Sony was not playing: capturing the mass market that forms below a premium category once that category exists. This is the distinction most incumbents miss. Market creation and market capture are separate activities, and the company that educates a market about quality is frequently not the one that wins it when the volume arrives.**

The scoreboard is genuinely humbling. A company founded in 2016 that sources from contract manufacturers holds roughly 30 percent of India's earwear market; a company that has made audio hardware since 1955 holds 8–10 percent. Nothing about Sony's engineering explains that. The explanation is strategic.

## Two different games, scored differently

Sony has seventy years of acoustic engineering, proprietary driver technology, and in the WH-1000X series the benchmark for consumer noise cancellation. On every technical axis its products are superior. boAt, founded by Aman Gupta and Sameer Mehta in 2016, did not contest any of that. It noticed something Sony had not bothered to: millions of young Indians were buying their first earphones at ₹500–1,500, and at that price the only options were generic white-box products with no identity. Sony started at ₹3,000, Bose at ₹8,000 — the aspirational brands were simply absent from the segment where most of a 1.4-billion-person market actually shopped. boAt built for that gap: products good enough for the price, cool enough to wear visibly, branded with enough personality to feel like a choice rather than a compromise.

## Who creates a market rarely captures it

Here is the mechanism worth internalizing. Sony created India's premium audio consumer — it taught the market what noise cancellation and driver quality were, the education that justifies a ₹15,000 purchase. But education at the top opens demand at the bottom, and the mass segment that formed below Sony's price had different buyers with different wants: young consumers who had seen AirPods on an Instagram Reel, who treated headphones as personal identity, who could spend ₹1,499 but not ₹8,000. Sony had taught them to want headphones and then declined to sell them a pair they could afford. boAt captured the demand Sony's own marketing had helped create.

## Brand and distribution, not drivers, won the volume

boAt's advantage was never acoustic; it was consumer-brand and go-to-market. IPL sponsorship and associations with cricketers like KL Rahul and Hardik Pandya put it where its buyers' attention was. An aggressive social presence made it feel native to the platforms where the purchase decision actually happened. And a product ladder let a customer who started at ₹1,499 stay with the brand as their budget grew. boAt is not a better audio company than Sony. It is a far better consumer company for the 90 percent of the market Sony never tried to serve.

## What this means for operators

- **Decide whether you are creating a market or capturing one — they need different companies.** Creation rewards engineering and education; capture rewards price, brand, and distribution. Being great at the first does not make you good at the second.
- **Educating the top of a market opens the bottom for someone else.** If you teach customers to want a category but refuse to serve them at the price they can pay, you are subsidizing a challenger's demand.
- **In a mass consumer market, identity beats specs.** The buyers boAt won were choosing who they were, not comparing frequency response. Where the product is "good enough," the brand is the product.
