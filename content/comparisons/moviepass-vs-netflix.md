**[MoviePass](/case-study/moviepass-unlimited-pricing-2017) and [Netflix](/case-study/netflix-dvd-to-streaming) ran the same business model — flat monthly fee, unlimited consumption — to opposite ends, and the reason is the single most important and most ignored fact about subscriptions: the model is not self-executing. Netflix built a subscription whose costs did not rise with usage. MoviePass built one whose costs rose faster than its revenue with every additional movie watched. One became a defining company of the internet era; the other burned roughly $500 million and collapsed in eighteen months. Recurring revenue is not alchemy. The unit economics have to actually work, and here you can watch them work and fail side by side.**

The lesson is not that MoviePass had a bad idea. It is that the same idea is brilliant or fatal depending entirely on whether your cost scales with consumption — and that this is knowable in advance, with arithmetic.

## The same model, two cost structures

MoviePass launched in 2011 at $10 a month for unlimited theater tickets, borrowing the Netflix shape directly. But it paid full retail — $12–15 — for every ticket a subscriber redeemed, with no volume discount and no mechanism to cap heavy users. A subscriber seeing three films a month cost $36–45 against $9.95 in revenue. The theory was that behavioral data could be sold to studios and advertisers to cover the gap; the data revenue never came close. Netflix was built on the inverse foundation. Its DVD-by-mail service capped how many discs you could hold at once, a natural throttle on usage; when it moved to streaming it licensed content at flat fees, so a subscriber watching fifty titles cost the same as one watching one; and with originals from 2013 it amortized production across a global audience and built a library that appreciated. Netflix's costs were largely fixed regardless of how much any subscriber consumed. MoviePass's costs were variable and uncapped.

## Why "variable cost per use" is the whole story

This is the distinction that decides a flat-fee unlimited model. If serving one more unit of consumption costs you almost nothing, heavy users are free marketing and the model compounds. If serving one more unit costs you real money you cannot recover, your best customers — the ones who love the product most and use it most — are the ones bankrupting you. MoviePass's most engaged subscribers were its biggest losses, which is the exact opposite of a healthy business, where engagement and value move together. No growth rate fixes an economic structure that punishes the behavior it is designed to encourage.

## The death spiral was inevitable, not incidental

Because the losses grew with usage, MoviePass had only one lever: restrict the product. It imposed daily caps, peak-hour blackouts, and surcharges on popular films — each measure attacking the very value proposition that had acquired the subscribers. At its 2018 peak it had roughly 3 million members; by 2019 it was bankrupt. Netflix that same year passed 160 million subscribers and reached its first year of sustainable free cash flow. The contrast is not a matter of execution or luck; it was legible in the arithmetic from the first day.

## What this means for operators

- **Before launching flat-fee unlimited, check whether marginal cost is near zero.** If each additional use is almost free, unlimited is a growth engine. If each use costs real money you cannot recoup, unlimited is a countdown to insolvency.
- **Your heaviest users should be your cheapest, not your most expensive.** When engagement and cost move together, the customers who love you most destroy you. Align the model so that usage you want to reward is usage you can afford.
- **"We'll monetize the data later" is not a cost structure.** MoviePass bet an unproven ancillary revenue stream against a certain, growing loss. A subscription has to work on its primary economics; downstream revenue is upside, not a rescue plan.
