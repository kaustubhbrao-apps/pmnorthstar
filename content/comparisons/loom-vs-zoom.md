**[Loom](/case-study/loom-async-video) and [Zoom](/case-study/zoom-frictionless-video) are both video companies that hold opposite beliefs about what good communication at work even is. Zoom exists for the meeting that has to happen live; Loom exists for the meeting that should not happen at all. One fights for a slot on your calendar, the other fights to empty it. The reason to study them together is that they monetize opposite things — Zoom sells synchronous presence, the minutes people spend together in real time; Loom sells time saved, the value of the meetings that never get scheduled — and each model's durability depends on how defensible that unit of value is.**

The pairing reframes a market everyone thinks of as "video calls" into a question about whether presence or its absence is the thing worth paying for.

## Reliability versus the unnecessary meeting

Zoom's wedge was deceptively plain: make video calls actually work. While rivals fumbled installs, dropped connections, and clunky join flows, Zoom obsessed over reliability — fast to join, stable on weak networks, frictionless for whoever you invited. That looked unglamorous until the world went remote overnight and "the call that just works" became indispensable; Zoom grew into a verb on a viral invite-to-join loop where every meeting pulled in new users. Loom attacked the opposite target: the large share of meetings, updates, and walkthroughs that do not need everyone present at once, only one person recording their screen and face and sending it. Loom made that one click, and every recording is a shareable link — a growth loop where a sent Loom turns its viewer into the next recorder. Atlassian saw enough in that behavior to pay roughly $975M.

## Monetizing presence versus monetizing its absence

The deeper contrast is what each charges for. Zoom captures value from time spent together, which is why its growth tracked the explosion of synchronous remote work. Loom captures value from time not spent together — the asynchronous substitute for a meeting that would otherwise have consumed an hour of several calendars. These are not just different features; they are different theories of where workplace value leaks. Zoom bets the problem is bad live video. Loom bets the problem is too much live video. Both can be true, which is why both companies exist.

## Where each model is vulnerable

Durability cuts in opposite directions. Zoom owns an enormous market but faces relentless commoditization, as Microsoft Teams and Google Meet bundle "good enough" video for free — the classic fate of a capability a platform can give away. Loom owns a narrower behavior, but async-first communication scales naturally with distributed work and is harder to reduce to a free checkbox feature, because the value is in the workflow and the shareable artifact, not the video stream itself. The broad, commoditizable market versus the narrow, defensible behavior is the trade each accepted at founding.

## What this means for operators

- **Name the unit of value you sell, not the format you ship.** Both ship video; one sells presence and the other sells its absence. Knowing which you monetize tells you who your real competitor is and where your pricing power lives.
- **A huge market can be a commoditization trap.** Zoom's ubiquity is exactly what invites free bundled substitutes. Breadth without defensibility is a race to zero against platform owners.
- **Attacking the need, not just the tool, can be the stronger play.** Loom did not build a better meeting; it removed the meeting. Eliminating the job to be done is often more defensible than performing it slightly better.
