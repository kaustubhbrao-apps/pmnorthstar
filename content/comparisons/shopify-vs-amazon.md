**Amazon and Shopify sit on opposite sides of the single most important question in commerce infrastructure: do you own the customer, or do you serve the seller? Amazon aggregates buyers and extracts value from the merchants who need access to them. Shopify arms merchants and takes a small cut of their success, owning no consumer relationship at all. Amazon is the empire; Shopify is the arms dealer to the rebels. Each gave up something real to be what it is — Amazon traded merchant goodwill for consumer dominance, Shopify traded consumer traffic for merchant trust — and the surprise of the last decade is that both bets are winning at once.**

The clarifying lens is not revenue but relationships: who does each platform ultimately answer to, and what does that alignment cost and earn.

## Own the customer, or serve the seller

Amazon worked out early that the customer relationship is the most valuable asset in commerce, and that it should own it. The Prime flywheel — low prices pull buyers, buyer volume pulls sellers, seller competition pushes prices lower — created a gravity well that bends commerce toward Seattle. Merchants get hundreds of millions of Prime members and same-day fulfillment; the price is steep: 15–40% referral fees, customer data handed to Amazon, and the standing risk that Amazon spots their bestseller and ships an Amazon Basics version. Shopify, born when Tobi Lütke's frustration selling snowboards led him to build better tooling, embedded the opposite philosophy — serve the merchant, full stop, no competing interest. It gives sellers their own storefront, and takes a small percentage of what flows through it. One platform's customer is the buyer; the other's customer is the seller.

## The alignment shows up in the economics

The numbers expose the two bets. Amazon's third-party segment alone processes over $700 billion in GMV and layers on fees, fulfillment charges, and advertising to dominate global e-commerce. Shopify's $235 billion in merchant GMV is smaller and flows through a take rate of just 3–4% — the rest stays with the merchant, which is the entire point. Shopify's roughly 4 million merchants keep their margins, their customer email lists, their brand, and their pricing power. Amazon extracts more per transaction because it owns the demand; Shopify extracts less because it has chosen not to stand between the merchant and the buyer. The take rate is not a pricing decision, it is the philosophy made numerical.

## Marketplace or picks-and-shovels

The reason both can win simultaneously is that they answer different needs for the same merchant, which is why so many sellers use both — Amazon for its buyer traffic, Shopify for the owned brand and margin. Amazon's model produces higher revenue and adversarial supplier relationships; Shopify's produces lower revenue per transaction and genuine loyalty from millions of merchant-partners. In commerce infrastructure the recurring choice is whether to be the marketplace that owns demand or the picks-and-shovels that equip supply, and Shopify's $100 billion-plus market cap settled the open question of whether the second can be a giant business without ever touching the consumer. It can.

## What this means for operators

- **Decide whose platform you are — the buyer's or the seller's.** Owning the customer lets you extract more and makes your suppliers adversaries; serving the seller earns loyalty and caps your take. You cannot fully be both, and pretending otherwise erodes the trust that makes the second model work.
- **Your take rate encodes your alignment.** Amazon's high extraction and Shopify's thin cut are not just pricing; they are statements about who the platform serves. Set it to match the relationship you actually want, because customers read it as one.
- **Picks-and-shovels is a giant business, not a consolation.** You do not have to own the end customer to be enormous. Equipping a vast base of sellers and growing with their success is a durable alternative to aggregating demand — and often a more loyal one.
