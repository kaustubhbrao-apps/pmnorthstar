Most of the AI-and-copyright fight so far has been about **training** — whether models can learn from scraped content. On November 3, 2026, an EU court adviser moved the fight to a more dangerous place for AI-answer products: **what happens at answer time.** And the line he drew should reshape how anyone thinking about [generative engine optimization](/answers/what-is-generative-engine-optimization) plans for the next few years.

## What was decided (and what wasn't)

Advocate General **Maciej Szpunar** delivered a non-binding opinion in **Case C-250/25, Like Company v Google Ireland.** The core of it, as reported:

- **Answer-time reuse is exposed.** When a chatbot retrieves a press article in real time and reuses it to answer a user, the publisher's rights **"may, in certain circumstances" be infringed.**
- **Training is not (here).** Training models on publicly available material was treated as **"freely permitted under the text-and-data-mining exception."** The adviser called the training questions largely hypothetical in this case.

Crucially, this is an **opinion, not a judgment.** The Court of Justice of the EU still has to rule, and the Budapest court applies the result. AG opinions are influential but not binding — so read this as the clearest signal yet of where European law is heading, not as the destination.

## Why the distinction is the whole story

The split between *training* and *answer-time reuse* is the most important line in AI law right now, because it separates two very different business activities:

- **Learning from the web** — ingesting public text to build a model's general capability. Treated as permitted.
- **Reselling the web** — reproducing a specific publisher's article, in substance, to satisfy a user so they never visit the source. This is where the right may bite.

That second behavior is exactly what makes AI answers commercially threatening to publishers, and it's precisely the behavior now under legal scrutiny. It also maps onto a business model under pressure elsewhere — the EU Commission has separately questioned publishers about Google's AI opt-out, and the European Publishers Council filed an antitrust complaint over AI Overviews and AI Mode.

## What this means if you do GEO

[We've argued that the SEO funnel moved inside the model](/ai-decoded/death-of-seo-funnel-ai-search-2026) and that the game is now being the source the model cites. This opinion adds a legal dimension to that same shift:

- **If you publish content**, publisher rights and opt-out tools are gaining legal teeth. That strengthens your hand — the model that strips your article and gives nothing back is the exact behavior regulators are circling. Structure content to be *cited and linked*, and know your rights are becoming more enforceable, not less.
- **If you build AI answers**, the safe design is the one that **points to sources rather than substituting for them** — real attribution, real links out, and licensing where you're reproducing substance. An answer engine that cannibalizes the source is now carrying legal risk, not just reputational risk.

The deeper point: the AI-answer products that survive won't be the ones that absorb the web most completely. They'll be the ones that found a sustainable relationship with the people who make the content — through attribution, traffic, or licensing — before the courts forced one.

## The takeaway

A single non-binding opinion won't rewrite AI overnight. But it drew the line the whole industry will now argue over: **learn from the web, yes; resell it at answer time, maybe not.** For content teams, it's a reason to lean harder into original, citable work and to treat your publishing rights as an asset. For builders of AI answers, it's an early warning to design for attribution now — because "the model just summarized it" is starting to sound, in a European courtroom, a lot like "the model reproduced it."
